1 · the record

Why a new record, and what it holds

The best national record of homes lost to wildfire is the USGS dataset by Carlson and colleagues: 361 fires in 26 states from 2002 to 2022, one point per building, destroyed or not. It is one release, and it stops in 2022. California's fire service (CAL FIRE) inspects damaged buildings on the ground, but only in California.

We use those two records as training truth for models that read public satellite imagery, so the record keeps going where they stop. For every fire we produce one row per building: whether it was destroyed, a first call about ten weeks after ignition, and a second, more certain call about a year after.

653 fires in 35 states, 2002 to 2026 in the current public release
2,258,471 100,074 destroyed building rows: 73,534 destroyed from the USGS census, carried verbatim, and 26,540 called by our models
292 fires in 32 states after 2022, when the USGS record ends: 347,522 buildings, 17,684 destroyed
469 fires in 34 states outside California, where ground inspections are rare: 580,559 buildings

Every building our models score is scored by the final first call model; 62 fires also carry the second call. Microsoft footprint rows (66,265) are withheld from the public file for licence reasons. Seventeen fires from 2016–2022 are described twice, once by the USGS census and once by our pipeline: never add the two together. Release history lists every version.

2 · how it works

A list of buildings, then two calls on each

Step zero: the pre-fire inventory

Both models only score buildings on a list, so a house missing from the list is missing from the loss count. We start from two public footprint databases, FEMA USA Structures and Microsoft's, keeping only outlines drawn from imagery taken before the fire. A neural network then reads 0.6 m aerial photos (the latest USDA NAIP flight before the fire) and adds roofs the databases missed. Every point is tagged by source and size (major means 50 m² or more, the USGS definition). Possible phantom footprints are kept, not deleted.

Two calls, from two different models

The first call is made about 74 days after ignition. It compares Sentinel-2 satellite images from before and after the fire, at the building and around it, against its neighbours, against the whole fire, and against the same season a year earlier. The second call waits about a year and adds yearly satellite embeddings, a full year of change (does the site recover, stay bare, or get rebuilt?) and a later after-image. Both call a building destroyed at a probability of 0.88 or above. The second call ships beside the first; it does not overwrite it.

The neural network reads the whole image history

For each building, a small neural network reads every clear satellite look before and after the fire and gives one probability, which joins the tree model as one more signal. For the first call it sees only the first 74 days. Each fire's reading comes from a network that never saw that fire.

3 · how well it scores

Close to the best any model can do

F1 is one score for misses and false alarms: it is high only when both are low, and 1.0 is perfect. We report it two ways: counted over all buildings, and averaged fire by fire, so a small fire counts as much as a large one. Every fire is scored by a model trained without it.

The label ceiling. Where USGS and CAL FIRE both assessed the same buildings, they disagree on about 2% of them. When the label is wrong, a correct call is scored as a mistake, so even a perfect model tops out near 0.93 over all buildings and 0.86 per fire on the USGS fires.

4 · where it holds up

Unfamiliar places cost a few points

  • Held-out tests. Retrained without a whole year, state or EPA region and scored on it, the first call's per-fire F1 on USGS fires goes from 0.802 to 0.786 (year), 0.769 (state) and 0.743 (region). A new region costs about 6 points.
  • Never-seen fires. On seven fires never used in training, checked against American Red Cross and FEMA assessments (2,515 buildings), the first call scores F1 0.92 over all buildings. Precision is high everywhere; the misses are on Plains grass fires in Oklahoma (0.66) and in northern Minnesota (0.67), which the model ranks well but scores under the fixed cut.
  • By structure type (CAL FIRE): single-family homes 0.97, mobile homes 0.98, outbuildings 0.93. By fire size (USGS): fires with over 100 destroyed 0.91; 10–100 destroyed 0.80. Only one USGS fire has fewer than 10 destroyed, so small fires are not yet measured.
  • By year. 2017 on: 0.908. 2016 alone: 0.828, because only one Sentinel-2 satellite was flying (about 9 clear looks per building, not 25).
  • The building list outside California finds 0.77 of buildings in Red Cross and FEMA assessments with good coordinates, less in rural areas (0.56) and under dense canopy (0.45). Its precision has been measured only in California (0.83–0.96 on 17 fires) and at one Colorado fire (0.93).

5 · what's next

What we are exploring next

  • Know where not to call in progress: define the regions, fire types and structure classes where calls are reliable, with a no-call flag elsewhere.
  • Region-aware threshold planned: Plains grass fires rank well but fall under the 0.88 cut.
  • More small fires in training awaiting sign-off: adding 87 CAL FIRE incidents from 2023–25 cut the held-out-state cost from 3 points to 1.
  • A strict held-out test for the second call queued.
  • Wider image patches and a call past one year exploring.
  • Damage grades and an uncertainty tier on every building planned.
