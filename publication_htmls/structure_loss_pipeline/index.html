Which buildings did the fire destroy?

This project answers that question one building at a time, from free satellite and aerial imagery rather than from a site visit. For each wildfire it builds a list of the buildings that stood beforehand, then decides for each of them whether the fire destroyed it, and publishes the result as a dataset: one row per building per fire. It is designed to keep producing that record for fires that happen from now on, anywhere in the continental United States.

what this is

The project in four paragraphs

Knowing which buildings a wildfire destroyed matters for insurance, for recovery funding, for building-code research and for any model of wildfire risk. Today that knowledge is assembled by hand. The best existing record — a published dataset compiled by Carlson and colleagues — is a per-building census of destruction in 361 continental United States wildfires between 2002 and 2022, digitised by hand from imagery. It is the reference layer for anyone modelling wildfire exposure, and it ends in 2022.

This project produces the same kind of record automatically. The inputs are public and national: two building-footprint databases and federal aerial photography to enumerate the buildings that stood before a fire, and Sentinel-2 — a pair of free European Space Agency satellites that photograph every point on Earth about every five days at 10 m per pixel — to compare each building's own patch of ground before and after the fire.

The two halves are judged separately, because they fail differently. The list of buildings is scored against the hand-assembled census, which enumerates every major building inside a fire's area, so a missing building can be counted. The destroyed call is scored against fire-service damage inspections of fires that happened after that census ends, which is the strongest out-of-sample evidence available, and against independent assessments by federal and county teams outside California. Every accuracy figure on this site is measured on fires the model never saw during training.

The output is one file on one schema: one row per building per fire, carrying a continuous destroyed probability, the threshold behind the call, where the building sat relative to the fire perimeter, and which data source found it. The code is MIT-licensed and the data is CC BY 4.0.

before you use it

Four things to know about the data

  • There are two estimates per building, with different delivery times. The primary estimate is available about two and a half months after a fire starts and covers every building in every fire. The refined estimate arrives about a year later, once more imagery exists, and is more accurate: against fire-service inspections, scored on the same buildings, F1 goes from 0.9239 to 0.9607. It does not exist for every building — see the confusion matrix page for the coverage of each ground-truth source.
  • Never add up rows across sources for the same fire. Seventeen fires are described twice: once by the hand-assembled historical census and once by this pipeline, one row per building per source. Summing both descriptions double-counts those fires. The dataset page names the column that tells them apart.
  • The inspection ground truth is Californian. Structure-by-structure inspection records exist at scale only in California. Outside it the evidence is the historical census plus three independent one-off assessments. Beyond California reports what that evidence says.
  • Buildings far from a fire are almost never destroyed, and the model has almost no signal there. Accuracy figures are worth reading separately for buildings inside the fire perimeter or within 100 m of it, and for buildings 100 m to 2.4 km outside it. How certain splits them.

glossary

The terms this site uses

Every page defines its own terms where they appear. This is the short list, in one place, for a reader who wants it up front.

Building footprint
A polygon outlining a building's roof, drawn from overhead imagery. Two public databases supply them nationally: the FEMA / Oak Ridge USA Structures layer and Microsoft's US Building Footprints.
Building list
The set of buildings we believe stood in a fire's area before it burned: the footprint databases, plus buildings found by our own detector, each tagged with where it came from.
Our detector
A neural network trained on aerial photography to find buildings the footprint databases miss.
Primary estimate
The destroyed call available about two and a half months after a fire starts. It exists for every building in every fire covered.
Refined estimate
A second, more accurate destroyed call available about a year later, once a further post-fire image window and a year-over-year satellite summary exist. It exists for most buildings in most fires, not all.
Threshold
The model outputs a probability per building; we call a building destroyed above a fixed number — 0.85 for the primary estimate, 0.84 for the refined one. The number is fixed in advance, not tuned to the fire being scored.
Sentinel-2
Two free European Space Agency satellites that photograph every point on Earth about every five days at 10 m per pixel.
Aerial photography (NAIP)
The US Department of Agriculture's National Agriculture Imagery Program: aerial photographs of the continental United States at roughly 60 cm per pixel, reflown every two to three years.
Annual satellite embedding
A freely published yearly summary of what each 10 m patch of ground looked like across a whole year, as 64 numbers per pixel, so one year can be compared with the next.
Fire-service damage inspections
CAL FIRE's Damage Inspection programme: after a fire, inspectors walk the burn area and record, structure by structure, whether it was destroyed, damaged or untouched. Used here as ground truth.
The historical census
The published, hand-assembled record of buildings destroyed in 361 United States wildfires between 2002 and 2022, one point per destroyed building, compiled by Carlson and colleagues.
Study area around a fire
The official fire perimeter plus a 2.4 km margin. Buildings inside the perimeter or within 100 m of it are directly exposed; buildings from 100 m to 2.4 km outside are indirectly exposed and are very rarely destroyed.
Matched at 10 m
A ground-truth point and one of our building locations count as the same building when they lie within 10 m of one another, matched one-to-one, so no building is counted twice.
Held-out scoring
The model that scores a fire is trained only on the other fires, so no fire is ever in its own training set. Every accuracy figure on this site is measured this way.
Precision and recall
Precision is the share of our destroyed calls that were right; recall is the share of the real destruction we found.
F1
The harmonic mean of precision and recall: one number that punishes both missing real destruction and over-calling it. 1.0 is perfect.
Pooled vs mean-per-fire
A pooled figure counts every building equally, so the largest fires dominate it; a mean-per-fire figure counts every fire equally. Both are reported because they answer different questions.
95 % interval over fires
Computed by resampling whole fires 2,000 times. It says how much a number would move if we had happened to cover a different set of fires — the uncertainty that matters here, because buildings within one fire are not independent.
Why accuracy misleads
In a typical study area only about 6–7 % of buildings are destroyed, so a model that called everything "survived" would score about 93 % accuracy and be useless.
