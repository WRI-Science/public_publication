Wildfire structure loss

Testing three findings · 2026-10-19

Official loss counts that read 0, the wind behind the urban conflagrations in our records, and the 89-building line: what the literature says about each, the ways each could be wrong, and what held up when we went back to the data (Carlson 2002–22, our 2002–26 record, CAL FIRE DINS 2013–25).

By Carlo Broderick, NCEAS, UC Santa Barbara · 2026-10-18 19:50 PDT

Each panel has its own Plain / Technical switch; hover or tap any bar, line or heading for the numbers behind it.

What this page does

Three results from the highlights page looked the most interesting, so we tried to break them. For each one we read what other researchers have found, listed the ways the result could be wrong or could be pushed further, wrote down the tests and the bar for passing before looking, and then went back to the data.

  • Official zeros are real but small: about 1 in 13 big-loss fires, a few percent of buildings
  • Wind: every urban conflagration in our records burned in a wind event, and that could have failed
  • The 89-building line is mostly a size floor, not a tipping point

The starting point for each is on Wildfire building loss: highlights so far.

The three records used throughout

  • Carlson 2002–22: the USGS record of fires that destroyed 10 or more buildings, mapped by hand.
  • Our 2002–26 record: Carlson's fires, plus 2023–26 fires scored by our satellite model, with CAL FIRE's inspection results wherever CAL FIRE inspected.
  • CAL FIRE DINS 2013–25: California's door-to-door damage inspections, every incident on file.

Fire outline: MTBS burned area plus 100 m. An urban conflagration is a patch of at least 50 destroyed buildings, each within 30 m (centre to centre) of another. A block is a group of buildings that stood before the fire with centres within 35 m of each other.

Official zeros are real but small: about 1 in 13 big-loss fires, a few percent of buildings

As it stood: several 2023–25 fires that our model says destroyed buildings read 0 in the national situation reports, and public reports confirmed losses for 8 of the 13 we checked; how big the gap is nationally was open. We asked whether a 0 there is a measurement at all, matched every loss fire in the three records to the national reports, checked how much of the gap our model's own false alarms could explain, and sized the shortfall three ways.

The national count is a running tally that stays at 0 until losses are confirmed

In the national report a 0 is the default entry, not a measurement

From 2007 to 2025, about four in five daily fire entries in the national situation report read 0 buildings destroyed, and only 150 of 123,249 were left blank or marked unknown. Of the fires that reported a loss at some point, 37% began at 0. What it suggests: a 0 in this report is the default entry, not a measurement; it cannot by itself tell a fire with no losses from one whose losses were not yet counted.

About 1 in 13 fires that destroyed 10 or more buildings reads 0 or is missing nationally

We matched every fire that destroyed buildings in the three records to the national reports: the daily national situation report (2007–26) and the incident status forms behind it (ICS-209, research copy to 2020). Of fires that destroyed 10 or more buildings, 27 of 353 in Carlson 2002–22, 32 of 416 in our 2002–26 record and 7 of 127 in CAL FIRE DINS 2013–25 read 0 or are missing — about 1 in 13 to 1 in 18. They hold 3.0%, 2.5% and 0.4% of the destroyed buildings. Small fires (1–9 destroyed; Carlson has almost none) are usually missing, because the national report lists large fires only. What it suggests: the official count misses real loss fires, but not a large share of the buildings; the biggest disasters are counted.

True zeros cluster in Southern state-agency reports of fast grass fires

Where the final incident form records losses but the daily national report shows 0, the gap is largest in the South: MS 33%, FL 22%, OK 11%, TX 7%, against 2.3% in California. In Carlson's record, 8 fires with 10 or more destroyed buildings read 0 in both national sources, all in Oklahoma, Texas and North Carolina (the April 2009 Oklahoma and April 2011 Texas outbreaks), the same kind of state-agency report as the Texas Panhandle fires of 2024 that also read 0. What it suggests: the true zeros come from how some state agencies report fast grass fires, not from chance.

It holds on the records alone, not on our model: losses are missed, but a small share

Our satellite calls cannot prove the gap on their own: on fires inspectors found undamaged, 2 of 29 had 10 or more false calls, enough to produce about 17 (up to 54) of the 26 officially-zero fires with 10 or more calls. The checks that hold up do not use the model at all: inspection and imagery records against the national count, and the yearly total against the daily report. What it suggests: the finding stands on the records; the model is a pointer to where to look.

Wind: every urban conflagration in our records burned in a wind event, and that could have failed

Wind events are not always on: at the same towns in the same season they happen about one day in five, yet every urban conflagration in the three records burned in one, during the hours the town burned. Big blocks that destroyed buildings without becoming one were nearly all wind events too, but fires that reached a big block without recorded losses mostly were not. Wind brings fire into big blocks; block size decides which burn through. One known urban conflagration outside the records, Waldo Canyon 2012, had no recognised wind event.

What held, what did not

  • Held: the rule can fail. On ordinary days of the same season the same towns see a wind event about one time in five, so 10 of 10 is not built in.
  • Held: the wind event was in force during the hours each town burned, not just some day of the fire.
  • Did not hold: 'every one in a strong wind event'. Glass 2020 had only the strongest 1.1 % of offshore push, just outside a 1 % cut.
  • Weaker: 'so did most big blocks' is true of fires that destroyed buildings (our records), mostly in California; across all fires that reached a big block, those with no recorded losses had a wind event less than half the time (38%).
  • Did not hold beyond the records: Waldo Canyon 2012, an urban conflagration by the same definition but outside our three records, had no recognised wind event (a thunderstorm gust front drove it). The statement is about our three records, not a law.
  • Can't tell: whether wind adds anything once the block is known; almost every big-block fire had a wind event.

Others find wind events common, costly and not strictly necessary

The literature raises three challenges: wind events may simply be common where these fires happen; wind is not physically required; and a town-level warning can miss the wind that mattered. The first one can be tested.29 verified sources; topics: base rate, threshold, downslope, timing, method, necessary-not-sufficient, international. Already-covered sources (Abatzoglou 2023, Keeley & Syphard 2019, Balch 2024, …) not repeated.

Wind events are common where big blocks burn

Southern California has about 32 Santa Ana events a year, at least 2 a month from October to April (Guzman-Morales et al. 2016), and more than three in four of them start no fire (Keeley et al. 2021). So the fact that a wind event was in force says little until it is set against how often one is in force anyway.

Wind-driven fires cause most of the losses

Santa Ana fires burned about the same area as other fires in Southern California but caused 80 % of the 1990–2009 economic losses and spread three times faster (Jin et al. 2015).

Wind is not strictly needed

Dense wooden districts in Kobe 1995 burned as conflagrations at a mean wind of 2.6 m/s, slowly (Hokugo 1997). In the U.S., Waldo Canyon 2012 losses came under a thunderstorm gust front (Johnson et al. 2014) and Carr 2018 under a fire-made vortex (Lareau et al. 2018), winds a warning-based rule does not see.

Case-crossover: compare the event hours with the same place at other times

Epidemiology's standard way to ask whether an exposure is unusual at the moment of an event is to compare it with the same person (here, the same town) at other times of the same season and hour (Maclure 1991; Janes et al. 2005).

Point measurements can miss downslope winds

Downslope winds are very local and pulse (Cao & Fovell 2016); stations near the Thomas Fire read low while gusts reached 35 m/s (Fovell & Gallagher 2018). The Camp Fire winds were strong but not climatologically exceptional (Mass & Ovens 2021); Marshall had a High Wind Warning but no Red Flag Warning (Benjamin et al. 2023).

The wind-event rule could have failed: on ordinary days it says 'yes' about one time in five

A recognised wind event means a National Weather Service Wind Advisory or High Wind Warning was in force at the town, or the offshore air-pressure push behind the Santa Ana, Diablo or Oregon east wind was among the strongest 5 % of hours since 1991, at some hour while the town burned. At the same towns, at the same time of year, the rule is on about one day in five (18% for the median town whose block became an urban conflagration). So 10 of 10 in our record is not automatic. Wind adds nothing we can see once the block is known; a few events carry most of the losses

Wind events came with 116 of 321 fires in our record that never reached a big block, and none became an urban conflagration. The 89-building line is mostly a size floor, not a tipping point

As it stood: no fire whose largest block held 89 or fewer buildings became an urban conflagration (0 of 338, 714 and 429), while 8 of 23, 10 of 25 and 7 of 16 above it did. We asked how much of that zero the definitions build in, whether it depends on the two distances, on the building inventory or on California.

Others predict a size floor from geometry alone, so a zero below it needs a null model

What other researchers measured that bears on a minimum town size for an urban conflagration. Hover a source for its title.

Sources verified on Crossref or the publisher page on 2026-10-19; full list with what each measures in the literature note.

  • A built-in result must be tested against what the definition alone gives. When an outcome is part of its predictor, the two line up even if nothing real links them. The fix is to compare with what the overlap alone would produce, keeping the buildings and the number lost fixed and moving only where the losses fall.Mathematical coupling and part-whole correlation; fixed-total null models and random labelling of point patterns; whole-curve envelope tests. (Archie JP 1981; Tu YK 2006; Gotelli NJ 2000; Baddeley A 2015; Myllymaki M 2017)
  • A zero in a small group is a bound, not a fact. No events among 13–18 fires still allows a true rate of up to about one in five; a 'needed but not enough' claim has its own test for an empty corner of a scatter.Exact (Clopper–Pearson) bounds; Necessary Condition Analysis permutation test; Firth logistic for an empty class. (Clopper CJ 1934; Hanley JA 1983; Dul J 2016; Heinze G 2002)
  • Network theory expects a size floor that moves with the link distance. Below the spreading point, the largest burned group grows only slowly with town size, and the switch is a band rather than a line. How many neighbours each building has within the link distance is what counts, so changing the distance should move the line.Largest subcritical cluster ~ log N; finite-size rounding; random geometric graphs (threshold mean degree ≈ 4.5 in 2-D); outbreak size ~ N^(2/3) at threshold. (Bazant MZ 2000; Borgs C 2001; Dall J 2002; Ben-Naim E 2004)
  • Epidemiology's 'critical community size' is the closest analogue. It is defined as the size at which the chance is one half, with an interval, and it shifts with conditions; a single pooled line may mix regimes such as windy and calm fires.Bartlett's critical community size for measles persistence; its dependence on seasonality and drivers. (Bartlett MS 1957; Keeling MJ 1997; Conlan AJK 2007)
  • Closest concurrent work: weighted building-to-building exposure. One preprint on the 2025 Los Angeles fires weights each pair of buildings by how strongly they face each other (from 3-D lidar shapes) and finds loss rises steeply with that exposure. We could read only its abstract.Structure-to-Structure Exposure Network from LiDAR building meshes, 24,771 structures; nonlinear destruction vs exposure × hardening; IV for defence. SSRN full text blocked (HTTP 403). (Swietek 2026; Vibrant Planet Data Commons 2025)
  • Towns outside the U.S. with building-level maps could test the zero. Itoigawa 2016 (Japan, 147 buildings in a strong wind), Jasper 2024 (Canada), Viña del Mar 2024 and Valparaíso 2014 (Chile) have building-by-building damage maps; a close-built block of 50–89 that formed a 50-building chain would break the finding.NILIM report 980; Copernicus EMSR715 grading (free reuse with attribution); Reszka & Fuentes 2014; Jasper official address list. Downloads and licences not yet checked. (NILIM and BRI (Takeya 2017; Copernicus Emergency Management Service 2024; Reszka P 2014; CBC News 2024)

Below the line, almost no fire could have made a 50-building chain

A 50-building chain needs 50 destroyed buildings and 50 buildings close enough to chain. Of the 16, 18, 13 fires with blocks of 50–89 (Carlson 2002–22, our 2002–26 record, CAL FIRE DINS 2013–25), only 2, 2, 3 had both, and none became one. Above the line 8 of 18, 10 of 20 and 7 of 12 such fires did. What it suggests: the zero below the line is mostly built in: with two or three fires that could have done it, the data cannot tell whether those fires behaved any differently.

We kept each fire's buildings and its number of destroyed buildings, and moved the losses around 200 times. Scattered at random, or as one compact burned area, the same losses almost never make a 50-building chain below the line (chance of none 86%, 86%, 61% for a compact burn). Only when the losses are pushed into the block building by building does a zero below the line become unlikely, and then mainly in CAL FIRE DINS 2013–25, where Caldor 2021 and Kincade 2019 would have made one. What it suggests: the layout of the towns and the number of buildings lost explain the zero almost on their own.

The line moves with the chain distance, not with the block distance

Each cell is the smallest block that held a 50-building chain when blocks and chains are drawn at other distances. With 30 m chains the answer is 95 buildings for blocks drawn at 35, 40 or 45 m in all three records. But it moves with the chain distance: 25 m chains give 125 (our record), 40 m chains give 40, 50 m chains give 10. Whenever chains may be longer-linked than blocks, chains cross between blocks and urban conflagrations appear in small blocks. What it suggests: 89 is a property of pairing a 30 m chain with a 35–45 m block, not a constant: the line is not a fixed multiple of the chain size.

Weighting buildings by how strongly they expose each other does not sharpen the line

The closest concurrent study (Swietek 2026, 2025 Los Angeles fires) weights each pair of buildings by how strongly they face each other, measured from 3-D lidar shapes, and finds loss rises steeply with that exposure. We could read only the abstract. We tried the cheap version of the idea with what we hold: each pair weighted by a heat-like fall-off with distance (full weight at 10 m or closer, a quarter at 20 m), then a block made only of strongly exposed buildings, and the total exposure inside the block. Neither ranks fires better than simply counting buildings in the block, in any record. What it suggests: at the scale of whole fires, a count of close buildings already captures what distance-weighting adds; the real test of their idea needs building shapes and heights, building by building.

The zero holds, but it is mostly built in and rests on California

The zero itself is solid: it survives inventory errors, thinning and the wind check. What does not survive is reading it as a threshold in fire behaviour. What it suggests: keep the line as a description of where urban conflagrations are possible, say it is mostly built in, and test it next on fires outside California and outside the U.S. that have close-built blocks of 50–89.
