Structure-loss paper · urban conflagration definition

Defining an urban conflagration · 2026-10-13

How the working rule (a fire is an urban conflagration when at least 50 destroyed buildings form one connected patch, each within 30 m of the next) was reached and tested on three full records: 358 fires in the USGS Carlson record (2002–2022), 633 in our full 2002–2026 record and 276 CAL FIRE-inspected incidents (2013–2025), each with at least one building destroyed; plus Lahaina 2023 and Waldo Canyon 2012 as outside checks. Every distance and size from 5 to 100 m and 10 to 1,000 buildings, tests for a natural break, published distances, and 6 definitions scored head to head.

By Carlo Broderick, NCEAS, UC Santa Barbara · 2026-10-13 15:18 PDT

Short answer: the data do not single out a better definition, and they draw no natural line of their own. The size of the largest connected patch of destroyed buildings rises smoothly from one fire to the next, with no gap that separates town fires from the rest. Destroyed buildings cluster more than chance over a range of link distances, about 20–50 m between building centres, not at one distance. The working rule (30 m links, at least 50 buildings) sits inside that range, and it counts every fire in the CAL FIRE inspections that published reports call an urban conflagration (4 of 4). But it is a knife-edge choice: nudging either number swaps borderline fires in or out, and it misses 4 smaller or more spread-out fires that some reports describe as house-to-house fires (Angora, Chimney Tops 2, Grass Valley, Witch). None of the 5 alternatives did better. We suggest keeping the rule as a stated convention and always reporting which fires are firmly in and which are borderline.

How to read this page

Hover or tap any number, cell, line or point for what it means; switch to Technical at the top for methods and statistics. “Patch” always means: destroyed buildings linked hop by hop, each within the link distance of at least one other in the patch.

1 · The working definition and where it came from

An urban conflagration is a wildfire that turns into a town fire, spreading from building to building. To study them we needed a rule that says, from a map of destroyed buildings, which fires did that.

What the rule counts

A patch grows hop by hop. Each destroyed building joins the patch if it is within 30 m (centre to centre) of any destroyed building already in it. So a patch is a chain through a neighbourhood, not a circle. The fire's size as an urban conflagration is the number of buildings in its largest patch.Largest single-linkage cluster of destroyed building centroids at linkage L (LDC_L); working definition LDC_30 ≥ 50. Equivalent to connected components of the graph joining destroyed pairs ≤ L apart.

How we got to the working rule

  1. 8 October 2026. While checking whether our model finds the same big-loss fires as the labels, we needed a yes/no for “this fire became an urban fire”. The project lead suggested: a connected patch of at least 50 destroyed buildings with 30 m links. We reported 25 and 100 buildings and 50 m links alongside, and noted then that 30 m splits Paradise (Camp Fire), whose houses sit about 26 m apart.
  2. 12 October. We asked which fires reach that line. The best single predictor was the size of the largest block of closely spaced buildings the fire reached (about 20 m between walls): no fire below about 89 such buildings reached 50 destroyed in one patch. The chance rose steeply but smoothly, with no step.
  3. 13 October, morning. Six other ways of describing the loss (share of destroyed buildings with a destroyed neighbour, loss per square kilometre, how deep the loss went into town, and others) all separate these fires from the rest, but they form one continuous spread, not a separate class of fire.
  4. 13 October, this page. The rule itself was chosen, not derived. Here we test it.

3 · Does a natural break fall out of the data?

A definition is most defensible when the data draw the line themselves: a size where fires split into two groups, or a distance at which fire spread typically links buildings.

Is there a natural size? The largest patch, fire by fire

If urban conflagrations were a separate kind of fire, the curves would drop and then flatten, leaving a gap between ordinary fires and town fires. They don't: patch sizes thin out smoothly from 1 building to over a hundred. A formal test for a second peak found none at any distance from 5 to 100 m in any record (smallest unadjusted p = 0.12). The 50-building line sits on a smooth slope; it is a convention.Complementary CDF of LDC_30 over fires with n_dest ≥ 10. Silverman critical-bandwidth test (2,000 smoothed bootstraps) on log10 LDC_L, 10 L × 3 records, Holm-adjusted p ≥ 1.00 everywhere (min raw 0.117). Fisher–Jenks 2-class break at 30 m: 8 [5–11], 8 [5–11], 7 [6–16] buildings (bootstrap CI spans more than a factor 2); Gaussian mixtures prefer 2 components at L ≥ 20 m, but on skewed data that is expected and not separation (PA33).

Is there a natural distance? How the largest patch grows as the link distance grows

As the link distance grows, more destroyed buildings join the largest patch. If house-to-house spread had one typical range, the curves would jump at the same distance in every fire. They don't. Destroyed buildings cluster more than chance, building up between about 20 and 50 m

For each fire we redrew the same number of destroyed buildings at random among the fire's own buildings, and compared the largest patch. Real patches are bigger than chance at every distance above 5 m. Half of the extra clustering is reached by 20 m between centres and nearly all of it by 50 m; beyond that it adds little (in the CAL FIRE inspections it falls again). So the distances at which destroyed buildings hang together more than chance span roughly 20–50 m, and 30 m sits inside that range. 4 · What physics and published work say

A good definition should agree with what is known about how fire jumps between buildings, and with how fire scientists describe these events.

Published definitions and distances

No published source gives a number that defines an urban conflagration in a wildfire; the definitions are in words (building-to-building spread that overwhelms firefighting), or a count of homes lost. The distances that recur are gaps between buildings at which one burning building ignites the next: 1.8–40 m wall to wall, most often 9.1–18 m. For typical houses (about 13 m across) that is roughly 22–31 m between centres, which overlaps the range where our destroyed buildings cluster beyond chance.Literature search 2026-10-13 (WebSearch/WebFetch), each reference verified by DOI or official URL; distances converted to the grid as wall gap (edge-gap grid) or centre distance.

6 · Recommendation and what it would change

What the evidence supports, and how the list of fires and the exposure line change.

What we conclude

What the pre-set test says: we can't tell which definition is best. None of the 5 alternatives matched the published descriptions at least as well as the working rule while also being more stable or cleaner at its line, and the working rule itself does not sit on a stable plateau. Searching the whole grid for the best match to the published list (after the fact) picks 75 m links and at least 200 buildings, but chosen fairly (leaving each named fire out in turn) it matches no better than the working rule (0.70 vs 0.80 on our full record), so that is noise, not a better rule.

What we suggest for the paper. Keep “at least 50 destroyed buildings in one patch, each within 30 m (centre to centre) of the next” as a stated convention, and say why it is reasonable: 30 m between centres picks the same fires as about 15–20 m between walls for typical houses (estimated from building size; in Lahaina, where real outlines exist, it corresponds to about 10 m), inside the 9.1–18 m separations most often cited for house-to-house ignition, and inside the range where destroyed buildings cluster beyond chance. Always report the fires that qualify under every nearby rule (the core: Almeda Drive 2020, Eaton 2025, Palisades 2025, Sayre 2008, Tubbs 2017) separately from the borderline ones, and give the 50 m version as a sensitivity.

Worth testing next. A companion analysis that also links destroyed buildings up to 60 m apart when continuous tree cover lies between them turns Gatlinburg's largest patch from 16 buildings into 326, matching the report that calls it an urban conflagration, while Black Forest (7 → 11) and Bastrop (5 → 13) stay scattered. That idea, links through fuel as well as distance, was not part of the test fixed in advance here, so it is a candidate for the next round, not a result.

Does the 89-building line move?

Earlier we found that no fire whose largest block of closely spaced buildings held 89 or fewer met the working rule. That result holds only for the 30 m rule: with 50 m links, 12 fires in our full record with blocks of 89 or fewer count, and the line drops to about 21 buildings. Part of that line is built in. A 30 m patch of destroyed buildings lies inside one block of closely spaced buildings, so the block is at least as big as the patch in 99.7%, 99.7%, 100.0% of fires: no fire can count below about 50 by construction.
