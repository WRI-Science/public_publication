Structure-loss paper · urban conflagration definition

Defining an urban conflagration · 2026-10-13

How a working definition was reached and tested on Carlson 2002–22 (358 fires with a destroyed building), Our 2002–26 record (658) and CAL FIRE DINS 2013–25 (276 incidents): every link distance from 5 to 100 m and size from 10 to 1,000 buildings, published distances, 6 definitions scored against named fires, the 89-building block line with a held-out check on 2016–2022 fires, and wind, trees and block conditions.

By Carlo Broderick, NCEAS, UC Santa Barbara · 2026-10-18 14:57 PDT

In October 2026 a collaborator asked us: is there a way to quantify an urban conflagration, a wildfire that turns into a town fire spreading from building to building? This page shows how we answered, on three full records of destroyed buildings. The data draw no line of their own: the largest patch of destroyed buildings thins out smoothly from fire to fire, and destroyed buildings cluster more than chance over about 20–50 m, not at one distance. So we propose a stated convention: at least 50 destroyed buildings in one patch, each within 30 m of the next; a fire can hold none, one or several. Which fires get there: none whose largest block of closely spaced buildings held 89 or fewer (0 of 338, 0 of 714, 0 of 429 fires; 0 of 66 held-out fires). What else matters is hard to see: every urban conflagration burned in a recognised wind event, but so did nearly every near miss.

How to read this page

Words. A block: buildings standing before the fire whose centres are within 35 m of each other, chained hop by hop (about 20 m or less between walls). A patch: destroyed buildings linked hop by hop, each within the link distance (30 m unless a panel says otherwise) of at least one other in the patch. An urban conflagration: a patch of at least 50 destroyed buildings. Buildings “reached by the fire” are those standing before it inside the Forest Service / USGS burned-area outline (MTBS) plus 100 m (Carlson's or the public perimeter where MTBS has none).

1 · The question and the working rule

The question, the steps we took to answer it, and what the rule counts.

How we got to the working rule

The question, each step we took to answer it, and the proposal it led to. The numbers are the current ones, on the fire outline the paper now uses.

  1. 1
    12 October 2026 The question
    Cat Fong and the project lead asked: “Is there a quantification for urban conflagration?” — a way to say, from a map of destroyed buildings, which wildfires turned into town fires that spread from building to building. We started from a working rule first used on 8 October: at least 50 destroyed buildings in one connected patch, each within 30 m of the next.
  2. 2
    12 October Which fires reach that line?
    The best single predictor was the largest block of closely spaced buildings the fire reached: no fire whose largest block held 89 buildings or fewer reached the line (Our 2002–26 record: 0 of 714 fires; 10 of 25 above it). The chance rose steeply but smoothly, with no step.
  3. 3
    13 October Is town fire a separate kind of fire?
    Six other ways of describing the loss (share of destroyed buildings with a destroyed neighbour, loss per square kilometre, how deep the loss went into town, and others) all set these fires apart, but as one continuous spread, not a separate class.
  4. 4
    13 October Do the data draw their own line?
    No natural size: the largest patch thins out smoothly from fire to fire. No single natural distance: destroyed buildings cluster more than chance over about 20–50 m between building centres. Published work gives building-to-building ignition distances (most often 9.1–18 m wall to wall) but no number that defines an urban conflagration.
  5. 5
    13 October Is another definition better?
    We scored the working rule against 5 alternatives on how well each matches published accounts of named fires and how stable its list of fires is. None did better; the working rule is a knife-edge choice (nudging either number swaps borderline fires in or out).
  6. 6
    13–14 October A part of a fire, not the whole fire
    Patches, not fires, are the unit: a fire can hold none, one or several urban conflagrations. Fires with one that hold two or more: Carlson 2002–22 4 of 8; Our 2002–26 record 6 of 10; CAL FIRE DINS 2013–25 5 of 7. Linking houses through continuous tree canopy, as well as by distance, catches wooded-town fires the house rule misses.
  7. 7
    15 October A check on fires held out from the start
    On 2016–2022 fires kept out of all of this work, none of the 66 with a block of 89 or fewer became an urban conflagration. It is a weak check: none of the held-out fires became one at all.
What we propose. Call a patch of at least 50 destroyed buildings, each within 30 m centre to centre (about 15 m wall to wall for typical houses) of another in the patch, an urban conflagration, and say plainly that 50 and 30 m are a stated convention, not a line the data draw. A fire can hold none, one or several. Report 50 m links as the sensitivity (in Our 2002–26 record they count 24 fires instead of 10), and keep the fires counted under every nearby rule apart from the borderline ones.

A patch grows hop by hop, so it follows streets instead of filling a circle

Each destroyed building joins the patch if it is within 30 m (centre to centre) of any destroyed building already in it. So a patch is a chain through a neighbourhood, not a circle, and an urban conflagration is a patch of 50 or more.Connected components of the graph joining destroyed building centres ≤ L apart (single linkage); working definition: a component of ≥ 50 at L = 30 m.

2 · What the data did and did not decide

A rule of this kind needs two numbers: how close two destroyed buildings must be to count as linked, and how many linked buildings make an urban conflagration. We asked whether the data, published work or a better definition settle either number.

Is there a natural size? No: the largest patch thins out smoothly from fire to fire

Each line shows, for one record, the share of fires whose largest patch of destroyed buildings is at least a given size. If urban conflagrations were a separate kind of fire, the lines would drop and then flatten, leaving a gap between ordinary fires and town fires. They don't: patch sizes thin out smoothly from 1 building to the largest, 378. A formal test for a second peak found none at any distance from 5 to 100 m in any record. Destroyed buildings cluster more than chance, building up between about 20 and 50 m

For each fire we redrew the same number of destroyed buildings at random among the fire's own buildings and compared the largest patch with the real one. Real patches are bigger than chance at every distance above 5 m. Half of the extra clustering is reached by 20 m between centres and nearly all of it by 30–50 m; beyond that it adds little. Published definitions and distances: words and ignition distances, no defining number

No published source gives a number that defines an urban conflagration in a wildfire; the definitions are in words (building-to-building spread that overwhelms firefighting), or a count of homes lost. The distances that recur are gaps between buildings at which one burning building ignites the next: 1.8–40 m wall to wall, most often 9.1–18 m. 3 · The proposal

What we conclude: a stated convention, reported with its sensitivity

The proposal, why it is a convention rather than a finding, and which fires are firmly in.Decision rule fixed before scoring: a challenger replaces the working rule only if it matches the published list at least as well in all three records and is better on at least two of stability, borderline fires and agreement between records. Nearby rules: 25 m / 50, 30 m / 100, 40 m / 50, 40 m / 100, 50 m / 100. Leave-one-event-out best cell: Carlson 2002–22 0.55, Our 2002–26 record 0.70, CAL FIRE DINS 2013–25 1.00.

Proposal. Call a patch of at least 50 destroyed buildings, each within 30 m centre to centre (about 15 m wall to wall for typical houses) of another in the patch, an urban conflagration, and say plainly that 50 and 30 m are a stated convention, not a line the data draw. A fire can hold none, one or several. Report 50 m links as the sensitivity (in Our 2002–26 record they count 24 fires instead of 10), and keep the fires counted under every nearby rule apart from the borderline ones.

Why a convention. The test we fixed in advance cannot pick a best definition: no alternative matched the published accounts at least as well while also being better on at least two of stability, fewer borderline fires and agreement between our record and CAL FIRE's, and the working rule does not sit on a stable plateau. Searching the whole grid afterwards for the best match picks 75 m links and at least 200 buildings, but chosen fairly (leaving each named fire out in turn) it matches no better than the working rule (0.70 vs 0.80 in Our 2002–26 record), so that is noise, not a better rule.

Firm and borderline fires (Our 2002–26 record). Counted under the working rule and every nearby one: Almeda Drive 2020, Eaton 2025, Palisades 2025, Sayre 2008, Tubbs 2017. Counted under the working rule but not every nearby one: Camp 2018, Glass 2020, Marshall 2021, Sandalwood 2019, Woolsey 2018.

How this panel was checked
  • The decision rule was written down before any definition was scored; the after-the-fact grid search is reported, not used to choose.
  • Wall-to-wall equivalent: the wall gap (from footprint size) whose 50-building fire list best matches 30 m between centres, in each record.

4 · Which fires became urban conflagrations: the 89-building block line

With the rule fixed, which fires get there? The size of the largest block of closely spaced buildings a fire reached sorts them.

No fire whose largest block held 89 buildings or fewer became an urban conflagration

Fires are grouped by the largest block of closely spaced buildings they reached. None of the fires whose largest block held 89 or fewer became an urban conflagration (0 of 338, 0 of 714, 0 of 429 in Carlson 2002–22, Our 2002–26 record, CAL FIRE DINS 2013–25); above 89, 8 of 23, 10 of 25, 7 of 16 did. 5 · What else matters

Beyond block size: wind, trees between houses, and conditions that differ between blocks of the same fire.

Every urban conflagration burned in a wind event, but so did nearly every near miss

A recognised wind event is a strong offshore pressure push for the region (such as Santa Ana or Diablo winds) or a National Weather Service wind warning or advisory while the fire burned. Every fire that became an urban conflagration burned in one; so did almost every fire that reached a big block and did not. Wind goes with large losses, but among fires that reached a big block it does not tell the two groups apart.Offshore push: minimum regional sea-level pressure difference while the fire burned in the most offshore 5% of hours of 1991–2020 (ERA5); NWS Extreme Wind / High Wind Warning or Wind Advisory in effect (Iowa Environmental Mesonet archive, from October 2005; earlier fires 'unknown' and left out of the counts). Measured wind over the town (peak sustained and gust) did not separate the groups either. Fire weather is described here; it is never part of the definition.
