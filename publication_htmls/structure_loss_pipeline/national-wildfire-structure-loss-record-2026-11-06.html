An NCEAS Research Program

National Wildfire Structure Loss Record

This research program is building a science-grade, building-by-building record of the buildings wildfires destroy across the United States, read from public satellite imagery and dated maps of the buildings that stood before each fire. It continues the U.S. Geological Survey's national record of building loss, which covers the lower 48 states from 2002 through 2022, past 2022 with one consistent, reproducible method. We believe the record will help the wildfire science community study where and why buildings are lost; give fire agencies, recovery and resilience programs a common yardstick for comparing fires and communities; and support research on building vulnerability by insurers, planners and building researchers. The method has now run end to end on 1,894 U.S. fires from 2016 to 2025. The United States, Australia, Canada and Europe are priority regions for this research; work outside the United States depends on suitable data and local research partners.

Research pathway

From a tested method to a national record, and beyond

The method is built and tested. Phase 1, now under way, continues the U.S. record; Phase 2 lists pilots outside the United States that depend on data and partners, rather than current promises.

Completed

Phase 0 · Build and test the method

We built a two-step system, a list of the buildings that stood before each fire and a satellite comparison of each one before and after, and tested it on fires it had never seen, against the USGS hand-mapped record and against on-the-ground inspections by CAL FIRE, FEMA and the American Red Cross.

  • Every fire is scored by a version of the model that never saw it (leave one fire out).
  • End-to-end tests on 40 recent fires with independent building-level inspections.
  • Building-by-building comparisons on the Palisades and Eaton fires of 2025.
  • First runs outside the United States, in Canada, Mexico and Europe.

This page reports research evidence. All maps and figures are research estimates from a model, not official damage assessments, and are reported with their limits.

Current research phase

Phase 1 · Continue the U.S. record past 2022

Phase 1 runs the same method on every U.S. wildfire that destroyed buildings, continuing the USGS 2002–2022 record with a consistent, documented series. The first complete run, 1,894 fires from 2016 to 2025 and about 3.2 million buildings that stood before them, finished in November 2026; building-level checks of that run against the USGS record are under way.

Interested in research collaboration?Contact Carlo Broderick
  • Score every run against independent field inspections wherever they exist.
  • Measure accuracy across landscapes, fire types and building patterns.
  • Document where the method fails: missed buildings, sheds and outbuildings, and damage that is hard to see from space.
  • Publish the methods, the validation evidence and aggregate results, and share building-level data where source licences allow.

Scientific independence: Phase 1 is a research plan, not an operational service. NCEAS follows the scientific process and reports positive, negative and inconclusive results. The record is built for research and retrospective assessment, not for incident response.

Depends on data and partners

Phase 2 · Test the method outside the United States

The method needs only pre-fire building maps, public satellite imagery and, for testing, independent field records, so it could be tested wherever those exist. In Australia, a first step would be a validation-only pilot on the 2019–20 Black Summer fires, compared with an Australian fire agency's field damage assessments under that agency's data-use terms.

  • Measure how well a system developed in the United States transfers to Australian fires and buildings.
  • Identify failure modes specific to each region and what data or method changes would fix them.
  • Scope a broader Australian structure-loss record with research partners and funders.
  • Extend the same tests in Canada and Europe, where early runs are reported below.

Decision gate: work outside the United States starts only with suitable data and local research partners. We welcome introductions to fire agencies, universities, building and roofing research groups, insurers and funding programs.

How it works

Public imagery in → building losses out

Step 1

List the buildings before the fire

For each fire, the system lists every building that stood inside the burned area before ignition, from dated building maps, and adds buildings no map holds by finding them in pre-fire aerial photos.

USDA aerial photos (NAIP)USA StructuresMicrosoft building footprintsOverture MapsSentinel-2Fire perimetersIncident reports (ICS-209)

All of these cover the lower 48 states; comparable building maps and imagery exist in many other countries.

Step 2

Compare before and after

A damage model compares each building in Sentinel-2 satellite images from before the fire and about 74 days after, and gives it a score from 0 to 1 of how likely it is to be destroyed, with a second look about a year later. A score of 0.88 or higher counts as destroyed.

Step 3

Check against the ground

The damage model learned from the USGS hand-mapped record and CAL FIRE inspections on 199 fires, and every result on this page comes from a version that never saw the fire it is scored on. Independent field records from fire agencies and relief organizations are the test. We are grateful to the agencies that share these records; their work is what makes this possible.

Results on fires the system had never seen

Performance on 40 recently inspected fires

These are research results, not a guarantee for any one fire. On 40 recent U.S. fires with independent building-by-building inspections, none of them used in training, the full system found 61% of destroyed buildings, and 72% of the buildings it called destroyed had been destroyed. Most misses happen before the damage step: the building was missing from the pre-fire list.

Early results outside the United States

The model was run unchanged, with no local training, on fires in other countries.

  • Jasper, Alberta, Canada · 2024Against the town's own damage assessment, the system found 71% of destroyed buildings, and 93% of its destroyed calls were right.
  • Europe · Copernicus emergency mappingIn 16 areas graded by the EU's Copernicus Emergency Management Service, with 245 destroyed buildings, it found 60%, or 86% leaving out one area in Montenegro.
  • British Columbia and Baja CaliforniaIts destroyed counts for two 2023 British Columbia fires fell within the reported totals; in Baja California the result depends on the building-size cut.
Not yet

Australia: not yet tested.

A validation pilot needs Australian field damage records. It would show how well the method transfers and what would need to change before a broader Australian record.

Early findings from the record

A few fires cause most of the loss

A consistent record lets us ask questions no single fire can answer. In every record we hold, a small share of fires destroys most buildings, and the rise in losses over the past two decades sits in those worst fires: buildings destroyed per year rose about 3.3 times between 2008–16 and 2017–25 in the federal situation reports, while the number of fires that destroyed any building barely moved. These are findings for discussion, not settled results.

Other findings so far

From the first analyses of the extended record, tested on three independent records.

  • Town fires need a big block of close buildingsNo fire whose largest block of closely spaced buildings held 89 or fewer became an urban conflagration, in any of the three records; every one that did burned in a wind event.
  • Mobile homes are lost more oftenInside a fire, mobile homes are destroyed more often than houses; whether crowded buildings are lost more often depends mostly on a handful of very large fires.
  • Choosing fires by their losses misleadsStudying only fires known for their losses, as most studies do, makes a typical fire look two to three times as destructive. A complete record avoids that.

Who could use it

One loss record, many uses

Wildfire science

  • Study where and why buildings are lost, with one method across two decades of fires.
  • Test ideas about building density, building type, wind and urban conflagration on complete records rather than famous fires.
  • Compare countries once pilots outside the United States are in place.

Fire agencies and recovery

  • Compare fires and seasons with the same yardstick, after the fact.
  • A consistent count where field inspections are incomplete or were never made.
  • Learn which building settings were lost most often, to inform future research and preparedness.

Building and insurance research

  • Relate losses to building characteristics; NCEAS's companion roof-characteristics project maps roof materials across the United States.
  • Study building vulnerability and recovery with the same buildings before and after.

Planning and resilience programs

  • See where blocks of closely spaced buildings sit next to wildland, the setting of every urban conflagration in our records.
  • Track whether losses change after programs start, with one method over time.
