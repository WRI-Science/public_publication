Structure Loss Pipeline · historical companion

The historical companion

The published historical record of building loss in United States wildfires — 361 fires between 2002 and 2022, hand-assembled by Carlson and colleagues — is a point map: one dot per building, carrying the survey's own call of whether that building was destroyed or survived, and almost nothing else. This project attaches a building outline and a set of 60-centimetre image cells to every one of its 1,770,939 points, publishes the extra per-building attributes that come with them, and measures how far each of those attributes can be trusted.

What this page gives you

Every building point in the historical record now has a shape. Across all 361 fires and 26 states, each of the 1,770,939 points carries a polygon, its own image cells wherever imagery exists to support them, and a flag saying which of four methods produced the shape. The published point itself never moves. Ten of these attributes also travel with every row of the combined dataset, joined at 100.0000 % coverage.

The one caveat to carry with you: how well a shape can be found depends on whether the building burned. A present-day database of building outlines matches only 49 % of the points the record calls destroyed against 82 % of the points it calls surviving, because a building that burned in 2014 is often simply absent from a database compiled in 2022. Finding buildings instead with this project's own detector, run on aerial photography taken before the fire, cuts that gap by 61.9 % — and does not close it: the detector still misses 33.9 % of destroyed buildings against 21.0 % of surviving ones.

01 · what is attached

The record's point is the anchor; everything else hangs off it

Nothing in this product edits the published record. No row is dropped, moved, merged or relabelled; the damage call, the exposure class, the fire identifier and the coordinates are exactly as published. Everything described below is attached to that row, and every attached value records where it came from and how much it is worth.

Two things come from outside the record. Building footprint databases are public maps of building outlines — Microsoft's US Building Footprints and the FEMA / Oak Ridge USA Structures layer — and they supply a ready-made polygon whenever the record's point falls inside one. This project's building detector is a neural network trained on aerial photography: the US Department of Agriculture's National Agriculture Imagery Program, which photographs the continental United States at roughly 60 cm per pixel and reflies every two to three years. The detector finds buildings the databases miss, and it is run only on photography flown before the fire, so nothing it sees is contaminated by the fire's aftermath.

Each row therefore gains a polygon, a count of the 60-centimetre image cells that belong to that building, a source flag naming which of four methods produced the shape, and a confidence value that is null wherever the shape was inferred rather than observed. This page is about that geometry and its limits. How accurately this project calls a building destroyed is a separate question, answered with full confusion matrices on the confusion matrix page.

02 · the measured caveat

Buildings that burned are harder to find a shape for than buildings that did not

Four ways of supplying a shape were measured on the same buildings, split by whether the record calls the building destroyed or surviving. If a source were unbiased the two shares would match. They do not, and the size of the mismatch is the number an analyst needs.

⚠️ Never show a figure pooled across imagery eras. The photography available before a fire is not the same in every decade, and a fire with no pre-fire aerial photography at all has a detector match of zero by construction. A combined figure would therefore report the absence of a photograph as a failure of the detector. Each imagery stratum is measured and reported on its own; the table above is the stratum in which the detector runs at its full native resolution.

On the 106 fires whose pre-fire photography is 1-metre imagery upsampled to the detector's working resolution — 234,294 buildings, 12,289 destroyed — the same comparison gives a footprint gap of 0.482 against a detector gap of 0.068, an 86.0 % reduction, but on a coarser image and a much smaller destroyed count. The 103 fires with no pre-fire photography have footprints and circles only.

Source: confound.json, by_era.detector_upsampled and by_era.no_imagery.

Independent published work finds the same pattern. A separate analysis of this question, using present-day footprints across 178 fires between 2017 and 2022 in 18 states and 159,955 buildings, matches 0.461 of destroyed buildings against 0.896 of surviving ones — a gap of 0.435. Pre-fire photography is what narrows a gap of this kind, from 0.337 to 0.128 here, and nothing else available does.

Source: the published-comparison row of confound.json (prior_art_reference).

⚠️ The residual gap is real and it is yours to carry. Thirteen points of gap remain after the detector: it misses 33.9 % of destroyed buildings against 21.0 % of surviving ones. Some of that is buildings genuinely invisible at 60 cm — small, under canopy, shaded — and some is that the buildings which burn are, on average, the ones a machine finds hardest. Any analysis that uses these polygons to compare destroyed with surviving buildings inherits that residual and must say so. The per-building columns make it filterable: every row records which source produced its shape, and a confidence value that is null wherever the shape was inferred rather than observed.

03 · how a shape is chosen

Four ranked sources, and the row tells you which one it got

The sources are tried in order and the first one that produces a geometry wins. The choice is recorded for every building, so any source can be filtered out.

Reading it: 81.3 % of buildings take a real footprint polygon and 5.9 % an outline the detector drew, while 12.8 % get a circle standing in for a shape nobody ever observed.

Source: the four geometry_source__* columns of outputs/carlson_augmented/summary.csv on the data share, summed over 361 fires (1,770,939 rows).

Rank 1 uses a present-day database deliberately, and the row says so. A strictly pre-fire filter would leave rank 1 empty for nearly every fire before 2013: one California county's entire extract carries two scene dates, both in 2015, so a pre-fire filter keeps 0 of its 7,673 footprints. Every rank-1 row therefore carries the footprint's own image date and a flag saying whether that date precedes the fire, so a user who needs strictly pre-fire shapes can keep only those — on almost every fire, that is none of them.

The detector's own inputs are strictly pre-fire. The reference band the network reads is built only from footprints dated before ignition; a county with no pre-fire footprint at all is logged and skipped. No post-fire polygon ever enters what the model sees.

Where ranks 1 and 2 both exist, they agree. The median intersection-over-union — the shared area of two outlines divided by their combined area, so 1 is a perfect overlap — between the footprint polygon and the detector's outline for the same building is 0.81, and 96 % of pairs are within a factor of two on area. That is the check that the detector is finding the building and not its driveway. (summary.csv columns agree_iou_median, agree_area_within_2x, median over fires.)

⚠️ A circle is not a roof. 12.8 % of buildings have a circle rather than a shape, sitting over whatever happens to be there — ground, vegetation, a driveway. Filter on the cell-kind column before any analysis that assumes the polygon covers a building surface.

Source: manifest.json in the release directory on the data share (5,409,626,355 bytes, 7,447 files, 7,448 checksum lines verified), and the built directory measured on the data share; the vector total is the gpkg_bytes column of summary.csv.

The probability surface is the one product a user rebuilds rather than downloads. A probability raster stores, for each 60-centimetre cell, how confident the detector is that a building is there; points can be re-cut from it at any confidence threshold with no graphics hardware at all. So 5.41 GB ships and the rest stays regenerable. Every building still resolves to its own 60-centimetre cells in the smaller archive, because it is the instance raster — not the probability raster — that carries the identifier.

Reading it: a positive cell value is the identity of a building in the record, a negative one is a building the detector found that the record does not list, and zero is ground with nothing on it.

Source: the raster_cell_kind__* columns of summary.csv on the data share, and the column dictionary carlson-augmented-schema.md shipped with the release. Cell kinds over all 361 fires: 691,535 detector cells, 743,759 footprint cells and 220,986 circle cells, and 114,659 buildings with no cell of their own. That count ships as a column, raster_n_cells, so a reader can filter on it rather than infer it.

⚠️ 114,659 buildings — 6.47 % — own no image cell at all. A cell can hold only one identifier, so where two record buildings share a single footprint the second is left without one. The shortfall tracks the photography: 9.17 % on fires with no pre-fire imagery, against 4.46 % at native resolution and 3.29 % on upsampled imagery, and worst of all in 2008, at 18.8 %. Those rows are recorded rather than hidden: the cell count is a column, and zero is a legitimate value in it.

Source: outputs/carlson_augmented/summary.csv on the data share, column n_structures_without_a_cell, summed and grouped by era and by fire_year.

06 · reading it safely

Three things to do before you use these polygons

  1. De-duplicate on geometry before any pooled statistic. 18.3 % of the record's national rows sit at a location that more than one fire claims. Two rows for one building get two different identifiers and two different polygons, because each fire's chain of sources ran independently, on that fire's own photography and its own footprint vintage.
  2. Filter out inferred shapes for anything that assumes a roof. One column finds every inferred cell across the whole product, whatever era the fire falls in, and a second gives the number of cells a building actually owns — zero is a legitimate value and appears on 114,659 rows.
  3. Split by the destroyed flag, always, and never across imagery eras. The residual 13-point matching gap means a statistic pooled across destroyed and surviving buildings is measuring the two populations unequally, and a statistic pooled across the three imagery eras is measuring the photography rather than the buildings.

Source: the full column dictionary, carlson-augmented-schema.md, shipped with the release. Ten of these columns are in the combined dataset, joined on 100.0000 % of the record's 1,770,939 rows over 361 fires at a 0.5 m coincidence tolerance, with a maximum coordinate displacement of 1.3 × 10⁻⁸ m (augmented_geometry_coverage.csv in the release directory on the data share, 361 rows, minimum fraction_joined 1.0). The building identifiers here are this project's own: the record's published release carries no structure identifier of any kind.
