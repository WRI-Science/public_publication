Step zero: the pre-fire inventory

Both models only score buildings on this list. A house missing here is missing from the loss count, so the list is fixed from imagery taken before ignition.

Start

Footprint databases

FEMA USA Structures and Microsoft footprints, kept only if drawn from imagery dated before the fire.

Fill the gaps

Roof detector

Segmentation on 0.6 m aerial imagery from the last three flights. It keeps only roofs the footprints missed.

Output

Tagged inventory

One point per building, tagged by source, size (major ≥ 50 m²) and a triage score. Suspect ones are flagged, never deleted.

FEMA USA Structures · Microsoft Building Footprints · USDA NAIP aerials · scored on held-out ground, match within 10 m

Before either call, every fire needs a list of the buildings that stood there. Footprints: FEMA USA Structures and Microsoft's machine-generated footprints; each polygon carries the date of its source imagery, and we admit it only if that date is before ignition, because a footprint traced after the fire cannot contain a house that burned. Detector: a segmentation network on 4-band 0.6 m NAIP imagery, with the footprints fed in as a fifth band so it learns to fill gaps rather than redraw them. Its probability map is thresholded, patches of at least 20 square metres become points, and any patch overlapping a footprint (+3 m) is dropped as redundant. Running it on the latest flight plus the two before it (states are flown every two to three years) was the largest single gain on forested rural fires. Tags: source (which database, or detector and flight year), size class (major above 50 m², the USGS definition, or minor), and a triage score for footprints. About one footprint in seven inside a burned area has no match in the USGS record; the detector cannot yet separate phantoms reliably (best precision 36 percent), so they are ranked and flagged for a person, not removed. Numbers against the USGS record on held-out ground: recall 0.768 footprints alone, 0.809 with the detector; precision 0.83. Limits: roughly a fifth of major buildings are still missed, especially under closed canopy; an optional post-fire pass looks for pads and debris in the next aerial flight. Figures from the pipeline explainer dated 9 September 2026.

These are two separately trained models, not one model run twice. Second call model: a year of evidence

A separately trained model. It starts from the same base signals, then adds what only time can show.

Satellite embeddings

A yearly 64-number summary of every 10 m pixel. The year before vs the year after the fire.

A year of change

Every clear look out to 12 months: does the site recover, stay bare, or get rebuilt?

A second after-image

A later post-fire window, after smoke and ash have cleared.

Trade-off: it waits a year, and embeddings start in 2017, so it covers fewer fires.

Plus a neural column: the one-year network's probability, as one more signal.

damage_sentinel2_refined_final_v1 · called destroyed at 0.88 or above

Embeddings: Google DeepMind AlphaEarth Foundations annual satellite embeddings, a 64-dimension vector per 10 m pixel per year, available from 2017. We use a clean pre-fire year and the first post-fire year. Dynamics: per-scene change features out to +365 days. Second window: a later post-fire composite. The one-year neural column adds +0.102 per fire [+0.087, +0.119] over the base signals. This is the production second call model since v0.11.0: Carlson 0.925 pooled / 0.828 per fire, CAL FIRE 0.969 / 0.933.

The neural network reads the whole image history

Every clear scene

Dozens of Sentinel-2 looks per building, before and after

One token per look

bands, burn indices, change vs its own pre-fire self, date

Transformer + CNN

over time, plus the image patch around the building

One new column

its probability joins the tree model's table

Trained honestly

Each fire's column comes from a network that never saw that fire

Augmented

Random brightness and timing shifts teach it to ignore haze and revisit gaps

Time-legal

For the first call it sees only the first 74 days of the fire it scores

Tested and dropped: bigger networks, pooling, recurrent encoders, label-noise losses, sun-angle inputs

Tokens are 12 reflectance channels (the building's own pixel and its 3x3 mean) plus six indices and their difference from the building's own pre-fire mean; up to 128 time slots with a sinusoidal date encoding. A small transformer reads the sequence; a small CNN reads the 3x3 patch (wider 5x5 and 7x7 patches are being tested on the full imagery). Stacking: the network outputs one probability per building, joined as a column into the gradient-boosted tree, which learns how much to trust it. Leave-one-fire-out end to end. Augmentation that won: radiometric jitter plus time jitter, without rotations (+0.0059 over with rotations, canonical). Headline: +0.076 per fire [+0.062, +0.091] for the full first call model over the 62-signal base. Both networks are saved and score every fire in the record. A net trained on randomly truncated histories (all horizons) was tested and gives no gain at 74 days (+0.002, interval spans zero); an earlier screen figure (+0.0325) was a caching error and is withdrawn.

How both models learn and are tested

Training data

199 fires

191 Carlson fires, 2016–2022, 18 states
8 CAL FIRE incidents, 2024–2025
819,000 buildings · 73,000 destroyed

Testing

Leave one fire out. Every fire is scored by a model that never saw it, 199 times over.

Two scores. Pooled F1 counts every building; per-fire F1 counts every fire equally.

Four seeds, averaged, with intervals from resampling whole fires.

Carlson training fires start in 2016 because that is when Sentinel-2 imagery begins

Pool: 818,838 buildings, 73,443 destroyed. Carlson fires before 2016 predate Sentinel-2, so they are not in the training pool; they remain in the published record verbatim. F1 balances false alarms and misses. Per-fire F1 is the primary metric (a fire with 20 destroyed homes counts as much as Camp); pooled F1 is reported beside it. Fires with fewer than 100 buildings or 10 destroyed are reported but excluded from the per-fire average (188 of 191 Carlson fires qualify). Intervals come from resampling whole fires, never individual buildings, because buildings in one fire are not independent.

F1: one score for misses and false alarms

Precision

Of the buildings we call destroyed, the share that really were. Low precision means false alarms.

Recall

Of the buildings really destroyed, the share we caught. Low recall means misses.

F1

A balance of the two. It is high only when both are high; 1.0 is perfect.

Worked example

A fire destroys 100 buildings. The model calls 95 destroyed: 90 right, 5 false alarms. It misses 10.

Precision

90 / 95 = 0.95

Recall

90 / 100 = 0.90

F1

0.92

Why not accuracy? Only about 7% of buildings are destroyed, so a model that calls every building "survived" is 93% accurate, yet its F1 is zero.

Every F1 in this deck scores the destroyed call at the 0.88 cut, with each fire held out of training

F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall: 2 x P x R / (P + R), which equals 2TP / (2TP + FP + FN). Harmonic rather than ordinary mean, so a model cannot hide weak recall behind strong precision or the reverse. Worked example: TP 90, FP 5, FN 10 gives precision 0.947, recall 0.900, F1 = 180 / 195 = 0.923. True negatives (surviving buildings correctly called survived) do not enter F1 at all, which is why it suits a rare outcome like destruction; accuracy is dominated by them. The calls depend on the threshold: a building is called destroyed when its probability is 0.88 or above, and moving that cut trades precision against recall. Pooled F1 counts every building in one pile; per-fire F1 computes F1 for each fire and averages, so a small fire counts as much as Camp. AUC, shown later, is threshold-free.

Why no model can score 1.0: the label ceiling

Where Carlson and CAL FIRE both assessed the same buildings, they disagree on about 2% of them.

We grade our model against those labels. When the label itself is wrong, a correct call is scored as a mistake.

So even a perfect model tops out below 1.0. That cap is the ceiling.

How we know: 43,929 buildings were assessed by both teams. A statistical model reads their pattern of agreement across 14 fires and estimates each team's error rate, without assuming either one is right.

Label ceiling, Carlson

0.93

pooled F1

0.86

per-fire F1

43,929 buildings assessed by both sources on 14 California fires

Estimated with a latent-class (Hui-Walter) model that infers each source's error rates without assuming either is right: CAL FIRE sensitivity 0.995 / specificity 0.984; Carlson 0.986 / 0.990. Applied at our own share of destroyed buildings (about 7%), that gives the best achievable F1: 0.934 pooled, 0.859 per fire. It is a conservative estimate: we already slightly exceed the matching AUC ceiling, so real headroom is somewhat larger. Much of the disagreement is definitional (Carlson's protocol excludes mobile homes and minor structures; CAL FIRE inspects them) rather than one team being careless.

AUC: how well the model ranks, before any cut

Pick one destroyed and one surviving building at random. AUC is the chance the model scores the destroyed one higher.

0.5 is a coin flip; 1.0 is perfect ordering. F1 also depends on where the 0.88 cut falls; AUC does not.

First call model0.9940.9790.991
Second call model0.9960.9830.995

High AUC, low F1 means the ranking is right but the cut is wrong. That is the pattern on Plains grass fires, where a region-aware threshold is the next fix.

Area under the ROC curve · leave-one-fire-out · four-seed mean probability · per fire = mean over 188 Carlson fires

AUC = area under the receiver operating characteristic curve; it measures ordering only, across every possible threshold. Both final models rank destroyed above surviving buildings about 99.5 percent of the time. The remaining F1 errors are mostly about where scores fall relative to the fixed 0.88 cut, not about ordering. External Plains fires (Oklahoma 2025 grass fires) showed high AUC with low recall under an earlier model, which is why a region- or prevalence-aware threshold is the next improvement. The AUC ceiling implied by label noise is about 0.988, which we exceed; the F1 ceiling is therefore conservative.
