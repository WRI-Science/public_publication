Structure Loss Pipeline · results by fire

Results by fire

This project estimates, building by building, which structures a wildfire destroyed, using free satellite imagery and public maps of building outlines. One row of the table below is one fire: how many buildings stood in the area around it, how many the model calls destroyed, and how many a ground survey recorded as destroyed. Across the fifteen California fires with a full inspection record, the estimate scores a mean F1 per fire of 0.788 on the destroyed class — F1 being the combined measure of how often our destroyed calls are right and how much of the real destruction we find.

How to read a row

A row is one fire, and the two columns to compare are the two destroyed counts. Destroyed, primary estimate is this project's answer: it is made about two and a half months after a fire starts, from before-and-after satellite images, and it exists for every building in every fire here. Destroyed, ground truth is the number of buildings a survey on the ground recorded as destroyed. Everything else in the row describes how well those two agree, and where they disagree.

Ground truth means somebody went and looked. For the California fires it is CAL FIRE's damage inspection programme: after a fire, inspectors visit each structure inside the burn area and record whether it was destroyed, damaged or untouched. The two fires outside California are scored instead against independent damage assessments — one-off post-fire surveys by FEMA and by Boulder County, drawn by people with no connection to this project.

No fire is scored by a model that had seen it. Eight of the fifteen California fires contributed training examples to the damage model; each of those is scored by a copy of the model refitted with that fire removed, marked held out. The other fires never entered training at all and are marked never trained on. Either way, the model that scored a fire never saw that fire.

A later, more accurate estimate also exists. A refined estimate is published about a year after each fire, once a second post-fire image window and a year-over-year satellite summary of the landscape exist; it is more accurate, and it covers most buildings in most fires but not all. This page reports the primary estimate throughout — see the method in full for how the two are built.

the headline

How close the estimate comes, fire by fire

Source: destroyed-class F1 per fire, with the realised per-fire cut-off, from forward_damage/per_fire.csv on the data share (15 fires, generated 2026-09-15); the summary row from the matching metrics.json.

What precision, recall and F1 mean here. Precision is the share of the buildings we called destroyed that really were. Recall is the share of the really destroyed buildings that we found. F1 is their harmonic mean, so it punishes both mistakes and cannot be gamed by being timid or by over-calling. The four underlying counts — agreed destroyed, missed, false alarm, agreed standing — are set out on the confusion matrix page.

Why we do not quote accuracy. Only about nine buildings in a hundred inside a fire's study area are destroyed. A model that called every building “survived” would therefore be right about nine times out of ten while finding nothing at all, so a high accuracy figure would tell a reader nothing.

What “matched at 10 m” means. A ground-truth point and one of our building locations are treated as the same building when they lie within 10 m of one another, matched one to one so that no building is counted twice. Precision, recall and F1 are computed on those matched pairs only. A destroyed building the survey knows about but our list of buildings never contained is therefore not blamed on the damage call: it counts against detection recall, the separate column showing what share of the survey's buildings we had at all. The 20 m column repeats the whole exercise with the matching distance loosened, as a check that the results do not hinge on where exactly a point was placed.

What the study area is. The area scored around a fire is the official fire perimeter plus a 2.4 km margin. Buildings inside the perimeter, or just outside it, are the directly exposed ones; buildings further out are counted in the inventory but are very rarely destroyed.

What the cut-off is. The model gives every building a probability of having been destroyed. The published call treats a building as destroyed when that probability exceeds 0.88, a number fixed in advance and identical for every fire. AUC, in the last numeric column, measures how well the probabilities rank destroyed buildings above surviving ones whatever cut-off you choose.

Why two kinds of average. A pooled figure counts every building equally, so the largest fires dominate it; a mean per fire counts every fire equally, so the smallest fire in the table weighs exactly as much as the largest. The two answer different questions — “how good is this on a building?” against “how good is this on a fire?” — so both are reported. In the summary row of the table below the counts are pooled across fires, while the precision, recall and F1 cells are averages over fires.

a second column

One call for classifying buildings, one for counting them

The published per-building file carries two destroyed calls, and the table above shows both. The primary one applies the same fixed cut-off of 0.88 to every fire. The count-tuned one sets its cut-off separately for each fire, reading only the spread of that fire's own model scores and nothing about the fire itself. The two are built for different questions: the fixed cut-off is the better per-building classifier, and the count-tuned one gets a fire's total closer to what inspectors found.

Reading it: the count-tuned call finds more of the destroyed buildings and is wrong more often when it does, which nets out against it on F1 while bringing the fire total much closer.

⚠️ What the count-tuned call buys is the total. Across the fifteen fires' whole building inventories it calls 16,370 buildings destroyed, against the fixed cut-off's 14,466 and the inspectors' 17,902 — so the share of the inspected loss that the published count reproduces rises from 0.808 to 0.914. The two columns therefore disagree in a legible way: the fixed cut-off is the better classifier of an individual building, and the count-tuned one gets the fire total much closer. Which one a reader wants depends on whether the question is about a building or about a fire, which is why both are published. In the per-building file, use damage_class to classify buildings and damage_class_adaptive if you are counting them, and say which column you used. Every headline figure on this site is the fixed cut-off.

outside California

The two fires scored against other people's assessments

South Fork (New Mexico, June 2024) is the only fire outside California in the published dataset, and the only verified per-building damage assessment outside California since 2022: federal search-and-rescue observations released by the village that burned. It is scored on the area the assessment actually covered (39.691 km²) rather than the whole 17,569-acre fire, because an assessment is not a census and scoring beyond its edge would turn every uninspected building into a false alarm by construction. At the fixed cut-off it scores precision 0.982, recall 0.739 and F1 0.843 on 1,637 matched pairs, with an AUC of 0.986; the count-tuned call reaches F1 0.874, and the best cut-off this fire could possibly have been given would reach 0.946 — a gap of +0.103 between the fixed cut-off and a perfectly chosen one. The fire's study area holds 9,568 buildings, 2,357 of them directly exposed (inside the perimeter or just outside it) and 7,211 further out; 732 are called destroyed at the fixed cut-off and 785 under the count-tuned one, all of them among the directly exposed.

Its detection recall (0.711) is lower than most of the California fires for a reason a user should know: Microsoft's building-footprint layer is not available for New Mexico here, so the starting list of buildings is the FEMA / Oak Ridge USA Structures layer alone, and the Lincoln County extract of that layer carries a single image date of 2017-05-26 — seven years before the fire. Buildings put up after that date are not in the starting list, and our building detector has to find them from aerial photography.

Marshall (Colorado, December 2021) is not part of the published dataset; it sits before the period the dataset covers. It is here because it is the only fire in this project with public per-building damage assessments that nobody here drew — two of them, in fact: a door-to-door field assessment by Boulder County, and a remote-sensing assessment by the FEMA Modeling and Analysis Center, which the table below shortens to FEMA MAC. Both are scored against the same inventory of 14,786 buildings.

Reading it: against two surveys drawn independently of this project, the estimate scores a destroyed-class F1 of 0.962 against the county's field assessment and 0.928 against the FEMA one, and the number of buildings it calls destroyed lands within a few per cent of the assessed total either way.

On these three assessments the count-tuned call is the stronger of the two. It is ahead on two of the three — Boulder County 0.9622 → 0.9719 and South Fork 0.8431 → 0.8740 at a per-fire cut-off of 0.827 — and behind on the third, FEMA 0.9278 → 0.9196. South Fork is the clearest case: the best cut-off that fire could have been given would reach 0.946, on an AUC of 0.986, so most of what the fixed cut-off costs there is recoverable in principle. Three assessments over two fires is not a distribution, and readers outside California should treat both numbers as indicative rather than settled. The wider evidence — nine independent assessments in six states — is on beyond California.

A winter fire gives a thinner stack of images, and the estimate still holds up. Sentinel-2, the pair of free European Space Agency satellites this project reads, photographs every point on Earth every few days at 10 m per pixel, but cloud and snow throw many of those views away. Marshall's before-fire window gives a median of 6 clear views per building and its after-fire window a median of 2, against 9–17 on a summer California fire. Even so, only a handful of its 14,774 scored buildings could not be called at all.

the per-fire maps

Look at a single fire

Each map is a self-contained page: the pre-fire list of buildings drawn on satellite imagery, coloured by the probability that each was destroyed, with the ground-truth survey beside it. A whole fire carries tens of thousands of buildings and will not fit in one browsable page, so each map draws full detail inside the densest 800 m neighbourhoods, plus the one neighbourhood outside them holding the most disagreements with the survey, and covers the rest of the fire with a coarse 250 m grid of rates. Each map states its own sampling.
