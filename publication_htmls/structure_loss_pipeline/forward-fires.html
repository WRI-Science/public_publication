Fire by fire

Every fire this pipeline has produced a per-structure loss call for, and the inspection record it was scored against. Fifteen California fires of 2024–2025 make up the modern half of the published dataset; two more sit outside it — one outside California and one inside the historical era — because they have damage assessments nobody in this project drew.

How to read this table

Detection and damage are scored apart. Matching is greedy one-to-one at 10 m between our inventory and the inspection records, so a building we never found counts against detection recall and not against the loss call. Precision, recall and F1 are for the destroyed class, on matched pairs only.

Eight of the fifteen fires taught the damage model, and none of them is scored by it. Each carries a probability from a model refitted without that fire, marked lofo; the other seven are scored by the production model, marked production.

Every fire carries two loss calls. The primary one uses a single threshold of 0.85 for every fire; the advisory one uses a threshold fitted per fire from that fire's own score distribution and nothing else. The advisory column is the better count and the worse classifier — two loss calls, below, is why.

Two loss calls, and why the fixed threshold is the primary one

A per-fire threshold rule that reads only the model's own scores — never anything about the fire itself — was built to replace the single fixed cut, and on a table of inspected structures it clears a five-criterion bar written down before it was scored. On the product it does not, and the reason is not a flaw in the rule. It was validated on one population and applied to another.

The rule reads the fire's own score distribution and nothing else — that is what makes it label-free, and it is also what makes it move when the population underneath it changes. On a table of inspected structures where roughly half burned, the expected-F1 threshold sits near the fixed one. On a whole inventory where nine structures in a hundred burned, the same arithmetic pulls the threshold down and the model starts calling structures destroyed that nobody ever looked at. Taking the threshold from the near-perimeter zone, whose share destroyed is nearer the validation table's, is what keeps it in range.

⚠️ What the advisory call buys is the count. Over the fifteen fires' whole inventories it calls 15,951 structures destroyed against the fixed cut's 14,504 and DINS's 17,902 — a ratio of 0.810 → 0.891. So the two columns disagree in a specific, legible way: the fixed cut is the better classifier per structure and the adaptive cut gets the total much closer. Which one a reader wants depends on whether they are asking about a building or about a fire, and that is exactly why both ship. The release notes put it in one line: "use damage_class to classify buildings; use damage_class_adaptive if you are counting them" — and say which column you used. Every headline on this site is the fixed cut.

The rule was scored against the same five criteria on the product's own population, and no variant clears them — five candidates, three populations, the bar written down before anything was scored. That is why the advisory call is advisory and the fixed threshold is the default. The best candidate is the one that ships: it takes the threshold from the near-perimeter zone, costs −0.0186 [−0.0336, −0.0070] mean-fire F1, gets the count to 0.891 of the inspected total, and gains outside California, where mean-fire F1 goes 0.8986 → 0.9121.

One leak a reader would reasonably suspect is closed by measurement rather than assertion: re-fitting the calibration map leave-one-fire-out gives a variant that is bit-identical on 204,797 of 204,797 rows, because the product already reads each held-out fire's own fold calibration.

Source: outputs/operating_product/{decision.md,report.md,by_variant.csv,paired_delta_ci.csv}; the bar was pre-registered in logs/CHANGELOG.md before it was scored.

Outside California the advisory call splits, and one fire cannot speak to it at all. On Marshall's two independent assessments: Boulder County 0.9705 → 0.9725, FEMA MAC 0.9273 → 0.9109. And on South Fork NM 2024 — the only post-2022 non-California per-structure truth set that exists — the advisory call is clearly the better of the two: F1 0.8484 → 0.8825 at its Direct-scoped cut of 0.756, against an own-cut ceiling of 0.945 and an AUC of 0.985. Three assessments over two fires is not a distribution, but all of this project's out-of-state evidence points the same way: the ranking travels and the fixed cut does not.

The two fires that are not in the dataset

South Fork (NM, June 2024) is the only verified post-2022 per-structure damage assessment outside California — FEMA Urban Search & Rescue observations published by the Village of Ruidoso. It is scored on the assessment hull (39.691 km²), not the whole fire, because an assessment is not a census and enumerating over the whole 17,569-acre fire would make every uninspected building a false positive by construction. It scores P 0.982 / R 0.747 / F1 0.848 at the fixed threshold on 1,637 matched pairs, AUC 0.985, against F1 0.883 under the advisory one and 0.945 at the fire's own best threshold — a threshold gap of +0.097. It runs on its own perimeter frame: 9,568 structures, 2,357 in the near-perimeter zone and 7,211 beyond it, 738 called destroyed at the fixed threshold and 799 under the advisory one, all of them near the perimeter. Its detection recall (0.661) is low for structural reasons: New Mexico has no cached Microsoft footprint layer, so the detector's prior band is USA Structures alone, and Lincoln County's USA Structures extract carries a single IMAGE_DATE of 2017-05-26 — seven years before the fire.

Marshall (CO, December 2021) is in the historical era, not the modern one, so it is not in the release. It is here because it is the only fire in this project with a public per-structure assessment nobody in this pipeline drew — two of them, in fact, plus Carlson's own points. Marshall also answered the winter question. Its pre-fire window gives a median of 6 clear Sentinel-2 observations per structure and its post-fire window a median of 2, against 9–17 on a summer California fire — and even so only a handful of its 14,774 scored structures could not be called. Snow does not stop it; the post composite is simply thin.

the per-fire maps

Look at a fire

Each map is one self-contained page: the pre-fire inventory drawn on satellite imagery, coloured by predicted destroyed probability, with the inspection record beside it. Whole fires carry tens of thousands of points and will not fit in one browsable page, so each map draws full detail inside the densest 800 m neighbourhoods plus the cell holding the most model misses outside them — the interesting failure — and covers the rest of the fire with a coarse 250 m rate grid. The sampling is stated on the map itself.
