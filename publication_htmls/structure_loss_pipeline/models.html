Structure Loss Pipeline · the production models

The two production models

Two models decide whether each building was destroyed. The first call model answers about 74 days after a fire starts; the second call model answers about a year later, once more satellite imagery exists, and is more accurate. Both are evaluated on 199 fires, each fire scored by a copy of the model that never saw it. This page describes what each model reads, how accurate it is and where it is weakest.

what they read

Inputs, including the neural column

Both models are gradient-boosted decision trees. Every input is measured at the building from public Sentinel-2 imagery — two free European Space Agency satellites that photograph every point on Earth about every five days at 10 m per pixel. No fire attribute (its size, perimeter, containment or reported losses) and no location is an input.

  • First call model (236 inputs): how the ground at and around the building changed between the last clear images before the fire and the first ones after it, that change measured against the rest of the fire and against the building's neighbours, the spread of change among the buildings within 250 m and 1 km, and a before-image drawn from the same season as the after-image.
  • Second call model (243 inputs): the same before-and-after change, plus a second after-image about a year out, the year-over-year change in a freely published annual satellite summary, and how each building's patch of ground behaved scene by scene through the year after the fire — a step down that stays down, or a dip that recovers.
  • The neural column is part of each model. A small neural network reads the whole series of satellite scenes around each building — every clear scene before the fire and, for the first call, every scene up to 74 days after it; for the second, up to a year — and its output is fed to the model as one input among the others. It accounts for most of each model's gain over the model this project used before. The network that scores a fire never trained on that fire.

harder tests

What happens when more than one fire is held out

Holding out one fire at a time still lets the model learn from that fire's neighbours in space and time. So both models were also refitted with a whole region, state or year held out, and with two fires held out together. The number is the penalty: the held-out score minus the one-fire-out score on the same historical fires, so negative means worse.

The tree model was refitted for every design, but the neural column was held out only by fire: under a region, state or year hold-out, the network that scored a fire may have trained on other fires from the same region, state or year. A stricter test, with the network refitted for each design, is in progress.

In one sentence: a per-fire score here is honest for a fire like the ones already in the pool, and costs up to about a point of F1 for a fire in a region the model has not seen — at least that much.

before you use them

Limitations

  • The calls in the current download are not yet these models'. The neural column can only be computed for a new fire once its network is saved for reuse, which is in progress. Until then these models are evaluated on the 199 fires above, and the per-building calls in the published dataset are the ones made by the previous models. The next release rescores the dataset with these models and adds the fires of 2023–2026.
  • Grass fires on the Great Plains, Oklahoma's in particular, are the weakest region for both models, with wide intervals. Treat a per-fire number there with care.
  • Small fires. Fires that destroyed 10–100 buildings score clearly below larger ones, and fires that destroyed fewer than ten cannot be evaluated yet.
  • The inspection ground truth is Californian. Outside California, the only per-building evidence is the historical census.
