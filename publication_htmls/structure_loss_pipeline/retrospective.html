Structure Loss Pipeline · the era test

Running the pipeline backwards

If you join a hand-digitised record that stops in 2022 to a machine-made one that starts in 2023, the first question is whether a change across that join is a change in fires or a change in method. The only way to answer it is to run the new method on old fires the old record already covers. This page is that experiment.

What this page tells you

Take a destroyed count from this pipeline for a fire in the Carlson era and you get about three-quarters of what Carlson reported — and the missing quarter is buildings we never found, not buildings we mis-called. That factor is roughly flat across 2016–2022 and it is noisy from fire to fire, so a trend across the 2022/2023 boundary is threatened by which fires are in each half, not by when they burned.

Sixteen California fires were rebuilt end to end on their own public perimeters, with only footprints that predate each fire, and scored against Carlson's own census. Twenty-three carry the damage half. None of them is a row in the published dataset.

01 · the fires

Which old fires can carry the test, and why most cannot

Every California fire in Carlson's 2016–2022 window — eighty-three of them — was put through four admission tests, and the test each failure failed is written into the candidate table rather than the fire simply being absent from it.

  1. Carlson holds it properly — at least 100 destroyed and 100 surviving buildings, the same bar a training fire faces, so neither truth source enters on easier terms.
  2. An inspection record exists — a CAL FIRE incident with at least 100 inspections, matched to the Carlson fire by distance rather than by name.
  3. A public perimeter exists to define the frame.
  4. An aerial flight exists before the fire, under the state's own imagery calendar.

⚠️ Six of the sixteen rebuilt fires are fires the detector was trained on — Creek 2020, Zogg 2020, Fawn 2021, McKinney 2022, Oak 2022 and Mill 2022 — and they score higher: mean recall 0.792 against 0.751 on the ten it never saw, and the detector's own contribution is +1.4 points there against +5.5 here. Read the six as an optimistic bound and the ten as the out-of-sample number. Every row of the per-fire file carries the flag.

02 · the imagery floor

How far back the method can actually reach

The obvious floor is the satellite: there is no before-and-after optical pair at 10 m before 2016, so nothing earlier can be done at all. The practical floor turns out to be something else — the calendar on which a footprint database was extracted.

A footprint database built today lists buildings that did not exist when an old fire burned, so every retrospective inventory keeps only footprints whose own image date precedes the ignition. What that costs, per fire:

The shape matters more than the average. From 2019 onward the rule costs nothing at all, on twelve fires out of twelve. Before 2019 it is a county property rather than a year one: Kern County's whole extract was derived from scenes taken five and six weeks after the Erskine fire, so Erskine has no pre-fire footprint at all and the pipeline refused to build it. Lake County, two months later in the same year, keeps 89.6 %. Seventeen of the eighteen admitted fires have an inventory; Erskine is the one that does not, and that is a finding rather than a shortfall.

03 · the whole pipeline, end to end

Three-quarters of the count, and the missing quarter is detection

The number a user of the joined dataset would actually feel: find the buildings, call the losses, and compare the total to what the hand survey reported for the same ground.

The unit is a matched pair — an inventory point this pipeline found, scored by a model fit that never saw that fire, tied one-to-one within 10 m to a Carlson building, inside the region both datasets looked at.

So we actually recover 5,767 of Carlson's 9,783 destroyed buildings — 59 % — and add 1,711 calls on structures Carlson did not record as destroyed, 877 of them on buildings it never enumerated at all: outbuildings below its definition, or buildings it missed. The two errors partly cancel and the aggregate count looks better than either half. This is the single most important caveat this experiment produces, and it is invisible in any total.

The shortfall is detection, not damage. On the buildings we do find the calls are good — pooled destroyed precision and recall agree with each other to three thousandths, so the model is neither systematically over- nor under-calling on ground it can see. The end-to-end ratio tracks detection recall at Spearman +0.550, and the worst ratios are the worst-detected fires: Ranch 2018 (recall 0.620 → ratio 0.476), Caldor 2021 (0.693 → 0.536), Nuns 2017 (0.668 → 0.595).

Zogg 2020 is the exception in both directions. It is the only fire that over-calls — ratio 1.796, 176 against 98 — and it carries the worst matched-pair numbers in the set by a wide margin (κ 0.494, F1 0.633, AUC 0.842). It is also the worst fire on the damage half below. Two independent protocols agree that this is the model's weak point in the Carlson era.

⚠️ The two truth sources disagree with each other by up to 1.8×, and that is a definition problem, not an error. The inspectors record more destroyed structures than the survey on 12 of 16 fires — up to 1.84× on Zogg — and fewer on four, down to 0.82× on Ranch. Inspectors count mobile homes, second dwellings and outbuildings the survey never counted, and several inspection records can sit on one building. A modern count validated against inspections is not in the survey's units, whatever the model does.

06 · what this does not settle

Four limits worth carrying away

  1. These sixteen fires are not rows in the published dataset, and adding them needs a rule that is documented but not yet enforced: never sum across the two halves for the same fire. A fire like Glass 2020 would otherwise carry the hand enumeration and our inventory of the same buildings.
  2. Sixteen fires in one state is a small sample for a rank correlation, 2019 is a single fire, and every ratio is computed at one fixed threshold.
  3. Six of the sixteen are detector training fires and score higher. The out-of-sample recall is the ten-fire figure, 0.751.
  4. Two fires are missing. Dixie 2021 has no inventory — its frame is 3,959 km² and needed a quarter of this experiment's entire imagery budget for one fire — and Erskine 2016 has none for the footprint-vintage reason above.
