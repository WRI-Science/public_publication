What does this pipeline add to the building footprints already out there?

Structure Loss Pipeline · QA · pre-fire structure inventory and per-structure damage, scored against CAL FIRE DINS inspections on 20 California fires

What the detector adds over the footprints

Across 15 fires and 38,497 CAL FIRE inspections, the footprint database alone — USA Structures ∪ Microsoft, 171,079 points — already matches 0.744 of the records at 10 m. Adding our detector takes that to 0.753. That is 343 structures: 0.9 points of recall, and 3.5 % of the 9,845 records the database missed. The footprints are the product; we are slightly better than them. What we add is concentrated on the rural fires, exactly where the database is worst. Park recovers 94 records — 7.9 % of its misses — off a baseline recall of 0.432. Where the database is already good, we add almost nothing: Coches 2, baseline 0.825, gains 0. Precision is not measurable from DINS: of 2,267 detector-only points, 347 are confirmed against an inspection, 409 sit inside an inspected area and match nothing, and 1,511 are where nobody inspected — so at most 54 % of the additions inside an inspected area are wrong, and DINS cannot say more than that.

The database does not miss what it misses because the buildings are new. The merge rate is not affected that way — fusing a house and its garage into one blob is a real failure mode of a 0.6 m probability raster, and it is worst where buildings are packed.

Damage classification — is this structure destroyed?

Per-structure damage classification is the pipeline's second product: given a structure and before/after Sentinel-2, was it destroyed? The honest score is leave-one-incident-out across 8 CAL FIRE DINS incidents (36,272 structures). Training on the fires the product is actually for takes pooled F1 from 0.845 to 0.898 and pooled AUC from 0.953 to 0.969, and no incident gets worse.

What looked like an operating-point problem was mostly a training-distribution problem: under v2 the per-incident best cuts sit between 0.64 and 0.93 instead of tracking each fire's base rate across a 0.65-wide range.

v1 trains on USGS Carlson fires only (all ≤ 2022) and is applied to these incidents unchanged. v2 trains on Carlson plus the incidents and is scored leave-one-incident-out — fit on Carlson and the other seven, predict the held-out one — so the fire being scored is never in its own training set. Same learner, same 62 features, same seed; the only change is which fires are in the pool. Bold = v2 beats v1. The single fixed cut wins. “fixed cut 0.9 (inherited)” takes the best mean per-fire F1 at 0.864. Every adaptive rule buys at most 0.7 points of pooled F1 and gives up 0.5–2.1 points of mean per-fire F1 to do it. Pooling is not neutral here: the two Los Angeles fires are most of the structures, so a pooled-F1 cut is whatever those two want and the small low-base-rate fires pay for it. A new fire is one case, not eighteen thousand cases, so the bundle ships the single cut — chosen on mean per-fire F1 — with a fitted base-rate rule alongside it for a caller who wants per-fire cuts.

What is being measured

The detector is trained on five Carlson fires and a set of WUI counties. The fires below are none of them. For each one the pipeline builds its pre-fire structure inventory from public data only — footprints, optionally a U-Net on 0.6 m NAIP — and then asks a single question: of the structures CAL FIRE inspectors recorded after the fire, how many does that inventory contain?

Matching is greedy one-to-one at 20 m: closest pairs first, every point used once. One-to-one matters — nearest-neighbour matching lets a single inventory point claim a whole cluster of inspections and turns a footprint that covers four units into four hits. 20 m rather than 5 m because the point-dataset audit shows the two reference datasets do not agree with each other at 5 m; a DINS point is where an inspector stood, not the centre of the roof.

How to read the columns

  • Major vs minor. DINS inspects sheds, detached garages and barns; the USGS Carlson record never contained them and public footprint datasets rarely do. The two are always reported separately, because pooling them silently lowers recall and hides which one moved.
  • Precision only inside the inspected area. An inventory point 2 km from any inspection is not a false positive — nobody looked there. Precision is computed on inventory points within 60 m of a DINS record; everything outside is excluded rather than counted as wrong.
  • Training overlap is an honesty flag. It is the share of the fire's DINS records that fall inside a training chip. Where it is above zero, that fire is not fully out-of-sample and its recall is optimistic. It is measured, not assumed.
  • Recall is not the ceiling. Several inspected structures regularly share one building footprint, so a footprint-derived inventory cannot reach 1.0 under one-to-one matching. The long table carries any_within_radius — the share of DINS records with some inventory point within the radius — as that ceiling.

What the misses actually look like

A recall number says how many structures a recipe missed. Only the imagery says what it missed. Each grid below is 12 NAIP windows of 120 m, rendered by scripts/qa_audit_crops.py from the same seed for every recipe, with the detector's own probability raster tinted red at p ≥ 0.5 over the pre-fire imagery. Cyan outlines are pre-fire USA Structures footprints (first county of the fire); yellow circles are the inventory points the recipe produced. Three grids per recipe, asking three different questions:

  • misses — centred on DINS records that recipe did not match. Recall, in pictures: an empty tile means the inspector recorded a structure no imagery shows; a tile with a house and no yellow circle is a real detection failure.
  • called — centred on inventory points inside the inspected area. Precision, in pictures: is there a building under the point the recipe produced?
  • random — windows drawn anywhere inside the fire frame. On these fires that is mostly forest, which is the point: red tint out here would be the detector calling trees a building.
