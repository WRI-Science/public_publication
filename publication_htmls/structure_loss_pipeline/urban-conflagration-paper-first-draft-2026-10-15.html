Abstract

An urban conflagration is a fire that spreads through a built-up area from building to building faster than firefighters can stop it. Such fires caused the worst U.S. wildfire disasters of the last two decades, yet no numeric definition exists. We use three full per-building records: the USGS Carlson record (2002–22, 361 fires with buildings in reach of the fire), our 2002–26 record (726 fires) and the CAL FIRE damage inspections (2013–25, 445 incidents). SIG We define an urban conflagration as at least 50 destroyed buildings joined in one chain, each within 30 m (centre to centre) of another. This is a stated convention: patch sizes show no natural break. DEF Two conditions were met by every event. First, the fire reached a block of more than 89 closely spaced buildings. No fire with a smaller block had an urban conflagration: 0 of 338 (Carlson), 0 of 701 (our record) and 0 of 429 (CAL FIRE). OUT Second, the town burned during a recognised regional wind event: 10 of the 10 urban conflagrations in our record did. WC Neither condition is enough. Of the 25 fires that reached a block above the line, 10 became urban conflagrations. Of the 15 that did not, 14 also burned in a wind event. SIG·WC Tree canopy between houses, wind at the town or across the region, downwind lean, network position and clumping beyond chance do not explain which ones did; fences could not be tested for lack of data. FW·TW·WC·DWL·NET·FC Nor did any of eight pre-registered measures of how the fire reached the town; a less downhill final approach is a hint only. FMT Outside the U.S., in an outcome-blind frame of 417 fires in 10 countries scored with our damage model, none of the 178 fires with a block of 50–89 buildings had an urban conflagration. The share above the line abroad (5 of 239) cannot yet be compared with the U.S. share, because the model found only 43 % of the buildings graded destroyed there. INT Across the lower 48 states, 75,788 blocks above the line lie within 2.4 km of wildland. They hold 15.5 million buildings, 9.7 % of all mapped buildings. Every urban conflagration in the three records reached such a block, and no fire that reached none became one. MP2·OUT A final-map spread model puts the share of destroyed buildings lit by a burning neighbour at most 28–39 % across the three records. H2H·OUT That share is larger in bigger blocks, and clearest in Marshall, Camp and Almeda Drive. H2H Whether the ~89-building line is a true tipping point cannot be told. Percolation theory predicts a rounded climb at town sizes, and a test would detect sharpening at most 12 % of the time. STP·FSS·LRN·OUT The wildfire does most of the work. Big blocks let house-to-house spread add to it.

Introduction

A few fires cause most wildfire building loss in the United States. Fires driven by downslope winds were 13.4 % of western U.S. fires but caused 60.1 % of the structures lost since 1999 (Abatzoglou et al. 2023). LRC Expert accounts describe these disasters as the same sequence: wind-driven embers ignite many houses at once, burning houses ignite their neighbours, and firefighters are overwhelmed (Cohen 2008; Calkin et al. 2014, 2023). LRC Calkin et al. (2014) found that sequence in each of the 14 wildfires they examined that destroyed more than 100 homes. LRC

No numeric definition exists. The National Wildfire Coordinating Group glossary defines a conflagration as "a raging, destructive fire", often with a moving front, and has no entry for an urban conflagration (NWCG glossary). LRC Countryman (1964) wrote that where a fire becomes a "mass" fire "has never been specifically defined". The only numeric rule that applies to part of a city is a firestorm rule about simultaneous burning, not spread (Glasstone & Dolan 1977). LRC Fire-service and insurance writers define an urban conflagration by spread past barriers (NFSA 2022; Dunn 2008). Dunn (2008) separates a block-sized "group fire" from a conflagration that crosses streets. LRC Wildland-fire researchers attach numbers only to whole fires: "more than 100 homes" (Calkin et al. 2014), "hundreds to thousands" (Cohen 2017). LRC Engineering work gives distances between pairs of buildings, from 1.8 m to about 18 m wall to wall, not sizes (McNamara & Mell 2024; Knapp et al. 2021; NIST reports reviewed in LRC). LRC

The newer view treats an urban conflagration as part of a fire. Investigators now describe a wildland fire that "becomes" an urban fire in some neighbourhoods. Lahaina was "rapidly transforming from a grassland fire into an urban conflagration" (FSRI 2024). In Palisades and Eaton, fire behaviour "transcended initial WUI character" (FSRI 2025). In Marshall, "a fast-moving low-intensity grass fire became a highly destructive urban fire" (FEMA 2025). LRC The National Fire Protection Association's report on the 1991 Oakland Hills fire already said that one subdivision's burning was "more characteristic of an urban conflagration than a typical wildland/urban interface fire" (NFPA 1992). LRC Calkin et al. (2023) call these "urban fires initiated by wildfires" and name Camp, Marshall, Lahaina, Almeda Drive and Gatlinburg as fires that did their damage as urban conflagrations. LRC Zamanialaei et al. (2025) and Norlen et al. (2026) use the same framing. LRC If an urban conflagration is a part of a fire, a fire can hold none, one or several. No study has counted such parts across many fires. LRC

What is known, and what is disputed. Inside a burning town, how close buildings stand is the strongest measured predictor of loss (Knapp et al. 2021; McNamara & Mell 2024; Zamanialaei et al. 2025; Monroy et al. 2025 for IBHS). LRC Across whole landscapes, loss peaks at low to intermediate housing density (Syphard et al. 2012). Inside burning towns, denser means more loss (Alexandre et al. 2016). LRC Tree canopy near houses predicts loss in the Camp Fire (Knapp et al. 2021) but not in Woolsey (Mockrin et al. 2023), and only weakly or inconsistently in Eaton and Palisades (Kenny et al. 2026; Norlen et al. 2026). LRC How much loss spreads house to house has been estimated for very few places. One is Waldo Canyon's Mountain Shadows, where 296 of 344 destroyed homes were ignited by other structures (NIST TN 1910). The other is the Witch Fire's Trails community, where house-to-house spread was "not a significant contributor". LRC Percolation theory says fire spread through scattered fuel has a critical density (Beer 1990; Spyratos et al. 2007). No paper has applied it to observed building loss. LRC Graph centrality has been scored against real losses in two fires only, where it performed at about coin-flip level (Chulahwat et al. 2022). LRN

This paper. We ask four questions across three full national records. (1) What did every urban conflagration have in common? (2) Is that enough to predict one? (3) If not, what does not explain the rest? (4) How much of the loss was house to house? We also ask whether the line we find is a tipping point. Several explanations proposed in the literature turn out not to hold in our data: trees between houses, wind strength, downwind lean and network position. We report these nulls as results.

Defining an urban conflagration

The rule. An urban conflagration is a chain of at least 50 destroyed buildings, each standing within 30 m (centre to centre) of another destroyed building in the chain. We call such a chain a patch. A fire has an urban conflagration if any of its patches reaches 50. DEF·PAT

Why 30 m. No single natural linking distance exists, but there is a range. Destroyed buildings cluster more than chance at every distance above 5 m. Half the excess is reached by 20 m and 90 % by 50 m, in all three records. 30 m lies inside that range. It also lies inside the published house-to-house separations, most often 9.1–18 m wall to wall. On equal-area circles, 30 m between centres selects the same fires as about 15 m between walls. DEF

Why 50 is a convention. Patch sizes thin out smoothly. No test found a second peak at any linking distance from 5 to 100 m in any record (Silverman test, Holm-adjusted p = 1.0 everywhere). DEF Sizes are heavy-tailed but do not follow a power law. NET The rule matches published descriptions where they are clear. It catches 4 of 4 fires that reports call urban or house-to-house fires in CAL FIRE, 6 of 10 in our record and 4 of 8 in Carlson, and counts none of 5 spread-out losses. It misses Angora, Grass Valley, Witch 2007 and Chimney Tops 2 (Gatlinburg). DEF It is not inside a stable plateau of nearby rules. Its stability against neighbouring rules is 0.535 (Carlson), 0.604 (ours) and 0.715 (CAL FIRE). The pre-registered verdict was "can't tell (knife-edge)". DEF We keep it as a stated convention and give 50 m links as the sensitivity check.

A fire holds parts. Six of the 10 fires in our record with an urban conflagration hold two or more patches of 50; they hold 50 such patches in all. PAT At 30 m a burned town splits into street-sized pieces:

  • Tubbs: 7 patches of 50 or more; the largest holds 143 of 5,185 destroyed buildings (2.8 %);
  • Camp: 12 patches; the largest holds 94 of 16,493 (0.6 %);
  • Marshall: one patch of 57 of 1,196 (4.8 %).

PAT Only 11 % of the destroyed buildings in those 10 fires (4,470 of 41,342) sit in any patch of 50. PAT So the rule marks the part of a fire where loss linked up through the town, not the whole loss.

The compound alternative. A compound urban conflagration is a group, linked at 75 m, that holds at least two patches of at least 20 destroyed buildings (patches at 30 m). It picks 8 fires in Carlson, 10 in ours and 5 in CAL FIRE. It catches the same named urban fires as the 30 m rule and is steadier: stability 0.89, 0.91 and 0.94. In our record it drops Woolsey and Sandalwood (one big patch each, not several) and adds Erskine and Dixie (several patches, none of 50). NET Compound groups hold 79 % of Camp's destroyed buildings (13,012 of 16,493), 42 % of Tubbs's (2,165 of 5,185) and 51 % of Marshall's (612 of 1,196). NET We report the 30 m rule as primary and the compound rule beside it.

A second kind, through trees. A house-and-tree patch links destroyed buildings up to 60 m apart when pre-fire canopy is at least 25 % at every 10 m along the line. It catches Angora and Chimney Tops 2, which the house rule misses, but misses Palisades. It is well defined by the pre-set bar. Combining it with the house rule ("either link") could not be told apart from the house rule alone. PAT One fire dominates the tree kind: 31 of Carlson's 43 house-and-tree patches are in Camp (Paradise). PAT

The block. The predictor used throughout is the block: the largest connected group of buildings in reach of the fire whose centres stand within 35 m of each other. On a 159 m² footprint that is about a 20 m gap between walls. QNT It is counted from the buildings that stood before the fire, whatever happened to them.

Is 89 a tipping point? We cannot tell, and theory says why

The climb. The chance of an urban conflagration is about 5 % at a block of about 70 buildings. It reaches about 50 % at about 188 buildings in our record (Carlson 70 → 209; CAL FIRE 67 → 209). OUT That is a 2.7-fold range of block size (1.7–3.9). STP·FOR Fig. 5 shows the climb.

The step test could not have found a step. The test behind the earlier wording "smooth, no step" would have missed a real step at 89 about half the time: power 0.57 on fires with at least 100 buildings in reach and 0.44 in Carlson. On the full records the step search stops at about 60 buildings, so power is 0.06 and 0.01. OUT STP Predicting each fire with it left out, a gradual rise beats a hard step: log score better by +4.6 (Carlson), +5.1 (ours) and +3.2 (CAL FIRE), against a bar of 2. OUT STP·FOR This reading is fragile in Carlson. It falls to −0.5 with U.S. buildings only and to −0.05 when unburned patches are removed from the outline, because two fires move from just above 89 to just below. USC·MTB

What finite-size theory predicts. In percolation, a large system switches sharply from "connected" to "not connected". A small one switches over a window. The window narrows only as side length to the power −3/4 in two dimensions (Newman & Ziff 2001; Mertens & Moore 2012). In buildings, that is block size to the power −3/8 (the review's own derivation). LRN Across our 2.7-fold range, theory predicts only about a 1.45-fold narrowing. LRN Mixing fires with different winds widens the climb further (Sander et al. 2003; Cardoso et al. 2022), and disorder rounds abrupt transitions in two dimensions (Aizenman & Wehr 1989). LRN

A simulation on the real layouts. If fire spread like a simple building-to-building process, bigger blocks would switch more sharply. The width of the climb shrinks as block size to the power −0.64 (−0.81 to −0.50) in our record. OUT That is steeper than the textbook −3/8. FSS But a test on our fires would detect it at best 10 % of the time in our record (12 % Carlson, 9 % CAL FIRE), against an 80 % bar. Even with each fire's true spread strength, which can never be measured, detection would take about 8 times as many fires. FSS The test on real fires was therefore not run, as pre-registered. FSS

What this means. A smooth climb is what we should expect at town sizes whether or not a real threshold lies underneath. "Not detectable" describes our data. It is not evidence against a threshold. FSS The line at 89 is a practical line on a steep climb, not a proven tipping point. Linking distance shows the same picture. The distance at which burned groups join up is a smooth climb with a median of about 125 m, set mostly by the town's own layout. NET

Discussion

A loaded gun and a trigger, and a third thing we cannot see. Every urban conflagration in three national records had a big block of close buildings, the loaded gun. It also burned in a regional wind event, the trigger. Those two conditions are what the newer literature describes: urban fires started by wildfires in close-built towns under extreme wind (Calkin et al. 2023; FSRI 2024, 2025). LRC But the trigger was pulled in 14 of the 15 near misses in our record, and they did not go off. WC Something decides which loaded, triggered block becomes an urban conflagration. It is not tree canopy between houses, fences as far as public data show, wind strength at the town or across the region, downwind lean, or position in the building network. Clumping beyond what a burned place produces is small. FW·TW·WC·DWL·NET·FC None of eight measures of how the fire met the town separates the two groups either: wind direction into the block, the fire's run, the fuels it crossed, night arrival, other large fires at once, fuel dryness, slope and front width. FMT Too many fires at once, the usual account of overwhelmed crews, finds no support: a later, better-timed count points the other way (post hoc). FMT Two patterns in how the fire arrived are worth testing on new fires. One is a less downhill final approach, a pre-registered hint that did not meet the bar. The other is an approach across built-up land rather than straight out of wildland, which is post hoc. Both point the same way abroad, but neither is shown there. FMT·INT Candidates that public data cannot yet measure include how many ignitions arrived at once, how many crews and how much water were available at each block, and how the buildings were built. The literature names suppression saturation as the step that turns ignitions into a disaster. It has not measured it across fires (Cohen 2008; Calkin et al. 2014; Cohen 2017). LRC

A mechanism: the wildfire does most of the work, and big blocks let house-to-house spread add to it. Three results fit together:

  1. At most a quarter to two-fifths of destroyed buildings were lit by a neighbour (28–39 %, upper bounds). H2H·OUT
  2. Even in fires with an urban conflagration, only 11 % of destroyed buildings sit in patches of 50. PAT
  3. Losses are no more clumped than a burned place of the same size. FC

So the wildfire, mostly through embers and the front, lit most of the buildings. Where the block was big, the house-to-house share was larger, about twice the share below the line, though part of that gap is a patchy front. H2H The clearest house-to-house fires were mobile-home parks and dense subdivisions: Almeda Drive, Marshall and Paradise in the Camp Fire. H2H·PAT This reconciles two views in the literature. Ember exposure decides where fire enters (Cohen 2000; Caggiano et al. 2020). Close spacing decides how far it runs once inside (Knapp et al. 2021; Alexandre et al. 2016). LRC It is also consistent with Waldo Canyon's 86 % house-to-house share (NIST TN 1910) being a dense-subdivision case rather than a typical fire, though our model cannot confirm that share (its trap gives the same 72 % there). H2H·LRC

Implications for mitigation. These are observational results, not tests of interventions.

  • Where to look. Because no measured factor picks which loaded block fires, every big block of close buildings within reach of wildland is a candidate. The national map marks 75,788 such blocks, holding 15.5 million buildings (9.7 % of mapped buildings in the lower 48 states and DC). MP2 The map says where an urban conflagration is possible, not where one is likely. About 1 in 5 fires that reached a marked block had one, and 29.9 % of marked blocks are marked only because of buildings under 40 m², so they are likely false alarms. OUT·MP2 Wind is shown beside the map, not merged into it, because nearly every fire that reached a big block burned in a wind event. WC·MP2
  • Spacing and block size. The data are consistent with FEMA's Marshall recommendation of about 30 ft (9.1 m) between structures (FEMA 2025). They are also consistent with blocks smaller than about 70 buildings being far less likely to have an urban conflagration. LRC·STP We did not test breaking up a block, and a 20 m centre-to-centre gap is not a building code.
  • Building-scale measures matter more in big blocks, where the house-to-house share is larger. Ember-resistant construction and clearing the first few metres (Syphard & Keeley 2019; Monroy et al. 2025) act on both ignition paths. LRC These variables are not in our records.
  • Network targeting is not supported by real losses. Protecting chokepoint buildings has been tested only in simulation (LRN §3). In our data, bridges between groups showed no sign of mattering. NET·LRN
  • Trees between houses are not the deciding factor among big blocks. They do link losses in sparse, treed towns such as Gatlinburg. FW

What other countries add. Condition 1 held in an outcome-blind frame of 417 fires in 10 other countries: none of 178 fires with a block of 50–89 buildings had an urban conflagration. The one exception is in a check (Antalya 2021, on Overture buildings only). INT All five urban conflagrations found abroad burned in a regional wind event on the ERA5 rule. INT The remainder cannot be compared yet. The share above the line abroad (5 of 239) is about what our damage model would see even at the U.S. rate, because it finds only 43 % of destroyed buildings there and missing buildings break chains apart. INT·file Settling it needs building-level damage truth abroad, such as inspections or more Copernicus gradings, or a damage model with higher recall outside the U.S.

Limits.

  • About ten events. There are 8, 10 and 7 urban conflagrations in the three records. The same California fires appear in all three, and 8 of 10 in our record are in California. QNT·SIG Intervals on rates above the line are wide (40 %, 21–61 %, in our record). SIG Every "not shown" compares about 10 fires with about 15. Lahaina and Waldo Canyon are out-of-sample checks, not enough to estimate a rate. SIG
  • Outline error. "Reached by the fire" depends on an outline drawn after the fire, so the block is partly an outcome. A fire that ran into town had its outline drawn around the town. QNT Counting every building lowers the AUC (0.994 → 0.962 in our record). PA2 Harris 2007 shows an outline can cross unburned ground. FOR The ring fixed before the fire agrees at a lower AUC (0.90). QNT
  • Inventory error. Carlson points carry no footprints, so spacing is centre to centre and large houses on large lots are under-linked. QNT CAL FIRE footprints are mostly from imagery after the fire. SIG Equal-area circles understate how connected real outlines are: at Lahaina, a 10 m gap links 43 buildings with circles and 111 with true outlines. DEF
  • Coarse wind. Wind cells are 2.5–31 km and ERA5 reads peaks 2.4–2.9 m/s low. Each fire gets one direction, with no wind inside the town. Valley airports and grids under-read some downslope winds, for example Camp's town gust of 15.8 m/s against 23.2 m/s at the nearest fire-weather station. TW·WC·DWL
  • Many tests. We report every pre-registered test, with Holm adjustment where pre-set. Results marked exploratory were not pre-registered.
  • The definition is a convention on a knife-edge. The compound and house-and-tree alternatives agree on the named urban fires but differ at the margin. DEF·NET·PAT
  • How the fire met the town is timed coarsely. Arrival comes from satellite passes hours apart, so night or day could be called for only 10 of the 25 fires in our record. Nine of the 69 blocks studied across the three records could not be timed at all. FMT
  • Abroad, the outcome is our model's call. It finds 43 % of the buildings graded destroyed there, so only condition 1 and the wind description carry over; the share above the line does not. Building maps abroad are mostly from after the fires. INT
  • The national map marks possibility, not risk. About 3 in 10 marked blocks rest on buildings under 40 m² and are likely false alarms. The wildland rule counts urban forest preserves. Building maps mix image dates, some after fires. The pre-registered check on past fires was not met (Marshall, Glass); the check that passes was added after the first checks were seen. MAP·MP2

Methods appendix

Building rows and reach. Buildings standing before the fire come from Carlson's points (2002–22) and our Structure Inventory Model (2023–26), with CAL FIRE inspection outcomes where inspected. A building is in reach when it lies inside the fire's MTBS burned-area outline or within 100 m of it; fires without an MTBS outline use the public outline already in use plus 100 m (Carlson fires without one: Carlson's outline). For CAL FIRE, the buildings in reach are Microsoft footprints within that area plus 100 m around every inspected building, with every inspection record kept. QNT·SIG·OUT Scripts: scripts/paper/p24a_conflag_fire_rows.py, scripts/paper/p27a_rows.py (switch --outline, paper default mtbs100 in run_conflag_chain.sh; checks mtbs100_unburned and current), outline rules in scripts/paper/p67_outline.py. Building points are projected to EPSG:5070. Every bearing is converted to true north. DWL·TW

Patch (outcome). Single-linkage groups of destroyed buildings at 30 m between centres. The size of the largest is the fire's longest destroyed chain. An urban conflagration is a longest chain of at least 50. Checks: 50 m links; ≥ 100. QNT·DEF Compound rule: a 75 m group holding ≥ 2 patches of ≥ 20 at 30 m. House-and-tree rule: links ≤ 60 m with canopy ≥ 25 % at every 10 m (USFS TCC, ignition year − 2). NET·PAT

Block (predictor). The largest single-linkage group of buildings in reach at 35 m between centres (about a 20 m gap between walls on a 159 m² footprint). QNT

Rule tests. The line is 89.09, fixed from PA31 (Firth logit; the cut that maximises sensitivity plus specificity, on fires with ≥ 100 in reach, our record). Rates below and above use exact (Clopper–Pearson) 95 % intervals. AUC intervals and cut intervals come from a fire bootstrap. Leave-one-fire-out AUC uses a Firth logit. QNT·SIG Pre-registrations: PA31 commit 5850b8d; PA33 fce86f7; PA34 cd8073d + 9958f05; back-cast C1/C2 in docs/ledger.d/2026-10-12-2240-conflagration-rule-backcast--prereg.md. QNT·SIG·DEF

Step and climb. Sup-likelihood-ratio step and hinge tests use a parametric bootstrap. Step power is from simulation (T1). The leave-one-fire-out log score compares a smooth logit with a pure step. Transition points are where the fitted chance reaches 5 % and 50 %. Prereg commit f6fc5a2. STP

Finite-size simulation. Bond-percolation spread on each fire's real layout uses a distance kernel, with log spread strength drawn from Normal(μ, σ). μ is calibrated so the simulated share of fires with ≥ 50 joined destroyed buildings equals each record's (8/361, 10/726, 7/445). A Firth model with a size-dependent width gives the power of the sharpening test along the true strength and along stand-ins with correlation 0.5 and 0.9. Prereg 6df0782 plus amendment 8922e52. FSS

Wind. Each town point is the destroyed building with the most destroyed buildings within 1 km. The burn window starts at the first FIRMS detection within 1 km of the loss area and ends when 90 % of loss-area buildings have a detection nearby (capped at 72 h). Wind is read over the window ± 12 h. TW The town measure axis_wmax uses CONUS404 to September 2024 and RTMA after. Gust axis_gmax uses RTMA from 2014 and ERA5 before. Regional measures use ERA5 only. The recognised-wind-event rule is given in Data. Group comparisons use Mann–Whitney and Fisher tests, with Holm adjustment across the 8 primary regional measures. TW·WC

Trees and fences. Canopy is measured in the 30 m square around each gap between neighbouring buildings of the biggest block. The comparison uses Mann–Whitney tests with Holm adjustment, and the fuel-linked block is scored by leave-one-fire-out AUC. Fence lengths come from OSM barrier=fence in the box around each above-line block. Prereg 46db1c3. FW

Downwind lean. For each destroyed building, neighbours ≤ 60 m are sorted by bearing relative to the town's wind while it burned (vector mean over the burn window ± 3 h). Lean = share lost downwind − share lost upwind, averaged over fires. Intervals come from a 95 % fire bootstrap. The null rotates each fire's wind direction 999 times. Prereg 4301b39 plus amendment c8508f2. DWL

Network tests. Buildings are nodes, linked within distance d. Per-fire AUC comes from 10 fire-grouped folds with a fire bootstrap of 2,000 draws and Holm adjustment over 9 tests. The bridge test uses neighbouring 30 m groups. The tipping distance is where the second-biggest group peaks over 10–150 m. Prereg fd02ae4 plus amendments. NET

Fair chance. Five baselines are compared on fires with ≥ 100 destroyed: (B1) random picks from the whole town; (B2) random picks inside the fire area; (B3) a solid round burned area of the same size; (B4) the whole fire-area town; and (B5) random picks following the fire's broad loss map (σ 200 m). The comparisons are the joining distance and the biggest-group share at 30, 50 and 75 m. Prereg 78ed1bc. FC

House-to-house share. The final-outcome model has a smooth wildfire background, built from the loss map with a 100 m hole and σ 200 m, plus a distance kernel exp(−d/λ). It is fitted by pseudo-likelihood summaries and approximate Bayesian computation on 400 simulated fires per fire, on the real layout. Checks: a recovery test on 40 held-out simulations per fire; a patchy-front trap (no house-to-house push, background varying over 50 m); and background σ of 100 and 400 m. The pooled share is destroyed-weighted, with 95 % intervals from 2,000 fire-bootstrap × posterior draws. Prereg 58abce5. H2H

How the fire met the town. Units are each fire's biggest block of at least 50 buildings (35 m links) in each record: 39 (Carlson), 42 (ours) and 27 (CAL FIRE) fires. Arrival is the first same-fire FIRMS MODIS or VIIRS detection within 1 km of the block, found by linking detections within 3 km and 24 h from the point of origin; the approach direction comes from the same fire's detections in the 24 h before arrival, or from the point of origin. The approach agrees with an MTBS-outline check in 39 of 41 comparable units. Measures: M1 cosine between the wind's heading and the approach bearing at the hour of peak wind (CONUS404 to September 2024, RTMA after); M2 straight-line km from the point of origin; M3 share of burnable vegetation that is grass, pasture or shrub in a ±45°, 0–2 km wedge (Annual NLCD, year before the fire); M4 sun below the horizon for the whole arrival window; M5 large fires listed for the Geographic Area that day as a percentile (IMSR; ICS-209-PLUS before 2007); M6 gridMET energy release component percentile against the same cell's 1991–2020 days within ±15 days; M7 rise per metre over the last 1 km (3DEP); M8 MODIS front width across the approach. Group comparisons use Mann–Whitney (Fisher for night), Cliff's delta with a 2,000-draw bootstrap, and Holm adjustment over M1–M8 within each record. The bar and the minimum detectable effect are in the pre-registration. Descriptive only: nothing here is a definition or model input. Prereg 606eef8. FMT

Outside the U.S. Frame outlines: NBAC (Canada), EFFIS (Greece, Portugal, Spain, Turkey, Israel), NIAFED (Australia 2019–20), MODIS MCD64A1 (Chile, South Korea, Japan), 2016–2025. Outlines with at least 2,500 m² of built-up surface (GHSL) within the outline + 100 m were kept. Blocks were built at 35 m from Overture and Microsoft footprints of at least 50 m², and buildings within 100 m of the outline count as reached. Destroyed is the first-call damage model's call at a raw probability of at least 0.88; the chain uses the paper's 30 m rule. The damage model was checked one-to-one at 10 m against Copernicus EMS gradings (EMSR746, EMSR900, EMSR908) for buildings of at least 50 m². Wind: ERA5 two-day peak wind or gust in the top 10 % of the place's 1991–2020 record. The approach test uses ESA WorldCover and the FIRMS same-fire chain. Prereg 4c6b6f5 plus amendments 1–6, all committed before any outcome was read. INT

National map. Buildings: Microsoft U.S. footprints v2 (at least 40 m²) plus Overture 2026-09-23.1 buildings not matched to a Microsoft footprint (by a footprint holding a Microsoft centre or by equal-area circles). Blocks: single-linkage at 35 m between centres over the lower 48 states and DC, labelled nationally. Wildland: NLCD 2019 forest, shrub, grassland and wetland classes where at least 50 % of the 270 m window is such classes. Distance is exact to 5 km. Marked: more than 89 buildings and at least one within 2.4 km of wildland. Sensitivities: 0.1–5 km, SILVIS 2020 interface or intermix, and Wildfire Risk to Communities burn probability above 0. The wind layer counts NWS High Wind Warning days a year, 2006–2025 (IEM archive), placed with zone outlines as issued. The check on past fires asks whether each fire's buildings in reach touched a marked block (records on MTBS + 100 m, Carlson's outline and the unburned-patch check). Only aggregates are published. Prereg 8234a83, amendments dc838f4 and 9d73be4. MAP·MP2·OUT
