Wildfire structure loss

How concentrated are wildfire building losses? · 2026-10-15

Five measures of how unevenly destroyed buildings are spread across U.S. wildfires: the share held by the top 1%, 5% and 10% of fires, the concentration curve, the Gini coefficient, whether the share of fires destroying any building is changing at the same exposure, and whether rising losses come from the largest fires or from more fires. Carlson 2002–22 (358 fires with any loss, 73,534 destroyed), our 2002–26 record (658, 91,861) and CAL FIRE DINS 2013–25 (317 incidents, 70,390), beside the federal incident reports (ICS-209-PLUS 2002–20, situation reports 2007–25), which also list fires with little or no loss.

By Carlo Broderick, NCEAS, UC Santa Barbara · started 2026-10-15 16:14 PDT · updated 2026-10-15 16:14 PDT

A few fires hold most of the loss: the top 10% of fires hold 74%–92% of destroyed buildings across the three records. Losses per year rose about threefold, and the rise is in the largest fires; whether more fires destroy any building, we can't tell.

Share of all destroyed buildings held by the most destructive 1%, 5% and 10% of fires

In every record a few fires hold most of the loss. The top 1% of fires with any loss hold 34% of destroyed buildings in Carlson (about 4 fires), 43% in our record (about 7) and 51% in CAL FIRE DINS (about 3); the top 10% hold 74%, 82% and 92%. One fire, Camp (CA, 2018), is 22%, 18% and 27% of the three records on its own. The incident reports, which also list the many fires that destroyed a few buildings, are even more concentrated (60% and 65% in the top 1%).Top-q share = 1 − L(1 − q) on the concentration curve of destroyed per loss fire (D ≥ 1); fire bootstrap (2,000 draws) for the three records. Report series: full years only (situation reports 2007–25, ICS-209-PLUS 2002–20), point values. Concentration curve: running share of losses as fires are added from most to least destructive

A concentration curve lines fires up from the most to the least destructive and keeps a running total of the buildings they destroyed, as a share of all destroyed buildings. If every fire destroyed the same number, the curve would follow the dotted diagonal; the more it bows up and to the left, the more the loss sits in a few fires. In all three records the curve climbs steeply: the worst fifth of fires already holds 85% (Carlson), 91% (our record) and 97% (CAL FIRE DINS) of all destroyed buildings. Dots mark the top 1%, 5% and 10% (panel above). Gini coefficient: how unequal the number destroyed per fire is

The Gini coefficient puts the concentration curve into one number between 0 and 1: 0 if every fire destroyed the same number of buildings, 1 if one fire destroyed them all. It is 0.81 in Carlson, 0.87 in our record and 0.93 in CAL FIRE DINS: losses are very unequal in all three. In the incident reports, split into two equal 9-year periods, losses became more unequal: 0.86 → 0.94 in situation reports (2008–2016 → 2017–2025; change +0.08, range +0.02 to +0.12) and 0.83 → 0.93 in ICS-209-PLUS (change +0.10, +0.03 to +0.18).G = Σ(2i − n − 1)xᵢ / (n Σx) over loss fires sorted ascending; three records: fire bootstrap, 2,000 draws (p76_conc_three_records.csv). Report periods: equal full-year halves, year-cluster bootstrap (p23_conc_equal_periods.csv).

Is the share of fires that destroy at least one building changing, at the same exposure?

Carlson, CAL FIRE DINS and our record list only fires that damaged buildings, so they hold no fires with zero loss; this panel uses sources that also list the fires that destroyed nothing. Can't tell whether the share is changing. Among federal incident reports of fires that threatened at least one structure (ICS-209-PLUS, 2002–20, 12,280 incidents), the chance of destroying at least one building at the typical exposure (9 structures threatened) was between 17% and 29% a year; the odds changed ×1.07 per decade (range 0.99–1.16), which includes no change and also a small rise. The situation reports, which cannot be adjusted for exposure, give ×0.97 (0.80–1.22) over 2007–25. The 2023–25 sources cover only three years: too few to tell.Exposure-adjusted: logit of (destroyed ≥ 1) on year/10 + log(1 + structures threatened), ICS-209-PLUS CONUS 2002–20 with STR_THREATENED_MAX ≥ 1 (p21_conc_p_any_loss.csv; per-year points from p23_p_any_by_year.csv, year fixed effects). Situation reports: binomial logit on year/10, year-block bootstrap (p76_imsr_any_loss_trend.csv). 2023–25 frames: exposure = buildings inside the outline.

Are losses rising because the largest fires got worse, because more fires cause losses, or both?

Buildings destroyed per year rose about ×3.3 in the situation reports (2008–2016 vs 2017–2025, range 1.4–6.3) and ×2.9 in ICS-209-PLUS (2003–2011 vs 2012–2020). We can't tell whether more fires destroy buildings: fires with any loss per year changed ×1.06 (0.77–1.50) and ×0.84 (0.63–1.16), ranges that include no change. Losses in each year's largest fire rose ×3.71 (1.05–9.78) and ×4.69 (1.09–17.11). So by the rule fixed in advance the rise is in the tail in both report series: the largest fires became more destructive, though the lower ends of those ranges sit just above 1. In the three full records the largest fire of each year rose in Carlson and our record (×5.9 and ×6.3); CAL FIRE DINS can't tell (×0.5 (0.1–4.6)), because its earlier six years hold the Camp Fire (2018). A few disaster years carry much of the rise: without each period's largest year, the situation-report total is ×2.6 (1.1–5.0), and without the two largest ×2.4 (0.9–4.9).Ratio of mean annual totals, later ÷ earlier equal full-year period, by within-year rank and for the count of loss fires; 95% ranges from 10,000 year-bootstrap draws (p23_growth_equal_periods.csv for the reports; p76_growth_three_records.csv for the three records). Pre-registered rule: 'tail' when the largest-fire range lies above 1 and the loss-fire range includes 1. Negative-binomial trend in loss fires per decade (all full years): situation reports ×0.95 (0.71–1.28), ICS-209-PLUS ×1.11 (0.79–1.56) (p23_loss_fire_count_trend.csv).

How this panel was checked
  • Equal-length, full-year periods: situation reports 2008–2016 vs 2017–2025; ICS-209-PLUS 2003–2011 vs 2012–2020; Carlson 2003–2012 vs 2013–2022; our record 2002–2013 vs 2014–2025; CAL FIRE DINS 2014–2019 vs 2020–2025. The first year is dropped to make equal halves; 2026 never enters.
  • Each source is compared only with itself; ranks are within each year, so every year contributes exactly one 'largest fire'.
  • Why the three records appear only on the two largest-fire rows: their coverage of smaller fires grew over time, so their count of fires with any loss rises for reasons of record-keeping (Carlson ×2.1 (1.2–3.8), our record ×3.8 (2.1–6.9), CAL FIRE DINS ×1.9 (1.0–4.8)). The incident reports list every incident in every year, so the broad question is answered from them.
  • Single-year dominance: 2018 holds 45% of the later period's largest-fire losses in the situation reports; every ratio is repeated without each period's largest year (hover) and the total without the two largest (caption).
  • Even the largest-fire rows can be touched by coverage in Carlson and our record: some early years hold very few mapped fires (Carlson has 1 fire in 2004 and 2 in 2010), so a year's largest loss may be missing there; the incident reports carry the verdict.
  • Bars are cut at 12×; a whisker that reaches the edge runs further (see its hover).
  • Ratios of means with year-bootstrap ranges; no share-of-increase is shown (with heavy tails its range left 0–100%; superseded by these ratios on 2026-10-13).
