Wildfire structure loss: paper exploration · 2026-10-12

Everything we have tested for the paper, in one place: losses by year 2002–2026, which fires lose buildings, whether building density raises loss, and mobile homes. Urban-conflagration and mobile-home analyses are being added.

By Carlo Broderick, NCEAS, UC Santa Barbara · 2026-10-12 15:23 PDT

Three findings so far. Losses grew because the worst fires got worse, not because more fires destroy buildings. Inside a fire, building density barely changes a building's chance of loss; there is no density threshold. The high-density losses that do stand out are mobile-home parks.

Concentration curve: share of all losses held by the most destructive fires

Fires ranked from most to least destructive. In the situation reports, the top 1% of loss fires hold 60% of all destroyed buildings, the top 5% hold 83% and the top 10% 90%. Carlson looks less concentrated only because it leaves out fires with 1–9 losses.

Is there a step? The jump in the odds of loss at 60 neighbours

A step test asks whether loss jumps at some density, instead of rising smoothly. Here: how much higher the odds of loss are just above 60 neighbours than just below, in the same fire. Carlson's estimates point up (about 2–3.5×), but every range includes 1 and the jump comes from Sayre's mobile-home park; in our full 2002–26 record it is about 1.1×. Searching every possible step location from 1 to about 62 neighbours gave the same answer: no step that holds up.Fixed step at n100 = 60 added to a 4-df spline in log2(1+n100) with fire fixed effects; free-location hinge and step models (ψ grid 1–99.5th percentile, Davies test, AIC vs spline) in docs/ledger.d/2026-10-12-2134-high-density-step--exploratory.md.
