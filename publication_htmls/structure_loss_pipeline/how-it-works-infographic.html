The process, in pictures

After a wildfire, somebody has to answer a very concrete question for every building in the burn area: is it still there? Today that answer comes from inspectors walking the ground, which is slow, expensive, and only happens in some places. This project estimates the same answer from free satellite pictures and public maps. Five diagrams below show where the data comes from, what the computer actually does with it, when each answer becomes available, how we check whether the answer is any good, and what you get at the end.

Figure 1

Where the information comes from

Nothing here is bought, and nothing is private. Five public sources do five different jobs, and it matters which is which — three of them tell the computer what to look at, and two of them are the answer sheets we grade ourselves against. An answer sheet is never allowed to become an input.

Source: the project's data-provenance and licence documentation; building counts from the published historical record's own release.

Figure 2

What the computer actually does

Two steps, in order, and each can fail on its own. If step one never finds a building, step two never gets to have an opinion about it — which is why we report both numbers rather than one combined score.

Figure 3

When each answer arrives

This is the part most people are surprised by. A satellite cannot tell you anything useful the day after a fire — there is still smoke, the fire may still be burning, and the ground has not settled. So the answer is built from a window of pictures, not a single one, and the window has to close before the answer exists.

Source: the pipeline's own window settings — before window 45 days ending the day before ignition, after window days 14 to 74. Those windows were re-tested against 41 alternatives in September 2026 and kept.

Figure 4

How we check whether any of this is true

The easy mistake in this kind of work is to grade a model on the same fires it learned from. It then scores beautifully and tells you nothing about the next fire. So every accuracy number on this site is produced the hard way: the fire being scored was removed from the model that scored it.

Figure 5

What you actually get

One continuous record from 2002 to the present, with a row for every building in every qualifying fire. Its two halves were made in completely different ways, so the record says which half every row comes from rather than blending them into a single number.

The honest limitations, in one place

Everything below is measured and published elsewhere on this site. It is collected here because an infographic that only shows what works is an advertisement.

  • A building we never found gets no answer. If the last aerial photo before the fire had a tree over the roof, the building is not on the list, and nothing downstream can rescue it.
  • Grass fires are harder than forest fires. The strongest signal in the satellite data is vegetation that burned. Where there was little vegetation at the building to begin with, the signal is faint — and that is exactly the kind of fire that is growing most quickly across the United States.
  • Almost all the fresh ground truth is Californian. The inspection records that grade recent fires come from one state's fire service. Every check we can run outside California is published, and there are not many.
  • The answer sheets disagree with each other. Where two expert surveys cover the same buildings they differ on about 2 % of them — and the model is no better than a coin flip on exactly those buildings. That is a floor on how good any model can look here.
  • This is an estimate, not an inspection. It is for looking at patterns across many fires and many years. It is not a basis for a decision about any one property.
