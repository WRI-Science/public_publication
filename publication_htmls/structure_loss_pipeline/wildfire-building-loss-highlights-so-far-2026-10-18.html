Wildfire structure loss

Wildfire building loss: highlights so far · 2026-10-18

A short tour, for discussion, of the clearest results from the U.S. wildfire building-loss work: losses over time, when fires turn into town fires, density and building type, and what the extended record adds. Mostly panels from our earlier pages, each linked to its full page.

By Carlo Broderick, NCEAS, UC Santa Barbara · 2026-10-18 13:12 PDT

Not exhaustive: a selection to start a conversation. Each panel has its own Plain / Technical switch; hover or tap any bar, line or heading for the numbers behind it.

The three records used throughout

  • Carlson 2002–22: USGS hand-mapped buildings for every fire that destroyed 10 or more.
  • Our 2002–26 record: Carlson plus the 2023–26 fires our satellite pipeline mapped, using CAL FIRE's inspection outcome wherever a building was inspected.
  • CAL FIRE DINS 2013–25: CAL FIRE's door-to-door damage inspections, every incident on file.

1 · Losses are rising, and the rise is in the worst fires

Three independent records agree that more buildings are being lost. The growth comes from the biggest fires, not from more fires destroying a few houses each.

Are losses rising because the largest fires got worse, because more fires cause losses, or both?

Buildings destroyed per year rose about ×3.3 in the situation reports (2008–2016 vs 2017–2025, range 1.4–6.3) and ×2.9 in ICS-209-PLUS (2003–2011 vs 2012–2020). We can't tell whether more fires destroy buildings: fires with any loss per year changed ×1.06 (0.77–1.50) and ×0.84 (0.63–1.16), ranges that include no change. Losses in each year's largest fire rose ×3.71 (1.05–9.78) and ×4.69 (1.09–17.11). So by the rule fixed in advance the rise is in the tail in both report series: the largest fires became more destructive, though the lower ends of those ranges sit just above 1. In the three full records the largest fire of each year rose in Carlson and our record (×5.9 and ×6.3); CAL FIRE DINS can't tell (×0.5 (0.1–4.6)), because its earlier six years hold the Camp Fire (2018). A few disaster years carry much of the rise: without each period's largest year, the situation-report total is ×2.6 (1.1–5.0), and without the two largest ×2.4 (0.9–4.9).Ratio of mean annual totals, later ÷ earlier equal full-year period, by within-year rank and for the count of loss fires; 95% ranges from 10,000 year-bootstrap draws (p23_growth_equal_periods.csv for the reports; p76_growth_three_records.csv for the three records). Pre-registered rule: 'tail' when the largest-fire range lies above 1 and the loss-fire range includes 1. Negative-binomial trend in loss fires per decade (all full years): situation reports ×0.95 (0.71–1.28), ICS-209-PLUS ×1.11 (0.79–1.56) (p23_loss_fire_count_trend.csv).

Is the share of fires that destroy at least one building changing, at the same exposure?

Carlson, CAL FIRE DINS and our record list only fires that damaged buildings, so they hold no fires with zero loss; this panel uses sources that also list the fires that destroyed nothing. Can't tell whether the share is changing. Among federal incident reports of fires that threatened at least one structure (ICS-209-PLUS, 2002–20, 12,280 incidents), the chance of destroying at least one building at the typical exposure (9 structures threatened) was between 17% and 29% a year; the odds changed ×1.07 per decade (range 0.99–1.16), which includes no change and also a small rise. The situation reports, which cannot be adjusted for exposure, give ×0.97 (0.80–1.22) over 2007–25. 2 · When a wildfire becomes a town fire

An urban conflagration here means at least 50 destroyed buildings in one chain, each within 30 m of another. A big block of close houses is a precondition; beyond that, the losses look like one smooth continuum rather than a separate kind of fire.

No fire became an urban conflagration unless it reached a block of more than 89 closely spaced buildings

Group each fire's buildings that stood before it into blocks (houses whose centres are within 35 m of each other). No fire whose largest block held 89 buildings or fewer became an urban conflagration: 0 of 338 in Carlson, 0 of 714 in our record and 0 of 429 in CAL FIRE's inspections. Above the line it happened in 35–44% of fires. What it suggests: a big enough block of closely spaced houses is a precondition; it is not enough on its own (wind and how the fire reaches the block matter too).Condition 1 on the paper outline (MTBS + 100 m), all fires with ≥ 1 exposed building (361, 739, 445 fires; 8, 10, 7 events). Cut 89 = two-rate split on log10(1 + largest exposed 35 m component). Part of the line is built in: a 30 m destroyed chain of ≥ 50 must lie inside a block of ≥ 50, so the evidence for 89 is the zero among blocks of 50–89. Exact binomial intervals. Held-out check, 2016–22 back-cast: 0 of 66 at or below 89.

Is there a natural size? The largest patch, fire by fire

If urban conflagrations were a separate kind of fire, the curves would drop and then flatten, leaving a gap between ordinary fires and town fires. They don't: patch sizes thin out smoothly from 1 building to over a hundred. A formal test for a second peak found none at any distance from 5 to 100 m in any record (smallest unadjusted p = 0.11). The 50-building line sits on a smooth slope; it is a convention.Complementary CDF of the largest destroyed chain at 30 m over fires with n_dest ≥ 10. Silverman critical-bandwidth test (2,000 smoothed bootstraps) on log10 of the largest destroyed chain at L, 10 L × 3 records, Holm-adjusted p ≥ 1.00 everywhere (min raw 0.114). Fisher–Jenks 2-class break at 30 m: 8 [5–11], 7 [5–11], 8 [6–16] buildings (bootstrap CI spans more than a factor 2); Gaussian mixtures prefer 2 components at L ≥ 20 m, but on skewed data that is expected and not separation.

Destroyed buildings cluster more than chance, building up between about 20 and 50 m

For each fire we redrew the same number of destroyed buildings at random among the fire's own buildings, and compared the largest patch. Real patches are bigger than chance at every distance above 5 m. Half of the extra clustering is reached by 20 m between centres and nearly all of it by 30–50 m; beyond that it adds little (in the CAL FIRE inspections it falls again). So the distances at which destroyed buildings hang together more than chance span roughly 20–50 m, and 30 m sits inside that range. Do losses lean downwind?

No. Across all three records, a building next to a destroyed one was lost about as often when it sat downwind of it as when it sat upwind. In a typical fire, neighbours downwind were lost 73.4 % of the time and neighbours upwind 74.0 % (our 2002–26 record, average over 159 fires). The difference, −0.5 points, is well inside what made-up wind directions give, and the ranges rule out a downwind lean larger than about 2 points in any record, far below the 12 points IBHS reported for Eaton and Palisades with a different measure. Wind clearly drives fires into towns (panels below); this panel finds no sign that it decides which neighbour of a lost house burns next.

How to read the comparison: when two neighbours are both lost, each sits downwind of the other in one direction and upwind in the other, so those pairs count the same on both sides. The test therefore asks where the survivors next to lost buildings sit: more often upwind of the losses (a downwind lean) or not.
Destroyed–destroyed ordered pairs are symmetric (each appears once downwind and once upwind), so p_down − p_up depends only on destroyed–survivor pairs: Σ destroyed-downwind = Σ destroyed-upwind = 97,556 pairs in R2; denominators 112,672 vs 112,350.

How this panel was checked
  • Pre-registered before any number (docs/ledger.d/2026-10-14-2355-downwind-lean--prereg.md); one later amendment added two checks (observation-based map only; fires where two maps agree) after the main run showed the 4 km map's direction was off for Tubbs and Marshall. Neither changes the answer.
  • Like for like: same statistic, distance (60 m), sectors and fire rule in all three full records, side by side. Fires without a town wind reading (fewer than 100 buildings nearby) are left out: our record 102 fires holding 1,705 destroyed buildings, CAL FIRE 24 incidents holding 583.
  • Denominators: building pairs within 60 m of a destroyed building, by sector; every share in the hovers names its pairs. Each fire counts once in the headline; the all-pairs version (big fires weigh more) and the version without the largest fire (Camp) are in the hovers and give the same answer.
  • Uncertainty from the independent unit: fires resampled 2,000 times; the rotated wind check keeps each town's real loss map and layout fixed and only turns the wind, so street and valley directions cannot create a false lean.
  • Town layout: a second measure compares the direction of lines between lost buildings with the direction of lines between all buildings in the town before the fire (streets, valleys). No record shows lost pairs lining up with the wind more than the layout does.
  • Wind direction is uncertain: burn-window directions from the 4 km simulation (CONUS404) are within 45° of the coarse global map (ERA5) for 271 of 327 fires; the two observation-based maps (HRRR, RTMA) agree for 228 of 243. Every alternative wind source gives the same answer (checks table).
  • Bearings use true north: building points were converted from the national map grid (whose north is about 14° off in California) before any angle was taken.
  • The side the fire came from gives a lean as small as the wind's, so this test cannot separate the two; neither shows a lean.
  • Claims match the evidence: 'no lean shown' with ranges that exclude a downwind lean above about 1–3 points depending on record. This is not a contradiction of IBHS (destroyed vs damaged, 60 m centres vs 3 m wall gaps, two fires); we cannot yet test their exact measure, which needs footprints and wall-to-wall gaps.
  • Fire weather is a description only here; it is never part of a definition or a model input.

3 · Density and building type

Density looks dangerous mostly because a handful of conflagrations happened in dense places: take out each record's five largest fires and the densest buildings hold a smaller share of the losses than of the buildings exposed.

How much more often are mobile homes destroyed than houses in the same fire?

4 · What only the new dataset can show

Two results that need what we added to Carlson's record: fires that destroyed few or no buildings, and an inventory of what stood before each fire.

Studying only fires known to have destroyed buildings makes a typical fire look 2 to 3 times as destructive

Most loss studies, Carlson's included, start from fires already known to have destroyed buildings. Our model lets us score every 2023–25 fire that reached at least 25 buildings, including fires nobody reported. In that full set a typical fire destroyed 9% of the buildings it reached; picking fires by reported loss doubles that (×1.9), and keeping only fires with 10 or more destroyed triples it (×3.0). What it suggests: per-fire destruction rates from loss-selected samples describe bad fires, not fires in general. Leads we have not chased (ideas welcome)

  • Official zeros that are not zero. Several 2023–25 fires that our model says destroyed buildings are recorded as 0 in the national situation reports, and public sources confirm losses for most of the ones we checked. How big the gap is nationally is still open.
  • Today's building maps forget past losses (last panel). Anyone measuring exposure from a current map undercounts the buildings old fires destroyed. How much does that bias published risk models?
  • Why does wind not pick the next house? Wind drives fires into towns, yet losses do not lean downwind house to house. Embers arriving from far upwind, or fire moving through yards, could both explain it.
  • Where it could happen next. We mapped every block big enough, within reach of wildland: the national map.
  • Outside the U.S. No fire below the line became an urban conflagration in ten other countries either, though our model is less reliable there and far fewer fires above the line did: the page; and what the literature says: a review.
