Structure Loss Pipeline · how we validate

How we validate

This project estimates, building by building, which structures a wildfire destroyed. Every accuracy figure on this site is measured on fires the model had never seen: to score a fire, we refit the model with that entire fire removed from its training data, and use the result. This page explains why the whole fire is the right thing to remove, where the decision threshold comes from, how the published data records which model made each call, and — at the end, without softening — the four things this design does not establish.

the short version

Three rules, and one number to carry

  • No published score came from a model that had seen the fire it scored. Not that building, not its neighbour, not any building in the same fire.
  • The decision threshold is held out too. It is chosen from scores that were themselves produced by models that had not seen the fires they scored, so it is not fitted on anything a model had memorised.
  • Nothing enters the model on the strength of evidence that has already been looked at. A candidate is measured on a larger set of fires, named in advance, before it is adopted.

Measured that way, against fire-service damage inspections, the destroyed call reaches an F1 of 0.9158 counting every building equally and 0.8729 counting every fire equally. Both are real; quoting one without the other is not. F1 is the harmonic mean of precision (what share of our destroyed calls were right) and recall (what share of the real destruction we found), so it punishes both errors.

the training pool

What the model learns from

Two sources of ground truth, each covering a different set of fires. The historical record is a published, hand-assembled census of buildings destroyed in United States wildfires, compiled by Carlson and colleagues from before-and-after imagery; fire-service damage inspections are CAL FIRE's programme of sending inspectors to each structure in a burn area to record its condition.

The 66 historical fires span eleven states — 41 in California, 9 in Oregon, 4 in Colorado, 3 in Washington, 2 each in Oklahoma and Tennessee, and one each in Florida, Kansas, New Mexico, Texas and Utah — across seven fire years from 2016 to 2022. The eight inspected incidents are the Airport, Borel, Bridge, Mountain and Park fires of 2024 and the Eaton, Palisades and TCU Complex fires of 2025.

⚠️ Both halves are dominated by a few fires. The three largest historical fires hold 29 % of that half's buildings, and Eaton and Palisades alone hold 84 % of the inspected half. That single fact is why every headline here is reported twice, and why the two versions differ by four to seven points.

The two sources do not count the same buildings

The historical record marks a major building that someone drew a point on from an image pair. An inspection marks a structure an inspector stood next to, including mobile homes, accessory dwellings and outbuildings, and grades it on six levels rather than two. We treat a structure as destroyed when the inspector graded it above 50 % damage, as surviving at 25 % or below, and we drop the 25–50 % band and the "inaccessible" grade rather than force them to one side: half-burned is neither, and inaccessible was never actually inspected. Over the fires the product publishes, that choice sets aside 352 inspection records.

On the same ground, over seventeen fires, the inspectors' destroyed count divided by the historical record's runs 0.82 to 1.84, median 1.24. That is a difference in what counts as a building, not in the model, and it is why no figure on this site ever pools the two truths into one number.

Source: the label-recode rule in the project's configuration, and the same-ground count table under outputs/combined_retrospective/ on the data share.

the method

Holding out a whole fire, and why nothing smaller will do

The rule is simple to state. For each of the 74 fires in turn: fit a model on all the other 73, and use it to score every building in the fire that was left out. Run it 74 times and every building in the pool carries a score from a model that never saw its fire.

The unit is the fire, not the building, and that is the whole point. Buildings inside one fire share a satellite scene, a season, a fuel type, a wind event, a smoke layer and a single survey crew. A model that has seen half a fire has effectively seen the other half, so holding out a random sample of buildings measures memorisation rather than skill. Removing the fire is the smallest hold-out that cuts every one of those shared threads at once.

The size of the difference is not hypothetical. The same model scored with blocks held out inside each fire reaches an AUC of 0.990; scored with whole fires held out it reaches 0.985. The gap is small here and much larger elsewhere: a published study of more than 100,000 of the same inspections reports 88.0 % under random cross-validation falling to 68.0 % once space is blocked, and the closest comparable per-building benchmark reports F1 0.800 on a random split against 0.655 with whole events held out. AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen destroyed building scores higher than a randomly chosen surviving one, so 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect ranking.

Source: the held-out scoring tables written beside the model on the data share; published comparisons as cited in the project's literature review.

One fire, end to end

The Eaton Fire of January 2025 is the largest fire in the pool by inspected structures. Here is exactly what happens to it.

  1. It is a training fire. 18,318 of its inspected structures pass the feature and label filters — 9,419 destroyed, 8,899 surviving — and they are training rows for every fold except their own.
  2. Its own model is fitted without it. A model is trained on the other 73 fires, 600,508 buildings, and nothing from Eaton is in it.
  3. That model scores all 18,318 Eaton rows. A structure on Altadena Drive gets a probability from a model that has never seen a building in this fire.
  4. The threshold is read off the held-out scores. Eaton's scores, together with the other seven incidents' held-out scores, are what the threshold rule reads. It picks the value that maximises the average of the eight per-fire F1 scores: 0.85.
  5. What it scores. At 0.85, Eaton's held-out model reaches precision 0.944, recall 0.898, F1 0.920, AUC 0.975. Eaton's own best possible threshold would have been 0.67 for an F1 of 0.924 — four thousandths, which is the price of shipping one number that works for every fire.
  6. The published product uses that same held-out model, not one fitted on all 74 fires. 64,319 inventory points are scored and 7,637 called destroyed.
  7. The check. Those points are matched one-to-one within 10 m to the field inspections: 14,955 pairs, precision 0.949, recall 0.905, F1 0.926, AUC 0.978 — plus a detection recall of 0.816, which is the part a confusion matrix cannot see, because it only counts buildings we found in the first place.

Source: the model's own summary and per-fold files, and outputs/forward_damage/per_fire.csv on the data share.

two ways to average

Counting every building, and counting every fire

Every result here is given twice, because the two answers a user might want are different questions.

Pooled

Throw every held-out building into one confusion matrix. This answers "across all these buildings, how often are we right?" — and it is dominated by the biggest fires, two of which hold 84 % of the inspected half.

Mean per fire

Score each fire separately, then average the fires, one vote each. This answers "what should I expect on the next fire?", which is usually the real question. A fire joins the average only if it has at least 100 scored buildings and 10 destroyed ones; below that its score is a statement about a handful of buildings. Those fires are still pooled and still counted.

the threshold

Where the cut-off comes from, and why it is held out as well

The model returns a probability; a threshold turns it into a call. Choosing that threshold is itself a fitted decision, so it gets the same treatment as the model. The rule is: try every value from 0.01 to 0.99, compute each held-out fire's F1 at that value, average the fires, and keep the best. On the eight inspected incidents that rule picks 0.85, and 0.85 is what the product applies to every fire.

It maximises the average of the fires rather than the pooled score, deliberately. Two fires hold 84 % of the buildings, so a threshold tuned on the pooled score is whatever those two fires want, and every small fire pays for it. A new fire is one case, not eighteen thousand cases. Because every score the rule reads came from a model that had not seen the fire it scored, the threshold is not fitted on anything a model had memorised.

Is the threshold holding the numbers back?

It can be checked, and it is. Beside every score at the shipped threshold, the pipeline also reports what that population would have reached at the best threshold fitted on its own answers — a number that can never be shipped, because it needs the answers. The gap between the two separates "the ranking is wrong" from "the threshold landed badly".

in the published data

Every row says which model called it

The model above is an evaluation device. The thing that ships is a per-fire product: our own list of buildings, each carrying a probability and a call. Some of those fires are in the training pool and some are not, so every published row records which model made its call.

Reading it: the held-out models are refitted and kept for exactly this purpose — same recipe, same seed, same threshold rule, the only difference being which rows the model never saw.

The consequence is the sentence that matters: no damage call in the published data was made by a model that had seen that fire. Of the fifteen recent fires the product covers, eight are training fires scored by their own held-out model and seven are outside the pool entirely. On the historical fires the same check was run explicitly: every scored row carries a held-out call, and the only rows that do not are 476 of 159,754 (0.30 %) that carry no probability at all.

keeping ourselves honest

Deciding what gets into the model in the first place

Holding fires out tells you how good a model is. It does not stop you from choosing a model by looking at the answer twenty times. Two further devices do that.

A larger set of fires, named before it is looked at

Every candidate addition to the model is first measured on the eight inspected incidents — which is eight fires, one region, two seasons. A second, larger set is therefore named in advance: 48 of the 66 historical fires, spanning eleven states and five years, and the candidate is scored on it once, against criteria written down beforehand.

It earns its keep. Across roughly 1,900 model fits, twelve candidate feature families have been measured. Three looked like real gains of one to three points on the eight incidents; on the 48-fire set the picture changed for every one of them, and changed in the same direction each time — California and the Pacific Northwest gain, Texas and New Mexico lose. The apparent headroom was an artefact of judging a method on eight fires from one state in two seasons.

Intervals that resample fires, not buildings

Every interval on this site is computed by resampling whole fires 2,000 times with replacement, recomputing the figure each time, and taking the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. Buildings inside a fire are not independent draws, so resampling buildings would give an interval far too narrow to be useful. When two models are compared on the same rows, the same fire multiplicities are applied to both sides, so the fire-to-fire spread cancels out of a quantity that never sees it.

the transfer test

Why we train on everything, and still report the transfer number

A reasonable reader will ask: if the fire-service inspections are in the training pool, how do we know the method transfers to data it has never met? The answer is that both models are built and both numbers are published, because they answer two different questions.

The transfer model

Trained on the 66 historical fires only, and then applied to the eight inspected incidents — fires it has never met, in a labelling scheme it has never met. Not one inspected building, in any fire, in any year, was ever shown to it. This is the cleanest transfer claim this data can support.

The shipped model

Trained on all 74 fires, and used for the published product, because the thing being delivered is a call on a real building and withholding half the evidence would make it worse. Its honesty comes from elsewhere: every fire in it is scored by a model that excluded that fire.

Both were run with everything else held identical — the same 62 features, the same learner, the same four random seeds, the same threshold rule, the same rows — over 308 model fits.

One more check runs in the opposite direction. Scored on the same 582,554 historical buildings under both recipes, training on the historical record alone gives a pooled F1 of 0.8509 and a mean per-fire F1 of 0.7824; training on both sources gives 0.8513 and 0.7797. Each pair lands inside the other's interval: adding the inspections neither helps nor hurts the historical half.

the limits

Four things this design does not establish

Each of these is measured rather than assumed, and each is a reason to be careful with a number from this site rather than a reason to discard it.

1. Fires near each other are not independent

Holding out a fire removes the fire, not the region. Two fires 30 km apart in the same fuel type in the same year are not independent draws, and the model that scores one has trained on the other. Sliced by state, the held-out results say the ranking travels and the threshold does not: AUC 0.991 in California against 0.959 everywhere else, and mean per-fire F1 0.807 against 0.735, closing to 0.829 and 0.800 if each fire were allowed its own threshold.

Source: the by-region held-out tables under outputs/generalization/ on the data share, read 2026-09-14.

2. There is no forward test in time yet

The historical half ends in 2022 and the inspected half is 2024–2025. Sensors, building stock and inspection practice all move over time, and holding out fires does nothing about that. A genuine forward test — score a season the model has never met, at a threshold fixed before that season existed — is registered and frozen, and it has not paid out, because the public inspection archive holds no records from the season in question. Until it does, holding out whole fires is the strongest claim available, and this site says so rather than implying the two are the same thing.

Inspection practice in particular is visibly unstable: the surviving share of inspection records runs 6–20 % for 2017–2018 against 46–87 % from 2019 onwards, because before 2019 the programme mainly recorded damaged structures.

3. The ground truth itself is not perfectly true

Both label sets are made by people, and they disagree. On 43,929 buildings that both of them describe, they disagree on 2.15 % — stable whether buildings are matched within 5 m or 20 m. Scored against each other, that is an F1 of 0.9838, which is the ceiling any model can reach against both at once. The disagreement is lopsided (663 buildings the inspectors call destroyed and the historical record calls surviving, against 280 the other way) and concentrated in exactly the class the model is worst on: minor outbuildings disagree 4.3 % of the time against single residences' 1.6 %.

On the buildings where the two sources disagree, the model is at chance — AUC 0.46 to 0.51. It fails precisely where people cannot agree. But removing those rows lifts F1 only from 0.9185 to 0.9252, so label noise explains a fraction of a point and not the rest.

Source: the truth-agreement tables written by the project's own point-comparison run on the data share, read 2026-09-14.

4. It is not yet a national claim

41 of the 66 historical fires and all eight inspected incidents are in California. Texas, Kansas, Florida, New Mexico and Utah are one fire each, and no out-of-state fire is later than 2022. One fire is not a state. Beyond California reports what the out-of-state evidence does and does not support.

⚠️ One more thing to hold on to: every accuracy figure here counts only buildings we matched to a ground-truth record. The product does not. Against the inspections, 4,496 destroyed records are never matched and appear in no confusion matrix; against the historical record, 3,057. If you want a count rather than a classification score, you have to multiply the detection step back in — never read one and quote the other.
