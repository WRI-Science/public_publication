How researchers think about urban conflagrations · 2026-10-13

148 sources checked one by one (98 peer-reviewed papers, 37 government reports, 13 industry, insurance, working papers or preprints; 1951–2026): how people define a conflagration, the ways fire moves through a town, 26 variables people measure or wonder about, and 11 questions we can test on parts of fires

By Carlo Broderick, NCEAS, UC Santa Barbara · 2026-10-13 16:46 PDT

Researchers agree that in the worst wildfire disasters most houses are lost to embers and to burning neighbours, not to the wall of flame from the forest, and that the disaster begins when too many houses catch at once for firefighters to handle. They do not agree on how much trees near houses matter, and nobody has given a number that says when a fire, or part of a fire, becomes an urban conflagration. Most writers describe it as something that happens in part of a fire: a grass or forest fire that turns into a town fire in some neighbourhoods. That matches the owner's idea that one fire can contain several urban conflagrations.Consensus: structure loss in WUI disasters is dominated by firebrand and structure-to-structure ignition under suppression saturation (Cohen 2000, 2008; Calkin et al. 2014, 2023; NIST TN 1910). Disputed: the marginal effect of canopy and defensible space once spacing is controlled (Knapp et al. 2021 vs Mockrin et al. 2023). Gap: no numeric, sub-fire (patch-level) definition; the transition is described per neighbourhood (FSRI 2024; FEMA 2025; Zamanialaei et al. 2025), never measured across many fires.

What people agree on, disagree on and don't know

The state of the field in one view

Agree

  • Most houses in the big disasters are lost to embers and burning neighbours, not to the flame front (Cohen; NIST; Westhaver; Ribeiro et al.).
  • Inside a burning town, spacing between buildings and the number of buildings close by are the strongest measured predictors (Knapp et al. 2021; Zamanialaei et al. 2025; IBHS 2025; Kenny et al. 2026; Farajpoor & Narimani 2026).
  • The disaster begins when ignitions outnumber firefighters (Cohen 2008; Calkin et al. 2014, 2023).
  • Strong, dry downslope winds are behind most of the loss (Abatzoglou et al. 2023; Keeley & Syphard 2019).
  • Fences, mulch, firewood and sheds can carry fire to houses (NIST experiments; FEMA 2025).

Disagree

  • Density: across a landscape, loss is highest at low to middle density (Syphard et al. 2012); inside a burning town, denser means more loss (Alexandre et al. 2016; Knapp et al. 2021).
  • Trees near houses: strong in the Camp Fire (Knapp et al. 2021), weak or inconsistent in Woolsey, Eaton and Palisades (Mockrin et al. 2023; Kenny et al. 2026; Norlen et al. 2026).
  • Clearing vegetation vs hardening buildings: which matters more (Syphard et al. 2014; Syphard & Keeley 2019; IBHS 2025).
  • Whether 'urban conflagration' is a kind of fire (insurance, fire service, NWCG) or a stage inside a fire (FSRI 2024, 2025; FEMA 2025; Calkin et al. 2023; NFPA on Oakland 1991).
  • Conflagration vs firestorm: a moving, wind-driven front vs a stationary fire with indrafts (NWCG; Countryman 1964; Glasstone & Dolan 1977); everyday use mixes them (Oakland 1991 is called both).

Don't know yet

  • A number: no source defines an urban conflagration by size, area or density.
  • Patches: nobody has mapped separate house-to-house patches across many fires.
  • How much loss is house-to-house vs embers from the wildland, fire by fire (done only for Waldo Canyon and the Witch Fire).
  • Whether a spacing threshold exists, or loss rises smoothly as buildings get closer.
  • How firefighting capacity and water pressure change whether a patch keeps growing.

Three ways fire moves through a town, and two things that make it a disaster

Hover the houses, trees, embers and arrows for what each part means and who measured it.

Kinds of urban conflagration in the literature

Real fires mix these. The kinds describe what dominated in a given neighbourhood, which is why they fit the idea of labelling parts of fires rather than whole fires.Classification by dominant ignition pathway (Caton et al. 2017; Hakes et al. 2017); the unit in every case study is a community or neighbourhood, not the fire.

House to house

A burning building sets the next one alight.Structure-to-structure spread by flame contact, radiant heat and firebrands from burning buildings.

Where it dominated: Mountain Shadows (Waldo Canyon 2012: 296 of 344 destroyed homes lit by other homes), Grass Valley 2007, Angora 2007, Coffey Park (Tubbs 2017), Paradise (Camp 2018), Marshall 2021, Lahaina 2023, Palisades Village 2025. Where it did not: the widely spaced Trails community in the Witch Fire 2007.

Key sources: Maranghides et al. 2015; Cohen & Stratton 2008; Murphy et al. 2007; Maranghides & Mell 2009; FEMA Marshall Fire Mitigation Assessment Team 2025

Trees, yards, fences

Burning trees, hedges, fences, mulch or sheds carry fire to a house or between houses.Vegetation-to-structure and connector-mediated spread (house-tree-house chains, fences, mulch, outbuildings).

Trees and yards next to a house expose it to flames and embers; fences, mulch, firewood and sheds act as fuses between houses that are otherwise too far apart. Experiments are clear; field evidence on trees is mixed.

Key sources: Knapp et al. 2021; Mockrin et al. 2023; Gibbons et al. 2012; Butler et al. 2022; Cohen 2004

Wind and embers

Strong dry winds throw embers far ahead, so many houses catch at once.Ember (firebrand) storms under downslope winds (Santa Ana, Diablo, chinook/foehn); long-distance spotting.

Downslope winds carry embers far ahead of the fire, so dozens or hundreds of houses ignite at the same time. Once enough houses burn, house-to-house spread takes over.

Key sources: Abatzoglou et al. 2023; Keeley & Syphard 2019; Storey et al. 2020; Maranghides et al. 2021; Blanchi et al. 2010

Too many fires

More houses burn at once than firefighters and water can handle.Suppression saturation: simultaneous ignitions exceed engine, crew and water-system capacity.

Every expert definition of a wildfire disaster includes this step: simultaneous ignitions overwhelm firefighting. Water pressure often fails too.

Key sources: Calkin et al. 2014; Calkin et al. 2023; Cohen 2017; Maranghides et al. 2015; Pierce et al. 2025

Wildland to town

The moment a grass, brush or forest fire turns into a fire that spreads through streets.Wildland-to-urban transition; distance into the built area; fire growth rate.

Researchers increasingly describe an urban conflagration as a stage inside a fire, in particular neighbourhoods, not as a kind of fire. Nobody has measured where and when the switch happens across many fires.

Key sources: Fire Safety Research Institute 2024; FEMA Marshall Fire Mitigation Assessment Team 2025; Zamanialaei et al. 2025; Calkin et al. 2023; Balch et al. 2024

How people define it

Definitions found, with their unit

33 definitions. None defines an urban conflagration by a number. 17 describe a block, a neighbourhood, a process or a stage inside a fire; apart from the firestorm rule, none of those gives a number. Open questions our data can answer

Literature gaps turned into tests on parts of fires

These are proposals, not results. Each would be pre-registered before any number is looked at, and reported on all three records (the USGS Carlson record, our full 2002–2026 record and CAL FIRE inspections).Unit of analysis: patch (connected component of destroyed buildings at a stated link distance) or exposed block; wind and fire growth are fire attributes, used only to describe patches, not as product predictors.

H1 · Is an urban conflagration a whole fire or a part of one?

Many fires contain more than one separate patch of linked destroyed buildings, and the number of such patches is not explained by fire size alone.

Test: Count patches per fire at several link distances; compare with fire area and buildings exposed.

From: Calkin et al. 2023; Fire Safety Research Institute 2024; Dunn 2008; Maranghides et al. 2022

H2 · Is there a separation threshold for house-to-house spread?

Within patches, the chance a building is destroyed falls with distance to the nearest destroyed building, and the reach is longer when the wind is strong.

Test: Distance-decay curves within patches, split by wind at the time the town burned.

From: Knapp et al. 2021; McNamara & Mell 2024; Prasad et al. 2022; Maranghides et al. 2023

H3 · Does density help or hurt?

Among patches the fire reached, denser groups lose a larger share; across all exposed land the share lost peaks at intermediate density.

Test: Same data, two scales, stated side by side.

From: Syphard et al. 2012; Alexandre et al. 2016; Kramer et al. 2019

H4 · Do trees between houses carry fire?

At the same spacing and wind, patches with more canopy between buildings grow larger, mostly where buildings are 20-60 m apart.

Test: Tree-linked vs house-only links; follows up our earlier test of trees between houses.

From: Knapp et al. 2021; Mockrin et al. 2023; Gibbons et al. 2012

H5 · Can wind-driven and house-to-house patches be told apart from the map of losses?

Wind-driven patches are stretched along the wind and contain more isolated destroyed buildings; house-to-house patches are compact.

Test: Shape and fragmentation of patches against wind direction.

From: Calkin et al. 2023; Maranghides et al. 2021; Maranghides et al. 2015

H6 · Does overwhelmed firefighting decide whether a patch grows?

Patches that start where many buildings are exposed at once grow into conflagrations more often than equally dense patches with few buildings exposed.

Test: Size of the exposed block as a proxy for simultaneous ignitions.

From: Calkin et al. 2014; Cohen 2017; Maranghides et al. 2015

H7 · Do building codes protect a patch, not just a house?

Patches with more post-2008 (California) or post-1990 buildings stop smaller.

Test: Needs year built; California first.

From: Baylis & Boomhower 2021; Kumar et al. 2026

H8 · What stops a patch?

Patch edges coincide with gaps (roads, parks, wider spacing) more than with changes in canopy.

Test: Compare edge buildings with interior buildings.

From: Murphy et al. 2007; Maranghides et al. 2015

H9 · How far into town does loss reach?

House-to-house patches reach further from the wildland edge than ember losses, which fall off within a few hundred metres.

Test: Distance from the wildland edge by patch kind.

From: Chen & McAneney 2004; Caggiano et al. 2020

H10 · Are mobile-home parks conflagration seeds?

Patches containing manufactured-home parks lose a larger share than other patches of the same density.

Test: Structure type where records have it (CAL FIRE DINS).

From: Troy et al. 2022; Maranghides et al. 2021

H11 · Do loss models transfer between neighbourhoods?

Predictors measured on patches (spacing, size of the exposed block, canopy between buildings) keep their accuracy when whole patches or fires are held out; building-level models lose more.

Test: Hold out whole patches and whole fires, not random buildings.

From: Farajpoor & Narimani 2026
