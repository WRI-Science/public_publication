Structure Loss Pipeline · confusion matrix

Confusion Matrix

Four numbers decide whether a damage model is any good: the buildings it called destroyed that were, the ones it missed, the ones it raised a false alarm on, and the ones it correctly left alone. Here they are against every answer sheet we have — as raw counts, as a share of all the buildings, and as a share of their own row, because the raw numbers on their own are hard to read.

how to read one

Two rows, two columns, four corners

The rows are what the truth says. The columns are what we said. Every building that both the truth and this pipeline know about lands in exactly one of the four corners.

hit · top left

It burned, and we said so. The share of this row is recall — how much of the real destruction we found.

miss · top right

It burned, and we said it stood. The share of this row is the miss rate. These are the losses a count built on our call would leave out.

false alarm · bottom left

It stood, and we said it burned. The share of this row is the false-alarm rate; as a share of everything we called destroyed it is 1 − precision.

hit · bottom right

It stood, and we said so. The share of this row is specificity. It is the largest corner on almost every fire, which is why accuracy alone tells you nothing here.

Every cell below shows three things: the count in large type, its share of all the pairs, and its share of its own row. Cells are tinted by that row share — green for the two corners where we agreed with the truth, orange for the two where we did not.

What one row of a matrix counts. Every count here is a building–fire pair: one building, in one fire, that both the ground-truth survey and this project know about. A truth point and one of our building locations are treated as the same building when they lie within 10 m of one another, matched one-to-one, so no building is counted twice.

Nothing here was scored by a model that had seen it. The model that scores a fire is trained only on the other fires, so every number below is measured on fires the model never saw. Thresholds are fixed in advance rather than tuned per fire.

Accuracy is the wrong number to look at. In a typical study area only about 6–7 % of buildings are destroyed, so a model that called everything “survived” would score about 93 % accuracy and be useless. That is why this page reports precision, recall and F1 — the harmonic mean of the two, which punishes both missing real destruction and over-calling it.

Two estimates, not one. The pipeline makes a primary estimate about two and a half months after a fire starts, from before-and-after satellite images; it exists for every building in every fire covered. It makes a refined estimate about a year later, once a second post-fire image window and a year-over-year satellite summary of the landscape exist; that one is more accurate, and it exists for most buildings in most fires but not all. Both are shown for every ground-truth source, on the same buildings, so the two matrices are a like-for-like comparison and not two different populations.

coverage

Where the refined call does not reach

The refined call needs a clean post-fire annual embedding and a second post-fire window. Where it has neither, there is no refined call at all — and a pair with no refined call is left out of the refined matrix rather than counted as a mistake.

Why the refined call does not reach every pair. A pair with no refined call is not counted as an error — it is left out of the refined matrix, which is why both matrices on this page are computed on the pairs that carry both.

where the gain came from

Which buildings the refined call fixed

Every scored pair is tagged with the conditions that make it hard — bare ground under the building, canopy over the pixel, a small outbuilding, a score sitting inside the cut band, and so on — and the same tags are applied to both calls. Two buckets carry most of the improvement against both answer sheets.
