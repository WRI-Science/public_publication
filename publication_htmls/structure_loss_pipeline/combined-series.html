Structure Loss Pipeline · the dataset

One layer, two eras

The USGS Carlson building-loss record and this pipeline's per-fire products, harmonised onto one schema, one row per structure per fire — so a user reads a single file instead of joining two datasets and discovering the joins do not work. Sixteen fires are now described by both methods. This page is what is inside the release, and the ways the two halves are not equivalent.

What you get, 13 September 2026

One file, 377 fires, 2,069,752 rows, one row per structure per fire per source. 361 of those fires are the 2002–2022 hand survey, carried verbatim — 1,770,939 rows. Sixteen are fires of 2024–2025 this pipeline produced. And seventeen more are fires the survey already covers that this pipeline has now also enumerated, so they are described twice, once by each method.

The internal build is 2,102,662 rows; 32,910 of them (1.57 %) are dropped from the released package for a licence reason set out at the bottom of this page.

⚠️ The row unit is now (fire, source), and the two descriptions of a dual-source fire must never be summed. Neither is a subset of the other: one is the survey's hand enumeration of a fire's buildings, the other is this pipeline's independent inventory of the same buildings. Group by (fire_id, series_source); a pooled count over an era picks one source. The building identifier is namespaced per source so nothing in the schema can collapse them, and the fire identifier is deliberately the same on both sides so the two descriptions are visibly of one fire. The layer holds 394 such pairs over 377 fires, and a test refuses a summary that aggregates across them.

Source: outputs/releases/v0.5.0/manifest.json rule_5b and dual_source_rule; docs/combined-series-spec.md rule 5b, enforced by slp.eval.combined_series.assert_no_cross_source_aggregation.

The survey's 73,534 destroyed is the sum of its own hand calls over all 361 fires and is not a model output. The 879 rows with no damage call are Franklin 2024 rows ceded to a later fire by the overlap-attribution rule, each carrying a reason.

Every row carries series_source (carlson or slp), an era whose boundaries are sensor launch dates rather than preferences, harmonised damage fields, per-row provenance, and the uncertainty its era can support: ours carries destroyed_prob; Carlson's is explicitly null rather than faked. A field only one era can fill is null on the other side and is never back-filled.

Our half is one detector, one frame and one damage model throughout, and the build manifest computes that rather than asserting it: the heterogeneity warning is null and no fire is missing a damage call. A provenance column says on every row whether the call came from the production model scoring a fire it had never seen or from a leave-one-fire-out refit because the fire sits in that model's own training pool — which is every one of the sixteen retrospective fires. An in-sample score is never shipped.

Every one of the survey's 1,770,939 rows now also carries ten geometry columns — which source produced the building's analysis polygon, how confident that is, what kind of raster cell it owns, how many cells it owns, and whether the detector found it. Join coverage is 100.0000 % on all 361 fires, maximum coordinate displacement 1.3 × 10⁻⁸ m. What is behind them is the augmented historical record.

04 · across the boundary

Is a trend across the join real, or is it the method?

The question a user of a joined dataset actually has. It is answered by running this pipeline on old fires the survey already covers — twenty-three for the loss call, sixteen end to end.

Two experiments answer it, and both are set out in full on running the pipeline backwards. The short version:

Source: outputs/combined_retrospective/{damage_pooled.json,end_to_end.{csv,json}}, 2026-09-12. The comparability section of docs/combined-series-spec.md is pinned to the ten-fire subset it was written on and is reproduced exactly by the wider run.

⚠️ Three things follow for anyone plotting across the join.

1. Apply a factor of about 0.77 to a modern destroyed count before comparing it to a historical one — and do not treat that factor as stable. It runs 0.48 to 1.80 across seventeen fires and it tracks how well the buildings were found, not anything about the loss model.

2. It is a net of a large miss and a large addition. We recover 6,451 of the survey's 10,808 destroyed buildings in region and add 1,862 destroyed calls on structures it did not record as destroyed, 970 of them on buildings it never enumerated at all. The two partly cancel.

3. The threat to a trend is composition, not time. The bias does not drift with the year; it drifts with the fire, and it tracks how much of a fire's building stock burned. A trend built from catastrophic fires crosses the join safely; one that mixes in many low-loss fires does not.

Source: outputs/combined_retrospective/end_to_end.csv and damage_trend.json; the full working is on the retrospective page.

A published rule of thumb has been withdrawn. Earlier versions of this dataset told users that every fire above about 7 % destroyed lands between 0.82 and 1.06 of the survey's count. On twenty-three fires instead of ten that is false — six of the twelve fires above 7 % fall outside the band. What survives is the direction: the mean ratio is 1.117 above 7 % destroyed against 0.952 below 2.5 %.

The two truth sources disagree with each other by up to 1.8×, on 13 of 17 fires in the same direction. Inspectors count mobile homes, second dwellings and outbuildings the survey never counted, and several inspection records can sit on one building. A modern count validated against inspections is not in the survey's units, whatever the model does — which is the second reason a structure count is not comparable across the join without a fire-specific correction.

Two flags on Carlson's own release

The series carries Carlson verbatim. Nothing is dropped, merged, re-attributed or corrected: it is the USGS's release, and silently cleaning another team's published census inside a derived product would make the two impossible to reconcile. A user who counts rows in the carlson half gets exactly what they would get counting rows in the source shapefile. Two properties of that release will nevertheless break a naive count, so they are measured once over the whole national file and shipped as columns:

ColumnTrue whenRows
carlson_exact_duplicatethe row repeats an earlier row exactly — the same twelve published attributes and the same coordinates20,296 of 1,770,939
carlson_multi_fire_locationmore than one Carlson fire claims this location, at 0.5 m324,910 (18.3 %), at 152,537 distinct locations

Source: outputs/combined_series/manifest.json carlson_release_flags, measured over the whole national release before any subset is applied; docs/combined-series-spec.md §4c. Both null on every slp row, and both are written by a build stage rather than by an ad-hoc read. The duplicate flag marks 20,296 rows in 40,580-row duplicate groups at 20,284 distinct duplicated rows; the multi-fire flag marks 324,910 rows at 152,537 of the release's 1,589,122 distinct locations.

One fire carries most of the duplication: CA_2008_SAYRE alone holds 19,859 of the 20,296 repeats — 40,146 rows at 20,287 locations. A national destroyed count that does not drop them is wrong by that much. The multi-fire flag marks every row at such a location, because without an attribution rule there is no "later" fire to prefer. De-duplicate on geometry before any pooled count across Carlson fires. These are flags, not fixes, and not a criticism: the duplicates are almost certainly an artefact of the 145-step select-then-merge lineage the release's own metadata records, and the multi-fire locations are a correct consequence of a definition — one row per (structure, fire) — that this series shares.

On the shipped layer the same exposure is now measurable on both halves. 328,786 of Carlson's 1,770,939 rows (18.6 %) and 9,264 of our 168,197 (5.5 %) sit where another fire also enumerated a structure, and 34 locations are coded destroyed under more than one fire — all of them on Carlson's side, because the overlap-attribution rule took ours to zero. (logs/CHANGELOG.md 2026-09-12 11:15 PT §3; outputs/forward_damage/overlap_series.json.)

MIT for the code, CC BY 4.0 for the data, copyright Carlo Broderick. One footprint source is ODbL-licensed, and the question is resolved by exclusion rather than by argument: every row whose geometry came from it is dropped from the package — 32,910 of 2,102,662 rows (1.57 %) across 29 (fire, source) pairs, every one of them ours. None of the survey's 1,770,939 rows is touched.

⚠️ The exclusion is uneven, and it is largest exactly where the footprint database is weakest. It removes 0.03 % of Atlas 2017's rows and 36.1 % of CZU 2020's, with South Fork NM at 28.4 % and Dixie 2021 at 16.5 %. A user counting structures from the package on a rural fire is counting a filtered inventory; the internal build keeps those rows, and every performance number this project reports about itself is computed on the full one.

Microsoft footprints were also used as a training prior for the detector, which is a different act from redistribution: the network reads a raster band saying a footprint database has a building here, and the weights and every structure the detector outputs are this project's own work. No Microsoft polygon, attribute or identifier is redistributed in any form.
