Structure density and urban conflagration

Structure loss vs building density: is there a threshold? · 2026-10-12

Cumulative share of destroyed buildings against density, and breakpoint tests matched to the roads paper (Fong et al. 2026). Carlson 2002–22, CAL FIRE inspections 2023–26, and our proposed 2002–26 record with and without fires that destroyed 1–9 buildings (614 fires, 346,852 exposed and 90,176 destroyed buildings), each also with its five largest fires removed. Density uses Carlson's own buildings for 2002–22 and our inventory for 2023–26, counted the way Carlson counts and calibrated to Carlson on overlapping fires.

By Carlo Broderick, NCEAS, UC Santa Barbara · 2026-10-12 13:49 PDT

No general threshold. Across our 2002–26 record, buildings with more than 20 neighbours within 100 m were 19% of exposed and 24% of destroyed buildings, and loss rises smoothly with density (a smooth curve fits better than any break, by 22 AIC). Remove its five largest fires (Camp, Eaton, Palisades, Tubbs, Almeda Drive) and the same dense buildings are 13% of exposed but only 7% of destroyed. The density premium comes from a handful of conflagration fires; it is not a level every fire crosses.

How to read this page

Cumulative curve. Buildings (or neighbourhoods, or fires) are lined up from sparsest to densest; the line shows the share of all losses piled up by each point. On the dotted diagonal, losses follow exposure. Below it, losses concentrate in the dense end (dense places lost more than their share); above it, sparse places lost more. The large dot is the fitted break; hover for its range and the verdict.

The lines. Solid = all fires; dashed = the same data with its five largest fires removed. Tick or untick a line in the key to show or hide it on both charts. Carlson 2002–22: Hand-mapped by USGS (Carlson et al.): every building inside each fire, burned or not. CAL FIRE inspections 2023–26: Every CAL FIRE damage-inspection incident, all sizes; outcome from the inspection, no model calls. Our 2002–26 record, with 1–9 fires: Carlson for 2002–22, then our pipeline for 2023–26 (every fire with a reported loss), with CAL FIRE's inspection result replacing the model call wherever a building was inspected. Our 2002–26 record, without 1–9 fires: The same, keeping only fires that destroyed 10 or more buildings in every year, so the whole record follows Carlson's rule.

Breaks. As in the roads paper, a one-break fit, plus a step model and a smooth curve. A threshold needs evidence that some break exists (p below 0.05) and a break model that fits better than the smooth curve. Ranges come from resampling whole fires 1,000 times.

Cumulative share of losses against density

Where do the losses pile up as density rises?

At the 500 m scale, Carlson's own counts show a step near 385 buildings/km² (odds of loss about 1.5×) that falls away without its five largest fires (p 0.49). In our 2002–26 record it is smooth (p 0.01); our inventory undercounts dense burned blocks, so neighbourhood-scale results after 2022 are the least reliable here. The two 2002–26 lines (with and without 1–9 fires) nearly coincide because those fires hold few of the losses; untick one to see the other.

Do denser buildings lose more than the rest of their own fire?

Destroyed ÷ expected, where expected uses each building’s own fire loss rate, so differences between fires drop out. The rise appears only in the densest few per cent of buildings. Bins with fewer than 300 exposed buildings are not drawn.

Is the rise in losses from bigger fires or from more fires?

Incident reports: the growth in losses from 2007–12 to 2013–22 came from the largest fires. Fires that destroyed 1,000 or more buildings account for 91% of the increase (95% range 69%–109%), while the number of fires with any loss did not rise. Given how many buildings a fire threatened, the chance of any loss was 23% in 2002–12 and 23% in 2013–22 (ICS-209-PLUS, at the median exposure). 2023–26 shows no further rise.
