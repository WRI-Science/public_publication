Structure Loss Pipeline · in plain language

How it works

This project estimates, building by building, which structures a wildfire destroyed, using only free satellite imagery and public maps of building outlines. That is two jobs, not one: first build a list of the buildings that stood before the fire, then decide for each of them whether the fire destroyed it. This page explains both in plain language, says how accurate each is, and shows how the same process runs on a fire nobody has looked at, anywhere in the lower 48 states. The headline: against fire-service inspections, the destroyed call scores an F1 of 0.916 across 36,272 inspected structures, with every fire held out of the model that scored it.

Why two jobs

A loss count is only as good as the inventory it starts from

Counting destroyed buildings sounds like one task. It is two, and the first is the harder one. Before a fire, you need a list of the structures that existed. After it, you need a call on each one. If the pre-fire list is missing a fifth of the houses, no amount of skill on the burned-or-not question recovers them, and the loss count is wrong by construction.

The best existing record of United States building loss is hand-assembled: a published dataset compiled by Carlson and colleagues, one point per destroyed building, covering 361 wildfires between 2002 and 2022. Hand digitisation does not scale, and that record ends in 2022. This project produces the same kind of per-building record automatically, from three inputs that exist for every fire in the country: national building-footprint databases, the United States Department of Agriculture's aerial photography programme (NAIP, roughly 60 cm per pixel, reflown every two to three years), and Sentinel-2 — a pair of free European Space Agency satellites that photograph every point on Earth about every five days at 10 m per pixel.

Part 1

Finding the structures

Public building-footprint databases already outline most buildings in the country, and they are hard to beat outright. The method is therefore built around them: start from the footprints, add what they miss with a purpose-trained detector, and keep a record of where every building in the list came from.

Start from the footprints, because they are the product

Two public databases already outline nearly every building in the country: FEMA's USA Structures, and Microsoft's machine-generated footprints. Each polygon carries the date of the imagery it was drawn from, and we admit a footprint only if that date falls before the fire. That rule matters more than it sounds: a footprint traced from post-fire imagery cannot contain a house that burned, so a database used naively undercounts losses in exactly the places that matter.

Add what the databases miss, with an imagery detector

A segmentation network reads the most recent pre-fire aerial imagery at 60 centimetre resolution, four bands, with the footprints supplied as a fifth input band so it learns to fill gaps rather than redraw what is already known. Its output is a probability map of where a roof is. We cut that map at a threshold, keep connected patches of at least 20 square metres, and turn each into a point. Any patch that already overlaps a footprint is discarded as redundant. What survives is the detector's genuine addition.

Because the aerial program flies each state only every two or three years, we also run the detector on the previous two flights and keep any structure seen in any of them. On the forested rural fires this multi-year union is the single largest improvement we found.

Tag rather than delete

Every point carries three tags. Its source: which database, or the detector and which year's imagery. Its size class: major above 50 square metres, matching the USGS definition of a building, or minor for sheds and outbuildings. And a triage score for the footprints themselves, explained next.

Auditing the footprints: triage, not deletion

About one footprint in seven inside a burned area has no corresponding building in the hand-assembled historical record. Some are artefacts at the edge of the digitised area, some are buildings that were demolished before the fire, some were never real. Removing them automatically is not currently defensible: a detector run without the footprint input ranks the suspect polygons reasonably well, but no cut-off exceeds 36 % precision, which means roughly two real buildings would be deleted for every spurious one removed.

So the method triages instead of deleting. Each footprint carries a detector score computed without the footprint input, and its distance to the nearest independent detection. Suspect footprints stay in the list, ranked, flagged for a person to check.

What the numbers say

Two independent datasets score this list, and they answer different questions. The hand-assembled historical record enumerates every major building in a fire's area, so it can measure both what we find (recall: of the buildings that really existed, the share we listed) and what we invent (precision: of the buildings we listed, the share that really existed). California's fire-service damage inspections — in which inspectors walk the burn area and record each structure's condition — cover only the structures inspectors visited, so they measure recall in damaged neighbourhoods but cannot measure precision at all. A ground-truth point and one of our building locations count as the same building when they lie within 10 m of each other, matched one-to-one.

The noise floor. Retraining the detector with nothing changed but the random seed moves a single rural fire by up to two recall points, and the overall score by a tenth of a point. Differences smaller than that are not real, and this site does not report them as results.

What the inventory cannot do

It cannot see a house under a closed tree canopy, and neither can the footprint databases, which were drawn from the same kind of imagery. The one place those structures become visible is after the fire, when the canopy is gone and a concrete pad or a debris field remains. A separate post-fire pass looks for exactly that in the next aerial flight. Its ceiling is set by timing: the next flight is often a year later, and debris removal has usually cleared the lot by then. It adds a few points where the imagery is timely and is kept as an optional step, not a dependency.

Part 2

Deciding whether each structure burned

The question is asked once per building on the list: given what satellites saw at this spot before and after the fire, was the structure destroyed? This is the part of the method that performs best, and it is where the published accuracy figures come from.

One window each side of the fire

Sentinel-2 photographs the ground in visible, near-infrared and shortwave-infrared light at 10 to 20 metre resolution, every five days, and has imaged every fire in the country since 2016.

For each structure we build a "before" picture from the 45 days ending the day before ignition, and an "after" picture from a 60 day window that opens two weeks after ignition, once the smoke has cleared. Within each window we take the median of all clear observations, so a single cloudy or smoky pass does not corrupt it.

What the model actually sees

At each structure's own 10 metre pixel, and averaged over the 3 by 3 block around it, we record six bands of light, four vegetation and burn indices built from them, and the before-to-after change in each. That is 62 numbers per structure. A gradient-boosted tree ensemble maps them to a probability of destruction. It is a tabular model, not an image model, and it trains in minutes.

The signal is the familiar one: a burned lot loses near-infrared brightness and gains shortwave-infrared reflectance. The single most informative number is the change in the normalised burn ratio averaged over the 3 × 3 block. The model uses optical imagery only.

Training on the right labels, and a fixed threshold

The model trains on the hand-assembled historical record and on fire-service damage inspections, which are the judgements it is meant to reproduce. Every accuracy figure on this site is measured on held-out fires: the model that scores a fire was trained only on the other fires, so no fire is ever in its own training set.

The destroyed call is made at a single fixed probability, 0.88. That number is chosen to maximise the average F1 across fires rather than the pooled F1, which the two very large Los Angeles fires would otherwise dominate; it is fixed in advance and is not tuned to the fire being scored. A second, advisory call ships beside it, using a threshold fitted to each fire's own distribution of scores: it gets a fire's destroyed total closer while classifying individual buildings slightly worse, and both are published so a user can choose. The method in full gives the numbers for each.

A second, more accurate estimate arrives about a year later

The estimate above is the primary one. It is available about two and a half months after a fire starts, which is as soon as a clean post-fire image window exists, and it covers every building on the list for every fire. Roughly a year later a second, refined estimate becomes possible, because two further pieces of evidence have appeared: a second post-fire image window, and an annual satellite embedding — a freely published yearly summary of what each 10 m patch of ground looked like across a whole year, as 64 numbers per pixel. Comparing the year before the fire with the year after is a compact and unusually stable way to see a place change.

The refined estimate uses 242 numbers per building instead of 62 and calls a building destroyed above a probability of 0.84. Against fire-service inspections, scored on the same 28,316 buildings, its F1 is 0.9606 where the primary estimate scores 0.9208. It is better wherever it exists, but it does not exist everywhere: fires that predate the clean annual summaries, and a small number of buildings with no second post-fire window, have no refined estimate at all.

What the damage model cannot do

A 10 metre pixel cannot cleanly separate a destroyed house from an intact neighbour 12 metres away, so the model is weakest where destruction is scattered rather than block by block. It also reads as a severity index rather than a switch: structures inspectors rated lightly damaged score around 0.5, between the intact and the destroyed, and about one in five of them crosses the destroyed threshold. The published dataset carries the probability, so a user who needs a different balance of misses and false alarms can move the cut.

Higher-resolution post-fire imagery would resolve individual buildings, but it exists only for some fires. Because the product must run the same way everywhere, it is an upgrade path, not part of the standard process.

Part 3

Running it on a new fire, anywhere in the lower 48

Nothing in the standard process is specific to California except the inspection labels it was validated against. Every input is national or global.

  1. 1
    Define the fire. An ignition date and an area. For most fires the perimeter from the national incident database is enough; the area is buffered so structures at the edge are included.
  2. 2
    Build the inventory. Fetch the two footprint databases for the counties involved, keep polygons dated before ignition, run the roof detector on the latest and two earlier aerial flights, union and tag. Output: a probability map and a point layer.
  3. 3
    Sample the satellite. For every point, the before and after windows from Sentinel-2, medians and changes, 62 numbers.
  4. 4
    Score. The trained model gives each structure a destroyed probability; the fixed cut gives the call. Output: the point layer with probability, call, source and size tags, and summary tables by fire.
  5. 5
    Check against independent truth. Each new California incident is scored before it is ever used for training, so it is a genuine forward test. Outside California, the hand-assembled historical record covers about a hundred fires in other states through 2022; scored with each fire held out of its own model, the area under the curve across them is 0.986. That is the evidence that the method is not California-specific.

Three limits travel with the dataset, and a user should plan around them. The list of buildings misses roughly a fifth of major buildings on held-out ground, and it misses destroyed buildings more often than surviving ones wherever footprints were drawn from imagery flown after the fire. About one footprint in seven is a candidate that may not correspond to a real pre-fire building; those are flagged rather than removed. And the destroyed call blurs at the scale of a single house among survivors, because the satellite pixel is 10 m across. All three are measured, and the measurements are published on this site.

Glossary

Footprint
A polygon outlining a building's roof, drawn by hand or by a model from overhead imagery.
Recall
Of the buildings that really existed, the share we found.
Precision
Of the buildings we listed, the share that really existed.
F1
A single score balancing precision and recall; 1.0 is perfect.
Held out
Data the model never saw during training, used to measure it honestly.
Major structure
At least 50 square metres of roof, the USGS definition of a building.
Triage score
A ranking of how suspect a footprint is, for a person to review; never an automatic deletion.
Primary estimate
The destroyed call available about two and a half months after a fire starts, for every building in every fire covered.
Refined estimate
A more accurate destroyed call available about a year later, for most buildings in most fires, once more imagery exists.
Sentinel-2
Two free European Space Agency satellites imaging every point on Earth about every five days at 10 m per pixel.
NAIP
The US Department of Agriculture's aerial photography programme: roughly 60 cm per pixel, reflown every two to three years.
Annual satellite embedding
A published yearly summary of each 10 m patch of ground as 64 numbers, so one year can be compared with the next.
Matched at 10 m
A ground-truth point and one of our building locations are the same building if they lie within 10 m, matched one-to-one.
Pooled vs mean-per-fire
Pooled counts every building equally, so big fires dominate; mean-per-fire counts every fire equally.
