Structure Loss Pipeline · generalization

Outside California

Every out-of-sample number this project publishes is CAL FIRE DINS, and DINS is California only. The product is not. This page is everything the project knows about what happens beyond the state line — eleven states of leave-one-fire-out evidence, one national count source, and the two fires with per-structure truth sets nobody here drew.

The finding, in one sentence

The model still puts the burned buildings at the top of the list outside California; the single threshold that turns that list into a call is what fails. That is the careful form, and it is supported twice on independent evidence. The stronger version — "the model loses seven F1 points outside California" — is not supported: twenty-five fires cannot resolve it.

The obvious fix is a threshold fitted per fire. It works on a table of inspected structures and it does not transfer to the product, which is why it ships as an advisory second call rather than as the default.

01 · the national evidence

Sixty-six fires in eleven states

The only national evidence the project holds is the Carlson bundle's own leave-one-fire-out table: 66 Sentinel-2 fires, each scored by a model that never saw it. Two columns carry the whole argument — F1 at the bundle's fixed cut, and the F1 each fire would reach at its own best cut. The second needs that fire's labels, so it is a ceiling and not a proposal; the difference between them is the loss that belongs to the operating point rather than to the model.

02 · the fix, measured against a bar written first

Can a per-fire threshold fix it?

On a table of inspected structures, yes. On the product, no — and the gap between those two answers is the most important thing on this page.

If the loss belongs to the threshold rather than to the model, the fix is a rule that sets a threshold per fire from the model's own scores. Thirteen such rules were scored on the 66-fire national table with the fire as the unit, against a five-criterion promotion bar written into the project change log before the first fold was scored.

⚠️ No attribute of the fire reaches any of these rules. Size, perimeter, burn severity, region, state and year are never supplied, at fitting or at application; a test pins it by renaming a fire, moving it to another state and asserting the threshold does not budge. State appears in the table above only to report the result.

Prior adjustment — the textbook answer to a shifted base rate — is the worst rule here: it assumes the score distributions are unchanged on the new fire, which between chaparral and grass they are not, so it reads a distribution shift as a prior shift and collapses to calling nothing at all on four of the 66 fires.

And then it was applied to the product

The bar was answered honestly and the rule was adopted. Applying it to the fifteen modern fires showed something the validation table could not: the rule was validated on one population and applied to another.

The rule reads the fire's own score distribution and nothing else — that is what makes it label-free, and it is also what makes it move when the population underneath it changes. On a table of inspected structures where roughly half burned, the expected-F1 threshold sits near the fixed one. On a whole inventory where nine in a hundred burned, the same arithmetic pulls the threshold down and the model starts calling structures destroyed that nobody ever looked at.

That is why the per-fire threshold ships as an advisory second column and the fixed threshold stays the default. The best candidate is the one that ships: it takes the threshold from the near-perimeter zone, whose share destroyed is nearer the validation table's, and it is the one place on this page where a per-fire threshold clearly earns its keep — non-California mean-fire F1 goes 0.8986 → 0.9121, and the destroyed count goes from 0.810 of the inspected total to 0.891.

Source: outputs/operating_product/{decision.md,report.md,by_variant.csv}; the bar pre-registered in logs/CHANGELOG.md before it was scored.

Why no single rule can win

The states split into two shapes and the label-free signal that fixes one wrecks the other. A rule that fits nothing at all — call the top of the fire's own score distribution — takes Texas from F1 0.109 to 0.623 against a labelled ceiling of 0.622. The signal is genuinely there in the scores, with no labels and nothing about the fire. The same rule then costs California 13 points, because California's fires are already at their threshold. And California is 41 of the 66 fires, so a rule that helps eleven fires a lot and hurts forty a little can still post a positive average — which is exactly what the California, per-state and worst-fire criteria exist to catch.

This independently reproduces the generalization finding. The Carlson-era row measured, on 25 non-California fires none later than 2022, a mean-fire F1 of 0.742 at the fixed cut against 0.798 at each fire's own cut — a gap of 0.055, with the ranking intact. On a 2024 fire, in a state the Carlson sample barely contains, against a truth set that did not exist when that row was written, the same shape appears: AUC 0.985, and 0.848 → 0.945 at the fire's own threshold, a gap of 0.097. The ranking travels and the fixed threshold does not — measured twice, on independent evidence, two years apart. It is also the one place the per-fire threshold clearly helps: 0.848 → 0.882.

Three things this number is not. It is not a precision estimate for the inventory: 4,507 inventory points sit inside the assessed area and only 2,488 structures were observed, and Urban Search & Rescue teams searched streets, not every building — an unmatched inventory point is far more likely to be a real building nobody walked past than a false detection. It is not the whole fire: there are no Mescalero Apache Reservation records, so the Salt Fire side is absent entirely. And it is not an official assessment — the publisher says so explicitly.

Its low detection recall is structural rather than model quality: New Mexico has no cached Microsoft footprint layer, so the detector's prior band is USA Structures alone — a weaker prior than any California run has had — and Lincoln County's USA Structures extract carries a single IMAGE_DATE of 2017-05-26, seven years before the fire, so anything built after 2017 is missing from the footprint half of the inventory entirely.

The count-level check, and why it only earns its keep outside California

ICS-209 structures-destroyed counts are the only per-incident loss figure that exists nationally. The source is the Dataset of United States Incident Management Situation Reports from 2007 to 2026 (CC BY 4.0), 21,733 incidents after normalisation. Two alternatives were checked and rejected: ICS-209-PLUS stops at 2020 and cannot answer a 2023+ question, and WFIGS is a verified negative — its public layer publishes 97 fields and none of them names a destroyed, damaged or threatened structure count, which is checkable rather than remembered.

⚠️ In California the ICS-209 count is not independent of DINS. 12 of the 14 matched forward fires give a ratio of exactly 1.000, because CAL FIRE runs the damage inspection and files the situation report. It is a validity check on the plumbing and it passes; it is not external corroboration. Outside California, where nothing cross-checks it, the floor is demonstrably soft: Smokehouse Creek (TX, 2024) reports 0 structures lost against roughly 138 in the state's own investigation, because the fire left the situation report before any assessment landed. Read an ICS-209 count as a floor, and remember it includes sheds and detached garages Carlson never counted — pooled, it is 1.370× the DINS major count.

Source: outputs/ics209_check/{forward_fires.csv,summary.json}; docs/outside-ca-truth.md §1.

04 · the era question

And does it travel backwards in time?

The Sentinel-2 bundle starts in 2016, so its era table has one row. The 30-metre Landsat archive reaches back to 2002, and a separate model on it — 100 fires — answers the question instead:

Age costs about four points of F1, and it does not change the diagnosis — the threshold gap is the same size in both eras. This is the project's only pre-2016 evidence.

⚠️ What none of this evidence is. Twenty-five fires outside California, none after 2022, several states with one fire each. Every number on this page above the South Fork section is Carlson-era leave-one-fire-out, so it says nothing about a 2023+ out-of-state fire — which is exactly the case the product is for, and which exactly one truth set in the world covers.
