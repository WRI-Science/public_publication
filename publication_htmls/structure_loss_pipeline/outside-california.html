Structure Loss Pipeline · beyond California

Beyond California

This project estimates, building by building, which structures a wildfire destroyed, using free satellite imagery and public maps of building outlines. Almost all of the inspection data used to check that estimate comes from one state: after a California fire, CAL FIRE's Damage Inspection programme sends inspectors to every structure in the burn area and records whether it was destroyed, damaged or untouched. Nothing like it runs nationally. So the question this page answers is whether the accuracy measured in California survives the state line.

The answer, in one number

Outside California the model scores a mean-per-fire F1 of 0.689, against 0.779 inside it — in both cases averaged so that every fire counts once, whatever its size. F1 is a single score out of 1 that combines precision — what share of the buildings we call destroyed really were — with recall — what share of the real destruction we found; it is their harmonic mean, so it punishes both kinds of error. Every figure on this page is held out: the model that scores a fire is trained only on the other fires, so no fire is ever graded by a model that has already seen its answers.

The evidence behind that comparison is much wider than it was. The model is now fitted on 199 fires rather than 74, and the held-out table it is scored on covers 191 fires in eighteen states — 82 in California and 109 elsewhere. On that much evidence the gap is +0.0899 with a 95 % interval of [+0.0417, +0.1402], which excludes zero: the difference is real, and most of it is recall — outside California the model misses more of the destruction rather than inventing more of it. What travels almost intact is the ranking: the model still puts the burned buildings at the top of the list nearly as well, and a large part of what is lost belongs to the single fixed cut-off that turns that ranked list into a yes-or-no call.

01 · evidence across the country

A hundred and ninety-one fires in eighteen states

Outside California the ground truth is a published, hand-assembled record of buildings destroyed in United States wildfires between 2002 and 2022, compiled by Carlson and colleagues: one point on the map for every building that burned. A ground-truth point and one of our building locations are treated as the same building when they lie within 10 m of one another, matched one to one.

Scored that way, 191 fires and 782,566 buildings are available. Eighty-two of the fires are Californian; the remaining 109 are spread across the seventeen other states in the table below. Each fire is scored by a model trained only on the other fires. Three of the 109 hold too few buildings of one class to score at all and are counted in the fire total but not in the averages, which is why the averages rest on 188 fires.

Four measures appear. Precision is the share of our destroyed calls that were right and recall is the share of the real destruction we found; F1 is their harmonic mean, so a model cannot score well by being timid or by being reckless. The confusion matrix page shows the four cells of right and wrong calls these are built from. AUC measures ranking alone: the probability that a randomly chosen destroyed building scores higher than a randomly chosen surviving one, where 1 is a perfect ordering and 0.5 is a coin toss. Average precision summarises precision across every possible cut-off instead of one.

The model gives every building a probability, and a building is called destroyed when that probability is above 0.88 — one number, fixed in advance, used for every fire in every state and never tuned fire by fire. The last columns of the table ask what each fire would have scored at its own best cut-off. Finding that cut-off requires already knowing the answers for that fire, so it is a ceiling rather than something the product can do; the distance to it is the part of the loss that belongs to the cut-off rather than to the model. Numbers marked pooled count every building equally, so the largest fires dominate; mean-per-fire numbers count every fire equally. Both are given because they answer different questions.

Two shapes of failure, and only one of them is a cut-off problem

02 · the second, advisory call

A cut-off that adapts to each fire

Alongside the default call at the fixed cut-off, every fire also carries a second, advisory call whose cut-off is read from that fire's own scores. Here is what it changes, and why it is advisory rather than the default.

If the ranking survives the state line and only the cut-off fails, a rule that picks a cut-off for each fire should recover much of the loss. The advisory call does this without using any answers: it reads only the spread of the model's own scores on that fire. No attribute of the fire reaches it — not size, not perimeter, not burn severity, not region, state or year.

Reading the score distribution is also what makes it move when the population of buildings changes underneath it. The study area around a fire is the official perimeter plus a 2.4 km margin, and most buildings in it were never inspected and never at much risk. On a population that thin the same arithmetic pulls the cut-off well below 0.88 — on the fifteen most recent fires the advisory cut-off lands between 0.605 and 0.994 — and more buildings get called destroyed. The trade is visible, and it is why the fixed cut-off remains the default.

⚠️ Six of these assessments are the American Red Cross's, and they carry conditions. They were published by the American Red Cross — five by its National Headquarters Disaster Cycle Services Data & Systems Operations Branch, and the Alexander Mountain one by its National Headquarters Geospatial Technology unit — and the Red Cross states only that there are “no constraints on the data, for use by the Organization and Partners”. And the source is gone. The Red Cross deletes an assessment when the relief operation closes, and all six of these had already been deleted. They were recovered from an Internet Archive crawl. A reader who wants to check these nine rows can fetch the federal and county assessments; the six Red Cross ones can no longer be fetched from the publisher at all.

Reading the table: on seven of the nine assessments the model does what it does in California — precision above 0.93, recall between 0.74 and 1.00. Oregon 2025 is the strongest external result this project has, and it is the first on a 2025 fire outside California. Minnesota and Oklahoma are the two failures, and they fail in the same direction and for the same reason, which the next section sets out. One caution on the smallest rows: the Colorado 2024 result is ten pairs. It is in the table because it is a state and a fire year this project previously had nothing for; it is not evidence of anything.

One fire end to end: South Fork, New Mexico, June 2024

The area scored is the area the teams actually walked — the hull of their assessment, 39.7 km². An assessment is not a census: scoring over the whole fire against 2,488 observations would turn every building nobody inspected into a wrong call by construction. The before-fire imagery is aerial photography from the US Department of Agriculture's National Agriculture Imagery Program, which photographs the continental United States at roughly 60 cm per pixel every two to three years; 2022 is the newest year before this fire. The assessment's damage categories are converted to destroyed or not by the same rule the project applies to California's inspection classes — more than 50 % damage counts as destroyed, 25 % or less as untouched — because both schemes are the same national percentage scale under different words.

Reading it: at the fixed cut-off the model is almost never wrong when it calls a building destroyed (precision 0.982) but misses a quarter of the destruction (recall 0.739), and the ranking is good enough — AUC 0.986 — that a better-placed cut-off would have caught most of what was missed: F1 rises from 0.843 to 0.946 at this fire's own best cut-off, which is 0.18. Over the fire's whole study area the model enumerates 9,568 buildings and calls 732 of them destroyed.

Source: ics209_check/outside_ca/ruidoso_southfork_2024/metrics.json and the fire's own product summary on the data share, data date 2026-09-15.

Three things this result is not. It is not a precision estimate for the whole building inventory: 4,507 of our building locations sit inside the assessed area while only 2,488 buildings were observed, and the search teams worked along streets rather than visiting every structure, so an unmatched building of ours is far more likely to be a real building nobody walked past than a false detection. It is not the whole fire: there are no records for the Mescalero Apache Reservation, so the Salt Fire side is absent. And it is not an official assessment — the publisher says so explicitly.

One limitation here is about the building list rather than the damage model. Our building detector — a neural network trained on aerial photography that finds buildings the public outline databases miss — found 0.711 of the assessed buildings within 10 m, lower than a California run would give. New Mexico has no cached copy of the Microsoft building-outline layer, leaving the national USA Structures layer as the only public starting list, and the county's extract of it carries a single capture date of 2017-05-26, seven years before the fire. Anything built after 2017 is missing from that half of the inventory entirely.

The one figure that exists nationally: reported counts

Beyond per-building inspections, one loss figure is filed for almost every incident in the country: the number of structures destroyed, reported by incident commanders in their situation reports. The source used here is the Dataset of United States Incident Management Situation Reports from 2007 to 2026 (CC BY 4.0), 21,733 incidents after normalisation. It is a count, not a per-building record, so it can only check whether our totals are in the right place.

The widest version of that check now runs over 75 fires in 21 states, all scored on the current model: 97,884 buildings enumerated, 3,179 called destroyed against 4,243 reported, a pooled ratio of 0.749. Dropping California — where the two figures are not independent — leaves 70 fires in 20 states and a ratio of 0.738. Forty-six of the 75 fires land within a factor of two of the reported count in each direction; twenty come in below half of it and nine above twice it.

Source: conus_run/count_check.json on the data share, data date 2026-09-15.

⚠️ Treat a reported count as a floor, and never as an independent check inside California. In California the reported count is not independent of the inspection data: CAL FIRE both runs the damage inspection and files the situation report, and on the fires where both exist the two agree to within half a percent. That is a check on the plumbing, and it passes. Outside California, where nothing cross-checks it, the figure is demonstrably soft: Smokehouse Creek (TX, 2024) reports 0 structures lost against roughly 138 in the state's own investigation, because the fire left the situation reports before any assessment landed. Reported counts also include sheds and detached garages that the historical record leaves out — pooled, they come to 1.370× the count of major damage recorded by California's inspectors, so a ratio below one is the direction to expect.

Source: ics209_check/summary.json on the data share for the two truth sources' relationship to each other, data date 2026-09-12.

04 · where it fails, and why

Two failures, one calendar

Minnesota scores zero and Oklahoma scores 0.349. Both are real failures of the shipped call, and in both cases the cause has been measured rather than guessed. It is not the fuel, not the imagery and not a bug.

The damage model's strongest single input is the change in a burn index between a composite of the images taken before the fire and a composite of the images taken after it. On a fire the model reads correctly, that index falls sharply at a destroyed building. On the Minnesota fires it rises — at every building, destroyed or not.

The reason is the season. Camp House ignited on 11 May 2025 at 47.2 °N, so the before-fire composite falls in late April, in northern Minnesota before spring green-up, and the after-fire composite falls in July at full canopy. The landscape greens up by more than the fire darkens it. The difference between destroyed and surviving buildings survives — it is −0.162, larger than on any fire the project has previously used as an example of a weak signal — which is why the model still ranks Minnesota's buildings well, at an AUC of 0.890. What has moved is the absolute level, by about +0.40, into the region the model has only ever seen when nothing happened. So every probability is low — the highest of the 508 buildings scored is 0.547, against a cut-off of 0.88 — and F1 is 0.000.

The control that settles it. A fire in the same state, in the same northern forest, is already in the training set: Greenwood, Minnesota, 2021. Its before-fire composite falls in July, and its burn-index drop at destroyed buildings is −0.480 — one of the strongest signals in the whole training set. Same state, same fuel, same sensor, same pipeline, same features. A July before-image gives a textbook result; a late-April one flips the sign. The fuel is fine; the calendar is not.

It is a gap in the training set, and it can be counted. Of the 199 fires the model is fitted on, 28 have a before-fire composite in spring — and not one of them is north of 40 °N. A northern-forest fire in spring is a combination the model has never seen. Oklahoma is the same failure, one degree less severe: its burn-index change is positive too, but the training set does hold southern-plains spring fires, so its destroyed buildings reach a mean probability of 0.47 rather than Minnesota's 0.02 — enough to rank them almost perfectly and not enough to clear a cut-off of 0.88. At its own best cut-off, 0.05, the same scores give an F1 of 0.898.

⚠️ This is not an argument for lowering the cut-off. A cut-off of 0.05 that rescues Oklahoma would ruin precision everywhere else. The cut-off is fitted on the inspected California incidents and is not moved by these results. The remedy this points at is a before-fire composite matched to the same season as the after-fire one — taking the previous year if necessary — rather than simply the 60 days before ignition. That is a change to how the imagery is chosen, it has a measured mechanism behind it, and it has not been built or tested. Nothing on this site depends on it.

Source: ics209_check/outside_ca/ddac_mn_north_2025/ and ddac_ksok_2025/ on the data share, with the by-month and by-fire comparison in the same run, data date 2026-09-15.

What this pair is worth to a reader. Kansas and Oklahoma scored 0.349 at the shipped cut-off and 0.898 at their own. That is the honest version of what a fixed threshold does when it travels: the model's ordering of the buildings is close to right, and a single number chosen on Californian fires is in the wrong place for a grass fire on the southern plains in March. Both figures belong together; quoting either alone misrepresents the product.

05 · the building list, outside California

How many of the buildings do we even find?

Every destroyed call starts from a list of buildings that stood before the fire. If a building is not on that list, no damage model can score it. Until now that list had only ever been measured against Californian inspections; here it is against every assessment the project holds elsewhere.

The building list is the union of two public databases — the FEMA / Oak Ridge USA Structures layer and Microsoft's US Building Footprints — plus buildings found by this project's own detector. The public databases are the baseline, and the detector is judged on what it adds to them. The denominator is the buildings somebody chose to inspect, inside the area they inspected, because over a whole fire every uninspected building would count as a false alarm by construction.

⚠️ A recall number can be about the truth set rather than about us

An assessment records a place as well as a damage class, and some record it badly: a parcel centroid, a mailing address, a row on a neighbouring fire. If the published coordinate is 60 m from the building, no detector can match it at 10 m, and the resulting figure measures the assessment rather than the building list. Every assessment therefore carries the median distance from its points to the nearest building this project holds, and the headline pool above keeps only those whose median falls inside the 10 m match radius. Four assessments fail that test — in Oregon, Minnesota and Utah — and are named and reported in full rather than dropped quietly.

The controlled demonstration, and it is unanswerable. Two independent assessments cover the same Oregon fire in 2025 and are scored against the same building list on the same day. The Red Cross points sit a median 4.9 m from one of our buildings and recall 72.6 %; the Oregon State Fire Marshal's points sit a median 11.5 m away and recall 35.6 %. Same detector, same fire, same day — a 37-point swing that is entirely the truth set's coordinates.

That is why no recall figure taken from an outside assessment is quoted anywhere on this site without the coordinate quality beside it.

06 · how much two experts agree

Two independent surveys of one fire

Every accuracy figure on this site treats one assessment as the truth. That assumption has a price, and on one fire it can be measured: two expert teams walked the same ground and recorded the same buildings.

On the Airport Fire in California in 2024, an American Red Cross assessment and CAL FIRE's own damage inspection both cover a shared area of 6.21 km². Restricting to the buildings both teams classified, and matching one to one at 10 m, gives 48 buildings that both call either destroyed or surviving.

Two expert assessments of the same 48 buildings, Airport Fire, California, 2024
Fire service says destroyedFire service says surviving
Red Cross says destroyed392
Red Cross says surviving07

They agree on 95.8 % of the 48, at a Cohen's κ of 0.8505 — κ being agreement corrected for the agreement two raters would reach by chance alone, where 1 is perfect and 0 is chance. The two disagreements both run the same way: the Red Cross calls a building destroyed where the fire service records no damage. Not one goes the other way.

Why this matters for every other number on this site: it puts a floor under the error rate. If two expert teams disagree on 4 % of the buildings they both visit, no model scored against either of them can be shown to be right more often than that. The equivalent measurement on 43,929 Californian buildings, using two published expert label sets, is 2.15 %. Forty-eight buildings is a small sample and is quoted as one.

Source: ics209_check/inter_rater/airport_2024/metrics.json on the data share, data date 2026-09-15. It is a label-noise measurement and not an accuracy measurement: neither team is the reference.

⚠️ The second pair agrees perfectly and that tells you nothing. The two Oregon assessments of the 2025 Rowena Fire co-locate on 45 buildings and agree on all 45 — but every one of the 45 is a building both teams call destroyed. There is no surviving building in both sets, so chance agreement is also 100 % and κ is undefined. A perfect agreement rate that carries no information is reported as exactly that, and not as a headline.

07 · older fires

Does it work on fires from before 2016?

The main model reads Sentinel-2, a pair of free European Space Agency satellites that photograph every point on Earth about every five days at 10 m per pixel — and that archive begins in 2016. To ask whether the method also works on older fires, a separate model was built on the Landsat archive, whose 30 m imagery reaches back to 2002, and run over 100 fires.
