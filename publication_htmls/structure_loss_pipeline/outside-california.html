Structure Loss Pipeline · beyond California

Beyond California

This project estimates, building by building, which structures a wildfire destroyed, using free satellite imagery and public maps of building outlines. Almost all of the inspection data used to check that estimate comes from one state: after a California fire, CAL FIRE's Damage Inspection programme sends inspectors to every structure in the burn area and records whether it was destroyed, damaged or untouched. Nothing like it runs nationally. So the question this page answers is whether the accuracy measured in California survives the state line.

The answer, in one number

Outside California the model scores an F1 of 0.734, against 0.8072 inside it — in both cases averaged so that every fire counts once, whatever its size. F1 is a single score out of 1 that combines precision — what share of the buildings we call destroyed really were — with recall — what share of the real destruction we found; it is their harmonic mean, so it punishes both kinds of error. Every figure on this page is held out: the model that scores a fire is trained only on the other fires, so no fire is ever graded by a model that has already seen its answers.

The gap of 0.0726 is smaller than the uncertainty behind it. With only 25 fires outside California to go on, the 95 % interval on that gap runs from −0.0110 to +0.1630 and still includes zero. What can be said with confidence is narrower and more useful: the model ranks buildings just as well outside California — it puts the burned ones at the top of the list — and what degrades is the single fixed cut-off that turns that ranked list into a yes-or-no call.

01 · evidence across the country

Sixty-six fires in eleven states

Outside California the ground truth is a published, hand-assembled record of buildings destroyed in United States wildfires between 2002 and 2022, compiled by Carlson and colleagues: one point on the map for every building that burned. A ground-truth point and one of our building locations are treated as the same building when they lie within 10 m of one another, matched one to one.

Scored that way, 66 fires and 582,554 buildings are available. Forty-one of the fires are Californian; the remaining 25 are spread across the other states in the table below. Each fire is scored by a model trained only on the other fires.

Four measures appear. Precision is the share of our destroyed calls that were right and recall is the share of the real destruction we found; F1 is their harmonic mean, so a model cannot score well by being timid or by being reckless. The confusion matrix page shows the four cells of right and wrong calls these are built from. AUC measures ranking alone: the probability that a randomly chosen destroyed building scores higher than a randomly chosen surviving one, where 1 is a perfect ordering and 0.5 is a coin toss. Average precision summarises precision across every possible cut-off instead of one.

The model gives every building a probability, and a building is called destroyed when that probability is above 0.85 — one number, fixed in advance, used for every fire in every state and never tuned fire by fire. The last columns of the table ask what each fire would have scored at its own best cut-off. Finding that cut-off requires already knowing the answers for that fire, so it is a ceiling rather than something the product can do; the distance to it is the part of the loss that belongs to the cut-off rather than to the model. Numbers marked pooled count every building equally, so the largest fires dominate; mean-per-fire numbers count every fire equally. Both are given because they answer different questions.

How much of the difference is real?

California and everywhere else were compared directly, each difference carrying a 95 % interval computed by resampling whole fires 2,000 times. That interval says how much the number would move if the project happened to cover a different set of fires, which is the uncertainty that matters when the sample is 25 fires.

The evidence outside California is not yet sharp enough to separate today's performance from the best a cut-off rule could ever deliver there.

Two shapes of failure, and only one of them is a cut-off problem

02 · the second, advisory call

A cut-off that adapts to each fire

Alongside the default call at the fixed cut-off, every fire also carries a second, advisory call whose cut-off is read from that fire's own scores. Here is what it changes, and why it is advisory rather than the default.

If the ranking survives the state line and only the cut-off fails, a rule that picks a cut-off for each fire should recover much of the loss. The advisory call does this without using any answers: it reads only the spread of the model's own scores on that fire. No attribute of the fire reaches it — not size, not perimeter, not burn severity, not region, state or year.

Reading the score distribution is also what makes it move when the population of buildings changes underneath it. The study area around a fire is the official perimeter plus a 2.4 km margin, and most buildings in it were never inspected and never at much risk: across a whole study area only about 0.09 of buildings are destroyed. On a population that thin the same arithmetic pulls the cut-off below 0.85, and more buildings get called destroyed. The trade is visible, and it is why the fixed cut-off remains the default.

Buildings inside the perimeter or within 100 m of it are the directly exposed ones, and a far larger share of them burn than in the thin ring from 100 m out to 2.4 km. The advisory call that ships takes its cut-off from that directly exposed zone, and that is the one place where a per-fire cut-off clearly earns its keep: outside California, mean-per-fire F1 goes 0.8986 → 0.9121, and the count of buildings called destroyed rises from 0.810 of the inspected total to 0.891.

03 · independent ground truth

A handful of one-off post-fire surveys record damage building by building outside California. They are the only way to check the model against ground truth that no part of this project assembled.

One fire end to end: South Fork, New Mexico, June 2024

The area scored is the area the teams actually walked — the hull of their assessment, 39.7 km². An assessment is not a census: scoring over the whole 17,569-acre fire against 2,488 observations would turn every building nobody inspected into a wrong call by construction. The before-fire imagery is aerial photography from the US Department of Agriculture's National Agriculture Imagery Program, which photographs the continental United States at roughly 60 cm per pixel every two to three years; 2022 is the newest year before this fire. The assessment's damage categories are converted to destroyed or not by the same rule the project applies to California's inspection classes — more than 50 % damage counts as destroyed, 25 % or less as untouched — because both schemes are the same national percentage scale under different words.

The same pattern appears on evidence of a completely different kind. Across the 25 fires outside California in the historical record, mean-per-fire F1 is 0.734 at the fixed cut-off against a ceiling of 0.800 at each fire's own. On South Fork — a fire from 2024, in a state the historical record barely touches, measured against an inspection set collected on the ground by somebody else — the shape repeats: AUC 0.985 and 0.848 → 0.945 at the fire's own cut-off, a gap of 0.097. The ranking travels; the fixed cut-off does not. It is also the clearest case for the advisory call, which lifts F1 from 0.848 to 0.882.

Three things this result is not. It is not a precision estimate for the whole building inventory: 4,507 of our building locations sit inside the assessed area while only 2,488 buildings were observed, and the search teams worked along streets rather than visiting every structure, so an unmatched building of ours is far more likely to be a real building nobody walked past than a false detection. It is not the whole fire: there are no records for the Mescalero Apache Reservation, so the Salt Fire side is absent. And it is not an official assessment — the publisher says so explicitly.

One limitation here is about the building list rather than the damage model. Our building detector — a neural network trained on aerial photography that finds buildings the public outline databases miss — found 0.661 of the assessed buildings within 10 m and 0.760 within 20 m, lower than a California run would give. New Mexico has no cached copy of the Microsoft building-outline layer, leaving the national USA Structures layer as the only public starting list, and Lincoln County's extract of it carries a single capture date of 2017-05-26, seven years before the fire. Anything built after 2017 is missing from that half of the inventory entirely.

⚠️ The outer ring of this fire cannot be scored. Between 100 m and 2.4 km from the perimeter there are 119 matched buildings and 0 destroyed ones. That is what the outer ring normally looks like, and it is why a cut-off taken from the directly exposed zone is the one that means anything here.

The one figure that exists nationally: reported counts

Beyond per-building inspections, one loss figure is filed for almost every incident in the country: the number of structures destroyed, reported by incident commanders in their situation reports. The source used here is the Dataset of United States Incident Management Situation Reports from 2007 to 2026 (CC BY 4.0), 21,733 incidents after normalisation. It is a count, not a per-building record, so it can only check whether our totals are in the right place.

⚠️ Treat a reported count as a floor, and never as an independent check inside California. In California the reported count is not independent of the inspection data: 12 of the 14 fires that match give a ratio of exactly 1.000, because CAL FIRE both runs the damage inspection and files the situation report. That is a check on the plumbing, and it passes. Outside California, where nothing cross-checks it, the figure is demonstrably soft: Smokehouse Creek (TX, 2024) reports 0 structures lost against roughly 138 in the state's own investigation, because the fire left the situation reports before any assessment landed. Reported counts also include sheds and detached garages that the historical record leaves out — pooled, they come to 1.370× the count of major damage recorded by California's inspectors.

Source: ics209_check/forward_fires.csv and ics209_check/summary.json on the data share.

04 · older fires

Does it work on fires from before 2016?

The main model reads Sentinel-2, a pair of free European Space Agency satellites that photograph every point on Earth about every five days at 10 m per pixel — and that archive begins in 2016. To ask whether the method also works on older fires, a separate model was built on the Landsat archive, whose 30 m imagery reaches back to 2002, and run over 100 fires.

One case — South Fork in 2024 — is the only recent out-of-state fire anywhere with per-building ground truth to check against, and a recent out-of-state fire is exactly what the product is for.
