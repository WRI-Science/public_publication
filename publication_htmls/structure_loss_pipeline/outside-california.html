Structure Loss Pipeline · generalization

Outside California

Every out-of-sample number this project publishes is CAL FIRE DINS, and DINS is California only. The product is not. This page is everything the project knows about what happens beyond the state line — eleven states of leave-one-fire-out evidence, one national count source, and the two fires with per-structure truth sets nobody here drew.

The finding, in one sentence, dated 12 September 2026

The ranking travels and is measurably a little worse outside California; the fixed cut fails badly on specific fires. That is the careful form, it is supported twice on independent evidence, and the stronger version — "the model loses seven F1 points outside California" — is not supported: twenty-five fires cannot resolve it.

The fix for the threshold half was adopted, applied, and then did not transfer. A label-free per-fire cut rule cleared a five-criterion bar written down before it was scored, twice over. Applied to the product it costs −0.0352 mean-fire F1 and gains the destroyed count, because the fold table it was validated on and the inventory it is applied to are different populations. Since v0.4.0 it therefore ships as a second column beside the fixed cut, not instead of it — the section below is the whole story, and it is the most important caveat on this page.

01 · the national evidence

Sixty-six fires in eleven states

The only national evidence the project holds is the Carlson bundle's own leave-one-fire-out table: 66 Sentinel-2 fires, each scored by a model that never saw it. Two columns carry the whole argument — F1 at the bundle's fixed cut, and the F1 each fire would reach at its own best cut. The second needs that fire's labels, so it is a ceiling and not a proposal; the difference between them is the loss that belongs to the operating point rather than to the model.

02 · the fix, measured against a bar written first

Can a label-free cut rule fix it?

If the loss belongs to the threshold, the fix — if there is one — is a rule that reads only the model's own scores. Thirteen such rules were scored leave-one-fire-out on the same 66-fire table with the fire as the unit: fixed cuts, the best-F1 cut refitted on the training fires' pooled scores, the cut with the best mean per-fire F1, the median of the training fires' own cuts, predicted-base-rate → cut (two forms), the plug-in expected-F1 cut on calibrated probabilities (two forms), Saerens–Latinne–Decaestecker EM prior adjustment (two forms) and two score-quantile rules.

⚠️ No fire attribute reaches any rule. Size, perimeter, dNBR, region, state and year are never supplied, at fitting or at application (goal-no-fire-covariates); a test pins it by renaming a fire and moving it to another state and asserting the cut does not budge. State appears in the table above only to report the result.

The candidates, the protocol and the promotion bar were pre-registered in the change log on 2026-09-10 at 17:50 PT, before the first fold was scored. On the pre-fix tables nothing cleared it and the best rule missed the gain criterion by 0.0002. On the corrected tables, with the Carlson half fully nested — for every held-out fire f and every other fire g, a probability from a model trained on the 64 fires that are neither, 66 outer + 2,145 pair fits = 2,211 — one rule clears all five. That pass is real and nothing below contradicts it. What it does not do is transfer to the product, and why not is the next section.

Prior adjustment (Saerens–Latinne–Decaestecker EM), the textbook answer to a shifted base rate, is the worst rule here: it assumes the class-conditional score densities are unchanged on the new fire, which between chaparral and grass they are not, so it reads a density shift as a prior shift and collapses to calling nothing on four of the 66 fires.

Two internal checks say the nesting actually happened. Every rule that fits nothing — fixed@0.84, fixed@0.87, fixed@0.90 and quantile_predicted_rate, which reads only the held-out fire's own mean score — is bit-identical between the nested and unnested runs. Only rules that fit something on the other fires' scores move at all, which is exactly and only what nesting can change. And the outer half reproduces the published destroyed_prob to a maximum absolute difference of 3.0 × 10⁻⁸ on 100.00 % of the 582,554 rows, so this is the same bundle refitted, not a different experiment.

The assumption behind the last remaining caveat was wrong in sign, and it was worth checking rather than believing. Removing the leak was expected to shrink the adaptive rules' advantage — that is the safe direction for a rejection and the unsafe one for an adoption. It grew it, by +0.0016 mean-fire F1 for the winning rule. The effect is small either way; every fitted rule moves by less than ±0.0033. The honest summary is that nesting was not what was holding this result up.

What remains is the whole of the case against adopting, and it is not a caveat a bigger computer can remove: a narrow pass on 66 fires, 25 of them outside California, with Texas, Kansas, Florida, New Mexico and Utah represented by a single fire each. Under the rule, Texas goes 0.167 → 0.462 and Oklahoma 0.402 → 0.524 at the 0.90 baseline, and California gains 0.005 rather than paying for it.

And then it was applied to the product

The bar was answered honestly and the owner adopted the rule. Applying it to the fifteen forward fires showed something the fold table could not: the rule was validated on one population and applied to another.

The rule reads the fire's own score distribution and nothing else — that is what makes it label-free and keeps it inside the covariate rule, and it is also what makes it move when the population underneath it changes. On a table of inspected structures where roughly half burned, the expected-F1 cut sits near the shipped one. On a whole inventory where nine in a hundred burned, the same arithmetic drags the cut down — to 0.158 on Coches 2 — and the model begins calling structures destroyed that nobody ever looked at.

⚠️ The pre-registered pass stands on the population it was measured on. Nothing about the bar was wrong: it answered a question about labelled folds and was read as an answer about the product. The open row names the next test rather than closing the question — the same five criteria evaluated on the product population, a Direct-zone-only variant whose base rate is nearer the fold table's and a whole-inventory variant, each pre-registered before it is run, as the original bar was (open-adaptive-rule-population-shift).

⚠️ That question has now been answered, and the answer is no. Roadmap 14 re-ran the same five criteria at the same thresholds on the product's own population — three populations, five candidate variants, the bar pre-registered again before anything was scored — and no candidate clears it. Nothing changes; the fixed cut stays the default. The best of them is the Direct-zone-scoped cut, which takes the rule's threshold from the Direct zone (base rate nearer the fold table's) and applies it to every row: mean-fire F1 −0.0186 [−0.0336, −0.0070] against the fixed cut, a destroyed-count ratio of 0.891, realised cuts 0.601–0.792 instead of 0.158–0.792 — and it gains outside California, 0.8986 → 0.9121 mean-fire F1, which is the one place on this page it matters most. It is being swapped in as the advisory column in a v0.4.1 that has not been cut; until it is, the advisory column on this site is the whole-inventory variant.

One check is worth keeping. Re-fitting the isotonic map leave-one-fire-out — the leak a reader would reasonably suspect — produces a variant that is bit-identical on 204,797 of 204,797 inventory rows, because the product already reads each held-out fire's own fold calibration. What the adaptive call does buy is the count. Predicted destroyed over the fifteen fires' whole inventories goes 14,504 → 17,362 against 17,902 inspected as destroyed — a ratio of 0.810 → 0.970. The release notes reduce the choice to one line: use damage_class to classify buildings; use damage_class_adaptive if you are counting them. Every headline on this site is the fixed cut.

⚠️ Outside California the adaptive call is the better of the two, on the one post-2022 non-California truth set that exists. South Fork NM 2024 goes F1 0.8484 → 0.8825. On Marshall's two independent assessments it splits — Boulder County 0.9705 → 0.9727, FEMA MAC 0.9273 → 0.8901. Three assessments over two fires is not a distribution, and this does not overturn the paragraph above. It does point the same way as everything else on this page: the ranking travels, and it is the threshold that does not.

A rule that fits nothing at all — call the top mean(prob) of the fire's own scores — reaches Texas F1 0.623 against a labelled ceiling of 0.622, from 0.109. The signal is genuinely there in the score distribution, with no labels and no fire attribute. It then costs California 13 points, because California's 41 fires are already at their cut. California is 41 of 66 fires, so a rule that helps eleven fires a lot and hurts forty a little can still post a positive mean — which is exactly what the California, per-state and worst-fire criteria exist to catch.

This independently reproduces the generalization finding. The Carlson-era row measured, on 25 non-California fires none later than 2022, a mean-fire F1 of 0.742 at the fixed cut against 0.798 at each fire's own cut — a gap of 0.055, with the ranking intact. On a 2024 fire, in a state the Carlson sample barely contains, against a truth set that did not exist when that row was written, the same shape appears: AUC 0.985, and 0.848 → 0.945 at the fire's own cut, a gap of 0.097 — wider at v0.4.0's cut of 0.85 than it was at v0.3.0's 0.87. The ranking travels and the fixed cut does not — measured twice, on independent evidence, two years apart. It is also the one place the per-fire cut clearly helps: 0.848 → 0.882.

Three things this number is not. It is not a precision estimate for the inventory: 4,507 inventory points sit inside the assessed area and only 2,488 structures were observed, and Urban Search & Rescue teams searched streets, not every building — an unmatched inventory point is far more likely to be a real building nobody walked past than a false detection. It is not the whole fire: there are no Mescalero Apache Reservation records, so the Salt Fire side is absent entirely. And it is not an official assessment — the publisher says so explicitly.

Its low detection recall is structural rather than model quality: New Mexico has no cached Microsoft footprint layer, so the detector's prior band is USA Structures alone — a weaker prior than any California run has had — and Lincoln County's USA Structures extract carries a single IMAGE_DATE of 2017-05-26, seven years before the fire, so anything built after 2017 is missing from the footprint half of the inventory entirely.

The count-level check, and why it only earns its keep outside California

ICS-209 structures-destroyed counts are the only per-incident loss figure that exists nationally. The source is the Dataset of United States Incident Management Situation Reports from 2007 to 2026 (CC BY 4.0), 21,733 incidents after normalisation. Two alternatives were checked and rejected: ICS-209-PLUS stops at 2020 and cannot answer a 2023+ question, and WFIGS is a verified negative — its public layer publishes 97 fields and none of them names a destroyed, damaged or threatened structure count, which is checkable rather than remembered.

⚠️ In California the ICS-209 count is not independent of DINS. 12 of the 14 matched forward fires give a ratio of exactly 1.000, because CAL FIRE runs the damage inspection and files the situation report. It is a validity check on the plumbing and it passes; it is not external corroboration. Outside California, where nothing cross-checks it, the floor is demonstrably soft: Smokehouse Creek (TX, 2024) reports 0 structures lost against roughly 138 in the state's own investigation, because the fire left the situation report before any assessment landed. Read an ICS-209 count as a floor, and remember it includes sheds and detached garages Carlson never counted — pooled, it is 1.370× the DINS major count.

⚠️ What none of this evidence is. Twenty-five fires outside California, none after 2022, several states with one fire each. Every number on this page above the South Fork section is Carlson-era leave-one-fire-out, so it says nothing about a 2023+ out-of-state fire — which is exactly the case the product is for, and which exactly one truth set in the world covers.
