Right and wrong calls

Each dot is a building in the damage record for that fire, coloured by whether our first damage call agreed with it. The call reads Sentinel-2 satellite images from before and after the fire and says “destroyed” when its probability is 0.88 or higher. Every call here was made leave-one-fire-out: by a model trained without that fire, so it is a fair test.

    How to read this map

    What is being compared. For the three CAL FIRE fires (Park, Eaton and Bridge), the record is the door-to-door damage inspection: a building counts as destroyed if inspectors graded it “Destroyed (>50%)”; every lower grade counts as not destroyed. For the Marshall and Carr fires, the record is the reference dataset of Carlson et al. (2025), which marks each building destroyed or not.

    What the probability is. The number on each dot is the average of four training runs of the same model, each trained on all the other fires in our 199-fire training set with this fire left out. That average is how our published accuracy figures are computed; a building is called destroyed when it reaches 0.88.

    Where the reasons come from. The short notes beside each wrong call are read off the data, not guessed: the probability and how close it is to the 0.88 cut; the inspector’s grade when it was damaged but not destroyed; whether the inspection lists it as a minor structure such as a shed; how many of its neighbours within 50 m were destroyed (our satellite pixels are 10 m across, so a surviving house among burned ones can look burned, and the reverse); how green the ground around it was before the fire (NDVI of 0.5 or more suggests tree or shrub cover that can hide a roof); and whether few cloud-free satellite images were available (two or fewer before or after). When none of these applies, only the probability is given. These are clues, not proven causes.

    What is not shown. Buildings outside the damage record for each fire are not drawn; this map is only for checking calls against the record.
