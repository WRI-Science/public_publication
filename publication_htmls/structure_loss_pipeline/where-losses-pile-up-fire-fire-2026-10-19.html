Urban conflagrations lean their losses toward the most crowded buildings, but many other fires do too

Each line is one fire. Sort the buildings the fire reached from the most isolated (fewest neighbours within 100 m) to the most crowded and add up as you go: the line shows how much of the fire's losses had been reached at each step. A line on the dashed diagonal means losses fell evenly on sparse and crowded buildings; a line that sags below it means they piled up at the crowded end. In our 2002–26 record all 10 urban conflagrations are drawn (orange) with the 145 other fires that destroyed 50 or more (blue). A typical urban conflagration had 25% of its losses in its most crowded fifth of buildings, against 17% for other big fires and 14% for fires with 10–49 destroyed (20% would mean losses simply follow the buildings). What it suggests: urban conflagrations do lean to the crowded end, but many other fires look the same, and some urban conflagrations (Woolsey, Glass) do not lean at all. How this panel was checked
  • All three records; fires with fewer than 10 destroyed left out (each loss would move the line 10 % or more).
  • Groups use the paper's definition (≥ 50 destroyed linked at 30 m), on the MTBS + 100 m outline.
  • Part of the difference is built in: an urban conflagration is defined by a crowded patch of losses, so its line is expected to sag.
  • Mobile-home rule fixed before the curves were drawn: 'substantial' = 5 points or a 0.05 gap; neither was reached in any record.

As a test, the curve picks out urban conflagrations only moderately well; the size of the largest block does far better

Can the curve tell an urban conflagration from another big fire? We fixed the test before drawing any curve. Take one urban conflagration and one other fire that destroyed 50 or more: the share of losses in the most crowded fifth picks the urban conflagration 72% of the time in our record (67% in Carlson's), with ranges that reach down to a coin toss. The size of the largest block of close buildings before the fire picks it 97% of the time (97%). Adding the curve to block size does not help. What it suggests: the curve's shape is a symptom of an urban conflagration, not a way to spot one; what the fire had to burn into (a big crowded block) says far more. Nor do the curves fall into a separate kind of fire: they form one continuum.Pre-registered (2026-10-18 21:42 UTC). Urban conflagrations vs other ≥ 50 fires: our record 10 vs 145, Carlson 8 vs 127. AUC(S2) 0.717 [0.539, 0.857]; AUC(−S1) 0.707; AUC(block) 0.974 [0.947, 0.994]; difference S1 − block -0.268 [-0.473, -0.110]. Logit log2(1 + block) + S1 vs block: likelihood ratio 2.50, p = 0.11 (Carlson 1.63, p = 0.20); leave-one-fire-out AUC 0.968 → 0.966 (Carlson 0.958 → 0.953). Verdict by the pre-set bar (p < 0.05 and a rise in leave-one-out AUC): no evidence the shape adds anything beyond block size. CAL FIRE 2023–26: 2 urban conflagrations, described only. Exploratory mixture on S1: BIC picks 1 component in our record, 2 in Carlson (Ashman D 0.77, below the 2 needed for separated groups): no separate group of fires. Circularity: the group is defined by a dense patch of losses.
