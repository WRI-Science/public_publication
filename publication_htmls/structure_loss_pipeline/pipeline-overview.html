Structure Loss Pipeline · the method in full

The Method in Full

This project estimates, building by building, which structures a wildfire destroyed, using only free satellite imagery and public maps of building outlines. It publishes a per-building record for every fire it covers, not a single loss total. This page is the complete technical account: how the list of buildings that stood before a fire is assembled, how the destroyed call is made, and how accurate each half is when measured against every independent record of the same buildings. The number to carry away is the first one below — on fires the model was never trained on, the destroyed call reaches a mean per-fire F1 of 0.783 against on-the-ground fire-service inspections.

Five things to know before the numbers start

  • The dataset is one layer, 377 fires, 2,069,752 rows. It carries the published historical record of United States wildfire building loss for 2002–2022 verbatim, sixteen fires of 2024–2025 measured by this method, and seventeen fires the historical record already covers that this method has also enumerated independently. Where a fire is described twice, on one schema, the two descriptions must never be summed.
  • Every fire gets two estimates. The primary estimate is made about two and a half months after a fire starts, from before-and-after satellite images, and exists for every building in every fire covered. The refined estimate is made about a year later, once a second post-fire image window and a year-over-year satellite summary of the landscape exist; it is more accurate, and it exists for most buildings in most fires but not all. Both are current products with different delivery times.
  • Every accuracy figure is measured out of sample. The model that scores a fire is trained only on the other fires, so no fire is ever graded by a model that saw its answers.
  • Our building detector is an addition to the public footprint databases, not a replacement. Measured against fire-service inspections it recovers 3.5 % of the buildings the public databases miss; measured against a complete building census it is worth about four times that, and most on fires whose footprint data is too recent to describe the pre-fire landscape.
  • Almost all of the signal sits close to the fire. Buildings between 100 m and 2.4 km outside a fire perimeter are very rarely destroyed, and every headline number here is carried by the buildings inside the perimeter or within 100 m of it.

01 · the product

What the dataset is, and who it is for

The published historical record of wildfire building loss in the United States is a hand-assembled census of what fires destroyed in the conterminous United States between 2002 and 2022 — 361 fires, 1,770,939 rows, one point per destroyed building, compiled by Carlson and colleagues and published through the US Geological Survey. It is the reference layer for anyone modelling wildfire exposure, and it stops at 2022.

This project continues it. For a fire from 2023 onward it produces one row per building inside the area around the fire, carrying whether the building stood before the fire, where it sat relative to the fire perimeter, and a continuous probability that it was destroyed together with a destroyed/survived call at a published cut-off. The historical rows and ours are published as one layer on one schema, so a user reads a single file instead of joining two datasets and discovering that the joins do not work.

Three audiences, in the order the design serves them. Researchers extending an exposure or risk model past 2022 — they need the two halves comparable, and a frank answer about where they are not. Agencies and insurers who want a per-building loss layer for a fire nobody has inspected — they need the probability, not only the call. Anyone checking the work — every fire has a browsable map of its predictions on imagery, and every number on this page names the file it came from.

For the same method in plain language and fewer numbers, read how it works. This page assumes a reader comfortable with remote sensing and out-of-sample validation.

02 · method

The two halves of the method, and why they are scored separately

Half one — the list of buildings that stood before the fire

The building list for a fire is the union of three sources. Two are public building-footprint databases — maps of building outlines that anyone can download: the FEMA / Oak Ridge USA Structures layer, filtered to outlines whose source imagery predates ignition, and Microsoft's US Building Footprints, filtered on its own published capture dates. The third is our building detector: a neural network of the U-Net family, with a 34-layer residual image encoder, run over aerial photography — the US Department of Agriculture's National Agriculture Imagery Program, which photographs the continental United States at roughly 60 cm per pixel and is reflown every two to three years. It finds buildings the databases miss, so the list of buildings at risk is as complete as we can make it. The network is trained on 24k–43k image tiles of 512 pixels a side, with public footprints burned in as training masks, split into spatial blocks that no tile crosses so that training and testing never share ground.

Two design choices carry most of the detector's value. The network takes the footprint database itself as an input band — a prior that says "a public map already shows a building here" — and it uses an ignore mask around truth points that no footprint covers, so the training loss is not penalised for finding a building nobody had drawn. The ignore mask lifts recall on buildings with no footprint label from 0.34 to 0.47 at unchanged precision, and supplying the prior band takes overall recall on the held-out census to 0.814.

A detection joins the list under a polygon-union rule: a detected point is kept unless it falls inside an existing footprint polygon plus 3 m. A simple centre-to-centre distance rule would delete every garage, shed and second dwelling near a house. Buildings under 50 m² of footprint are labelled minor and kept rather than dropped, because the historical record counted major buildings only and pooling the two silently lowers recall.

The primary artefact for each fire is a 60 cm building-probability raster (values 0–250, with 255 meaning not inferred). Points are cut from that raster, so trying a different cut-off is a re-cut rather than a fresh run of the network.

Half two — the destroyed call

At every point on the building list, reflectance before and after the fire is sampled from Sentinel-2 — a pair of free European Space Agency satellites that photograph every point on Earth about every five days at 10 m per pixel, available from 2016 onward — and from Landsat, the older free 30 m record that covers all years. The two composites open before ignition and close 74 days after it, which is why the primary estimate arrives about two and a half months after a fire starts.

That gives 62 optical features per building: the pixel on the building and the mean of its 3×3 neighbourhood, before, after and the difference between them, for a burn-sensitive index (the normalised burn ratio), a greenness index (the normalised difference vegetation index), and the near-infrared and short-wave infrared bands, among others. What carries the signal is the drop in the burn ratio from before to after and the same drop averaged over the neighbourhood — a separation of about 2.4 standard deviations between destroyed and surviving buildings on the Camp fire — together with the fall in near-infrared reflectance and the rise in short-wave infrared.

The learner is histogram gradient boosting, fitted on the historical record's own destroyed and surviving labels over 66 fires plus eight fire-service inspection incidents — 74 fires, 618,826 rows. It is scored with held-out scoring: the model that scores a fire is fitted on every other fire, predicts the fire it never saw, and the predictions are pooled. The cut-off is a single fixed 0.85: the model outputs a probability for each building, and a building is called destroyed when that probability is above 0.85. The number is fixed in advance rather than tuned per fire, and it is chosen to maximise the average F1 across fires rather than the pooled F1, which two large Los Angeles fires would otherwise dominate.

The one rule that governs what may be a feature. No attribute of the fire itself — its size, its perimeter, its burn severity, its region, state or year — ever reaches the model, either when it is fitted or when it is applied. An automated test pins this by renaming a fire and moving it to another state and asserting that no prediction moves. Perimeters scope the work — they decide where to look and which exposure class a building falls in — and they never predict. This is what keeps the destroyed call from being a restatement of the fire's own attributes.

Why the two halves are never pooled into one score

A missing building and a wrong damage call are different mistakes, and they are checked against different kinds of truth. The building list is judged against a census: the historical record enumerated every major building inside a fire's covered area, so a building we failed to find is measurable. The destroyed call is judged against inspections: CAL FIRE's Damage Inspection programme (DINS) sends inspectors to each structure inside the burn area after a fire and records whether it was destroyed, damaged or untouched, so it can say whether a building we found burned, but it cannot say whether a building we found exists. Pooling the two produces a number that answers neither question, so they are reported apart everywhere on this page.

03 · scope

The area around a fire, and the three periods

The area around a fire decides which buildings the dataset enumerates. It is the fire's official public perimeter — from the national wildfire incident feed, with the California state fire agency's perimeter as a fallback — buffered outward, matching the rule the published historical record used. Buildings inside the perimeter or within 100 m of it are the directly exposed ones; buildings from 100 m out to 2.4 km are indirectly exposed, and are very rarely destroyed. Over the fifteen recent fires this area holds 168,197 buildings, most of them in the outer ring.

Defining the area from the perimeter rather than from inspection records means the dataset enumerates the same population the historical record enumerated, and it means a fire can be processed in a state that has no damage-inspection programme at all.

measured against · the public building-footprint databases

How complete the building list is

The public footprint databases are not a competitor. They are the baseline, and they supply most of every building list this project publishes. The only honest question is what our detector adds to them.

What the detector adds, measured against inspections

Taking the two public databases together as the baseline, this comparison counts only the inspection records that the full building list matches and the baseline does not. A ground-truth point and one of our building locations are treated as the same building when they lie within 10 m of one another, matched one-to-one. Ten metres is the working radius because the tenth-percentile spacing between neighbouring inspection points is 8.7–11.3 m, and a 20 m match can easily be the house next door.

Source: outputs/structure_metrics/ on the data share, per-fire detection tables for the fifteen recent fires; pooled interval from outputs/uncertainty/detector_marginal/.

The honest size of the contribution: 343 buildings across fifteen fires — 0.9 points of recall, and 3.5 % of what the footprint databases missed, with a 95 % interval over fires of [0.025, 0.066]. Matching at 20 m instead, it is 394 of 8,632, or 4.6 %. The public footprints are most of the product, and we are slightly better than them.

What the databases miss is not mostly new construction. Of the 9,845 inspection records the baseline missed at 10 m, 8,208 have both a plausible recorded construction year and a dated footprint within 250 m, and only 85 of those 8,208 (1.0 %) were built after the date of the imagery that footprint was drawn from. The misses are small buildings — 5,279 of the 9,845 are inspected as minor structures — and buildings the original digitiser simply did not draw.

What the detector adds, measured against a complete census

Inspections cannot say whether a building we hold is real, because they only record buildings somebody visited. A census can: inside its covered area, the published historical record is a complete enumeration of major buildings, destroyed and surviving alike. Where the databases are incomplete, it is a matter of county coverage rather than of the detector. Ventura and Sonoma counties' pre-fire footprint vintages leave Thomas and Tubbs at 0.25–0.28 recall, while Woolsey and Creek sit at 0.84–0.87. And the databases miss destroyed buildings harder than surviving ones (0.407 against 0.590 pooled), which is exactly the direction that would bias a burn-probability model, so every published figure stays split by whether the building survived.

On held-out spatial blocks only — the tiles the detector never trained on, 38,821 census buildings — adding the detector to the baseline is worth +4.1 points of recall for 1.9 points of precision (0.768 to 0.809 recall, 0.851 to 0.832 precision at 10 m; 0.785 to 0.828 recall at 20 m). That is four times what the same detector appears to add when it is measured against inspections, and the reason is what the two kinds of truth are: a census enumerates every major building, while inspections cover only the buildings somebody visited, so most of what the detector adds is invisible to an inspection record by construction. It is the strongest evidence that the detector's additions are mostly real buildings.

The other direction — footprints with no building under them

A baseline footprint inside the census area with no census building within 10 m is a candidate phantom: a building demolished before the fire, a digitising error, a small building the census never counted, or a genuine census miss.

The edge effect is four times the size-rule effect, and neither is what the raw 14.8 % invites a reader to think. The 50 m² line also under-corrects, and the inspection record says by how much: 608 of the 23,594 candidates have an inspection record within 10 m, 536 of them major-sized, and 112 of those 536 are inspected as minor structures, at a median area of 74 m². Those are real small buildings above the area cut that no area rule can separate out. Read 0.094 as the tightest upper bound we can defend on footprints with nothing under them, not as a measurement of them.

Nothing is deleted from the building list. No operating point of the phantom signal reaches a deletion precision above 0.36, so roughly two deletions in three would remove a real building. The phantom score ships as a triage column for a human reviewer, and that review is not feasible at scale: the full queue for five fires is 23,594 polygons, or 66 person-days at one polygon a minute. A week of one analyst's time buys about 1,800 polygons — a sample for estimating the rate, not a cleaning pass.

measured against · CAL FIRE damage inspections

Accuracy against fire-service inspections

CAL FIRE's Damage Inspection programme sends inspectors to every structure in a burn area and records whether it was destroyed, damaged or untouched. It is the only building-by-building truth that covers fires after the historical record ends, which makes it the primary out-of-sample check — and it is California only, which bounds everything it can say.

Finding the buildings: how many inspected structures the list contains

Pooled over the 15 fires of 2024–2025, the full building list matches 0.753 of the 38,497 inspection records one-to-one at 10 m and 0.786 at 20 m. Per fire this runs from 0.825 on suburban Coches 2 down to 0.432 on rural Park, in the Sierra Nevada. Restricted to major buildings, the population the historical census counts, suburban fires reach 0.85–0.95.

Precision cannot be measured from inspections, and this page says so rather than printing a number that looks like one. Of 2,267 points that only the detector found, 347 are confirmed by an inspection record, 409 sit inside an inspected area and match nothing, and 1,511 sit where nobody inspected. So at most 54 % of our additions inside an inspected area are wrong, and an inspection record cannot narrow that further, because it records damaged and threatened structures rather than every structure.

The destroyed call, fire by fire, out of sample

The eight inspection incidents of 2024–25 are part of the model's own training pool, so none of them is scored by that model. Each is scored by a model refitted from scratch on the other seven incidents plus the historical fires — same features, same learner, same random seed, same cut-off — and asked to predict the incident it never saw.

Source: outputs/uncertainty/per_fire_ci.csv on the data share (within-fire scope, F1, cut-off 0.85, 2,000 resamples), dated 2026-09-12. Every interval this project publishes is explained on the uncertainty page.

⚠️ The unit of evidence is the fire, and there are eight of them. Resampling whole fires rather than individual buildings gives a pooled F1 of 0.9158 [0.8591, 0.9190] — an interval that barely reaches above the estimate, because two fires hold 84 % of the buildings and both score high, so a resample that draws either of them less often falls and one that draws them more often has nowhere to go. That width is the evidence, not a quirk of the estimator, and it is not narrowed anywhere on this page.

Is the destroyed probability a probability?

Not on its own — it is a reliable ranking before it is a calibrated probability. The chart shows how the predicted probability compares with the observed share destroyed, in ten equal-count bins, on both halves of the training pool.

Source: outputs/uncertainty/calibration_pooled.csv and calibration_ci.csv on the data share (ten bins), dated 2026-09-12.

Expected calibration error — the average gap between predicted probability and observed frequency — is 0.0514 [0.0365, 0.0737] on the 66-fire national table and 0.0651 [0.0514, 0.1032] on the eight inspection incidents. A monotone (isotonic) correction fitted with held-out scoring takes those to 0.0005 and 0.0071, and the Brier score from 0.0328 to 0.0208 and from 0.0729 to 0.0616. The corrected probability ships in a column of its own beside the raw score rather than replacing it: the correction is monotone, so it cannot re-rank anything, and the operating cut-off stays on the raw score. Calibration is not a per-fire cut-off rule, and it does not recover the accuracy the fixed cut-off loses outside California.

Two cut-offs ship on every scored building

Each scored building carries two destroyed/survived calls from the same probability. One uses the fixed cut-off of 0.85, the same number for every fire; that is the headline call, and every accuracy figure on this site reports it. The other uses a per-fire cut-off read from the spread of that fire's own model scores and nothing else — never any attribute of the fire — which on whole building lists lands between 0.601 and 0.792. It ships labelled as advisory. The difference between them is small and it goes in a consistent direction.

What the per-fire cut-off buys is the total. Over the fifteen fires' whole building lists, buildings called destroyed go from 14,504 to 15,951 against 17,902 inspected as destroyed — a ratio of 0.810 to 0.891. The two columns therefore disagree in a legible way: the fixed cut-off is the better per-building classifier, and the per-fire one gets the fire total closer. In one line: use the fixed call to classify buildings, use the per-fire call if you are counting them, and state which you used.

Outside California the per-fire cut-off is the better call outright. On the 66-fire national table, mean-fire F1 outside California goes from 0.8986 to 0.9121, and on the one post-2022 assessment outside California that exists, F1 goes from 0.8484 to 0.8825. On the one fire with two independent assessments it splits — 0.9705 to 0.9725 on one, 0.9273 to 0.9109 on the other. Three assessments over two fires is not a distribution.

the second product · the estimate that arrives about a year later

The refined estimate, made about a year after the fire

The primary estimate is made 74 days after a fire, from what the satellites had seen by then. The refined estimate is made about a year later, from evidence that only exists once the year is over — and it is better on precision and recall at once, not a trade between them.

Most scored buildings carry a refined estimate beside the primary one: a second model's probability, its own destroyed call, its own cut-off (0.84), and the years of imagery it read. It sees three things the first model cannot. It gets a clean before-and-after pair of annual satellite embeddings — freely published yearly summaries of what each 10 m patch of ground looked like across a whole year, as 64 numbers per pixel, with one year wholly before the fire and one wholly after. It gets the scene-by-scene behaviour of the year after ignition. And it gets a second, later post-fire image window. None of those can be assembled at 74 days, which is why this is a second product rather than a replacement for the first.

Thirty-one points of F1 on the buildings the first model is least sure about is what this second estimate is for. A burned building on bare ground is the case optical change detection has always been worst at — there is no vegetation at the building to lose — and it is exactly the case that a pair of annual summaries and a later image window are best placed to settle.

The refined estimate never edits the primary one. It is an additional column: a user who needs an answer at two and a half months uses the primary estimate, and a user who can wait gets this one.

Where the refined estimate is absent, it says why — and the reason is the calendar, not a failure. The refined block is either complete or empty on a given row and never partial, and it is empty throughout the historical half, because no model of ours made those calls. On the 28 fires it does reach, its three sources of evidence cover more than 99.9 % of buildings; what keeps it off the other six fires is that the years of imagery it needs do not exist for them yet.

Reading it in one sentence: given the same list of buildings, our destroyed call agrees with the hand-built record almost exactly; run end to end, it finds about three quarters of the buildings and therefore about three quarters of the losses. The Greek letter κ in the first row is Cohen's kappa, which is agreement after subtracting the agreement chance alone would produce, so 1 is perfect and 0 is no better than guessing.

⚠️ Do not compare a destroyed count from this method with a count from the hand-built record without a factor of about 0.77 — and do not treat that factor as stable. It runs from 0.48 to 1.80 across seventeen fires, it tracks how well the buildings were found rather than anything about the damage model, and it is the net of two large opposite errors: we recover 6,451 of the record's 10,808 destroyed buildings in the region compared, and we add 1,862 destroyed calls on buildings the record did not record as destroyed, 970 of them on buildings it never enumerated at all.

The threat to a trend across the join is composition, not time. The difference between the two methods does not drift with the year; it drifts with the fire, and it tracks how much of a fire's building stock burned.

The two independent sources of truth also disagree with each other by up to 1.8×. On the same ground, fire-service inspectors record more destroyed structures than the hand-built record on 14 of 17 fires, by up to 1.84×. Inspectors count mobile homes, second dwellings and outbuildings the record never counted, and several inspection records can sit on one building. A modern count validated against inspections is therefore not in the same units as the historical record, whatever the model does.

How good are the historical record's own labels? One fire can say.

Every accuracy figure in this project is measured against the historical record or against fire-service inspections, because nothing else covers the same buildings. On the Marshall fire (Colorado, December 2021) three independent lists do: the historical record's points, a county field damage assessment, and a federal remote-sensing assessment. That makes it the only place where the historical record's own label noise can be bounded.

Two properties of the historical record that will break a naive count

The dataset carries the historical record verbatim: nothing is dropped, merged, re-attributed or corrected, because silently cleaning another team's published census inside a derived product would make the two impossible to reconcile. Instead, two flags are measured once over the whole national file and shipped as extra columns:

Reading it in one sentence: about one in eighty historical rows is an exact repeat, and about one in five sits at a location more than one fire claims, so a national total that ignores both flags will be too high.

One fire carries most of the duplication: a single 2008 California fire holds 19,859 of the 20,296 repeats — 40,146 rows at 20,287 locations. A national destroyed count that does not drop them is wrong by that much. The multi-fire flag marks every row at such a location, because without an attribution rule there is no "later" fire to prefer. These are flags rather than fixes, and they are not a criticism: the duplicates are almost certainly an artefact of the long select-and-merge lineage the record's own metadata describes, and multi-fire locations are a correct consequence of a definition — one row per building per fire — that this dataset shares. On our own half the same exposure is zero: no building is called destroyed under more than one fire.

Two further limits bound every count comparison on this page. A national incident figure is an operational estimate made during the incident, and it stops being updated when the fire leaves the national situation report — usually before the damage inspection finishes — so read it as a floor. Three caveats belong with these numbers. South Fork's detection recall of 0.661 is well below California for structural reasons rather than model quality: New Mexico has no cached Microsoft footprint layer, so the detector's prior band comes from the USA Structures layer alone, and that county's extract carries a single image date seven years before the fire. The remote-sensing assessment's detection recall of 0.952 is not comparable with the other two, because its geometry is the USA Structures footprints, which are also the main source of our building list, so the two match each other by construction; the county field list's 0.710 is the honest detection figure. Marshall also settles the winter question. Its before window gives a median of 6 clear Sentinel-2 observations per building and its after window a median of 2, against 9–17 on a summer California fire — and even so only 12 of 14,786 buildings (0.08 %) could not be scored. Snow does not stop the method; the post-fire composite is simply thin.

10 · limits

What this dataset cannot do

  1. No fire has yet been scored before its ground truth existed. Every accuracy figure here comes from held-out scoring — the model that scores a fire is trained only on the other fires — which is the strongest check available from public data, but it is not the same as a prediction issued before anyone inspected the fire.
  2. Eight fires carry the inspection evidence, and two of them are 84 % of it. Every inspection interval on this page is a resample over those eight fires.
  3. The ring beyond 100 m from the perimeter cannot be scored and carries nothing. On the national table it is 438,704 of 582,554 rows at a destroyed rate of 0.0009, and the model scores an F1 of 0.10 on them. Every headline in this project is carried by the buildings close to the perimeter. On both fires outside California that ring holds no destroyed buildings at all.
  4. A list of building footprints undercounts inspected structures by roughly a fifth wherever several inspected structures sit on one parcel.
  5. One category of inspected structure should be excluded or scored separately. Infrastructure scores an F1 of 0.286 (259 records, 16 destroyed): the model over-calls utility structures nine times out of ten, and the model and the inspector do not mean the same thing by "destroyed" there.
  6. The intermediate damage classes do not separate. Structures inspected as "affected, up to 10 % damage" score a mean probability of 0.475 and "minor, 10–25 %" score 0.488, sitting almost exactly between untouched (0.166) and destroyed (0.933). Reading the score as a damage-severity index is defensible; reading it as destroyed-or-not puts a fifth of those buildings over 0.9.
  7. The historical half has no attribution rule of its own. 18.3 % of its 1,770,939 rows sit at a location a second fire also claims, and it carries no building identifier. De-duplicate on geometry before any pooled count across historical fires.
  8. The detector's additions cannot be proven right from inspections. At most 54 % of the detector-only points inside an inspected area are wrong, and the inspection record cannot narrow that, because it does not record every structure.
  9. Nothing before 2016 can be produced by this method at all, and nothing after 2022 has a hand-built census to be checked against. The comparison between the two periods is bounded on both sides by construction.
  10. Footprints are never deleted. The phantom rate is an upper bound and the triage ranking is a queue for a person, not a rule.

⚠️ The exclusion is uneven, and it is largest where the footprint databases are weakest — 0.03 % of Atlas 2017's rows against 36.1 % of CZU 2020's, with South Fork NM at 28.4 % and Dixie 2021 at 16.5 % — so a user counting buildings from the package on a rural fire is counting a filtered list.

Microsoft footprints are also used as a training prior for the detector, which is a different question from redistribution: the network reads a raster band saying "a footprint database has a building here", and the weights and every building the detector outputs are this project's own work.
