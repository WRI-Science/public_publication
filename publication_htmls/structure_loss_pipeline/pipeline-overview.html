Structure Loss Pipeline · deep dive

What burned, and what was there first

A reproducible pipeline that enumerates the structures standing before a wildfire and then predicts which of them were destroyed, from public data only — so the USGS Carlson structure-loss record (2002–2022) can be continued to the present. This page is the technical read: what the product is, how it is built, and what it measures against every other dataset that covers the same buildings.

Where the project is, 12 September 2026

The published dataset is v0.8.0, cut on 2026-09-13. One layer, 377 fires, 2,069,752 released rows: the 2002–2022 hand survey carried verbatim, our own sixteen fires of 2024–2025, and seventeen fires the survey already covers that this pipeline has now also enumerated — so those sixteen are described twice, and the two descriptions must never be summed.

Four things a reader should know before the numbers start.

  • The detector is a small addition to the footprint databases, not a replacement. Against the inspection record it recovers 3.5 % of what the public databases miss. Against a complete census it is worth four times that, and most on the fires whose footprint data is too young to trust.
  • Every scored row carries two loss calls — one at a fixed threshold, one fitted per fire. The second is advisory, and why is the most important caveat on this page. Most rows now carry a third, from a second model that reads imagery the first one could not wait for.
  • The ring beyond 100 m from the perimeter carries no signal. Every headline in this project is carried by the near-perimeter quarter of the data.
  • Nothing here is a forward hold-out yet. Every damage figure is cross-validated or leave-one-fire-out; the fix is time, and it waits on the next inspection archive.

01 · the product

What the product is, and who it is for

The USGS Carlson structure-loss dataset is a per-building census of what wildfires destroyed in the conterminous United States between 2002 and 2022 — 361 fires, 1,770,939 rows, hand digitised. It is the reference layer for anyone modelling wildfire exposure, and it stops at 2022.

This pipeline continues it. For a fire from 2023 onward it produces one row per structure inside the fire's frame, carrying whether the structure stood before the fire, where it sat relative to the perimeter, and a continuous probability that it was destroyed with a call at a published threshold. Carlson's rows and ours are published as one layer on one schema so a user reads a single file rather than joining two datasets and discovering the joins do not work.

Three audiences, in the order the design serves them. Researchers extending an exposure or risk model past 2022 — they need the two eras comparable, and the honest answer about where they are not. Agencies and insurers who want a per-structure loss layer for a fire nobody has inspected — they need the probability, not just the call. Anyone checking the work — every fire has a browsable map of its predictions on imagery, and every number on this page names the file it came from.

For the plain-language version of the method, read How the pipeline decides what was there, and what burned. This page assumes a reader comfortable with remote sensing and out-of-sample validation.

02 · method

The two parts, and why they are scored separately

Part one — the pre-fire inventory

A structure inventory for a fire is the union of three sources: FEMA/ORNL USA Structures footprints filtered to those whose IMAGE_DATE precedes ignition; Microsoft US Building Footprints v2 filtered on their own capture_dates_range; and a U-Net (ResNet-34 encoder) run over 0.6 m NAIP imagery of the whole frame. The network is trained on 24k–43k 512-px chips with USA Structures footprints burned as masks and spatial-block splits that no block crosses.

Two design choices carry most of the detector's value. The network takes the footprint database itself as an input band — a prior that says "a public layer has a building here" — together with an ignore mask around truth points that no footprint covers, so the loss is not penalised for finding a building nobody drew. The ignore mask alone lifted recall on buildings with no footprint label from 0.34 to 0.47 at unchanged precision, and feeding the prior took overall recall on the held-out census to 0.814. Detector work is frozen at one checkpoint, so later comparisons are about the damage model rather than about a moving inventory.

A detection is merged into the inventory under a polygon-union rule: a detected point is kept unless it falls inside an existing footprint polygon plus 3 m — a centroid-distance rule would delete every garage, shed and second dwelling near a house. Structures below 50 m² of footprint are tagged size_class = minor and kept, not dropped, because Carlson counted major buildings only and pooling the two silently lowers recall.

The primary artefact is the 0.6 m structure-probability raster per fire (uint8 0–250, 255 = not inferred); points are cut from it, so a different threshold is a re-cut rather than a re-inference.

Part two — the damage call

At every inventory point, before/after reflectance is sampled from Sentinel-2 L2A (10 m, 2016 onward) and Landsat Collection 2 L2 (30 m, all years) over composites that open before ignition and close 74 days after it. 62 optical features per structure: the point pixel and its 3×3 neighbourhood mean, pre, post and delta, for NBR, NDVI, NIR and SWIR among others. What carries the signal is post − pre NBR and its neighbourhood mean (Cohen's d ≈ −2.4 on Camp), the NIR drop and the SWIR rise.

The learner is histogram gradient boosting, fitted on the hand survey's own destroyed/surviving labels over 66 fires plus eight inspection incidents — 74 fires, 618,826 rows — and scored leave-one-fire-out: fit on every other fire, predict the held-out one, pool the predictions. The threshold is a single fixed 0.85, chosen to maximise the average F1 across fires rather than the pooled F1, which two large Los Angeles fires would otherwise dominate.

The model is optical only. Radar was in it and is not any more. Nine C-band radar columns had been promoted under a rule written in advance; on corrected feature tables they stopped clearing it, and they were dropped. Removing them costs nothing measurable — −0.0039 mean-fire F1, 95 % CI [−0.0106, +0.0025] — and it removes a dependency, because an optical-only model can be run on a fire from before the radar archive opened in October 2014.

⚠️ The one rule that governs what may be a feature. No attribute of the fire itself — size, perimeter, dNBR, region, state, year — ever reaches the model, at fitting or at application (goal-no-fire-covariates). A test pins it by renaming a fire and moving it to another state and asserting nothing moves. Perimeters scope — they decide where to look and which exposure class a structure is in — and they never predict. This is what keeps the loss call from being a restatement of the fire's own attributes.

Why the two halves are never pooled into one score

An inventory error and a damage error are different mistakes against different truth sets. The inventory is judged against a census — Carlson enumerated every major building inside a fire's footprint area, so a missing building is measurable. The damage call is judged against inspections — CAL FIRE DINS visits damaged and threatened structures, so it can say whether a building we found burned but cannot say whether a building we found exists. Pooling them produces a number that answers neither question, and the two are reported apart everywhere on this page.

03 · scope

The frame, the exposure class, and the three eras

A fire's frame decides which structures the product enumerates. Since 2026-09-10 it is the fire's public perimeter (NIFC/WFIGS, with CAL FIRE FRAP as a fallback) buffered outward, matching Carlson's own rule: Direct is the perimeter plus 100 m, Indirect is 100 m to 2.4 km. The legacy frame — a 2.4 km buffer around CAL FIRE DINS inspection points — enumerated a different population by construction and could not define a fire at all outside California, where there are no DINS points.

Changing to it added roughly a third again as many structures — 133,468 to 168,197 over the fifteen modern fires, almost all of them in the outer ring — and moved no accuracy number at all: every inspection record the wider frame reaches was already reached, because inspectors work the perimeter and its margin and everything the ring adds is beyond where anybody looked. What the frame buys is not accuracy but definition — the product now enumerates the population the historical survey enumerated, and it can be run on a fire in a state with no inspection programme at all.

The practical floor of the middle era is not Sentinel-2's launch date. Because the footprint database post-dates every fire in that era, a retrospective inventory keeps only footprints dated before ignition. From 2019 on that costs nothing — 129,680 of 129,680 footprints over twelve fires. Before 2019 it costs 8.5 %, county by county: one county's whole extract comes from scenes taken five and six weeks after the fire in question, which therefore has no pre-fire footprint at all and was refused rather than approximated. (outputs/combined_retrospective/footprint_vintage.csv, 2026-09-12; the full table is on the retrospective page.)

The historical half now has a shape and a raster cell of its own, too. All 1,770,939 of its buildings carry a polygon and 60-centimetre cells wherever the imagery supports one, so the whole twenty-five years share a single convention — and the measured bias in doing it the easy way is on the augmented historical record.

measured against · FEMA/ORNL USA Structures ∪ Microsoft v2

Against the footprint databases

The public footprint databases are not a competitor. They are the baseline, and they supply most of every inventory this pipeline publishes. The only honest question is what the detector adds to them.

What the detector adds, against inspections

Stage 9b treats USA Structures ∪ Microsoft as the baseline and counts only the DINS records that the full inventory matches and the baseline does not. The match radius is 10 m, because the 10th-percentile nearest-neighbour spacing of DINS points is 8.7–11.3 m and a 20 m match can be the house next door.

Source: outputs/structure_metrics/seg_all_v4_full__perimeter__forward/; ledger val-stage9b-marginal, goal-footprints-are-baseline. Pooled interval from outputs/uncertainty/detector_marginal/.

The honest size of the contribution: 343 structures across fifteen fires — 0.9 points of recall, and 3.5 % of what the footprint database missed, 95 % CI [0.025, 0.066] over fires. At 20 m it is 394 of 8,632, 4.6 %. The footprints are the product, and we are slightly better than them.

The reason the database misses what it misses is not construction after the imagery. Of the 9,845 records it missed at 10 m, 8,208 have both a plausible YEARBUILT and a dated USA Structures footprint within 250 m, and only 85 of those 8,208 (1.0 %) were built after that footprint's IMAGE_DATE. The misses are minor structures — 5,279 of 9,845 are DINS "Other Minor Structure" — and buildings the digitiser did not draw.

What the detector adds, against a census

DINS cannot measure whether what we hold is real, because it only records structures somebody inspected. Carlson can: inside a fire's coverage it is a complete enumeration of major buildings, destroyed and surviving. Stage 9c scores both directions on the five Carlson fires with a per-fire extract on disk.

The footprint database is precise and incomplete. 0.858 of what it puts inside Carlson's coverage is a Carlson building (0.901 away from the coverage edge), and it holds 0.571 of Carlson's buildings. Recall here is a county-coverage number, not a detector one — Ventura's and Sonoma's pre-fire USA Structures vintages leave Thomas and Tubbs at 0.25–0.28, while Woolsey and Creek sit at 0.84–0.87. And the database misses destroyed buildings much harder than surviving ones (0.407 against 0.590 pooled), which is exactly the direction that biases a burn-probability model, so every published number stays split by bldg_cond.

On held-out spatial blocks only — the chips the detector never trained on, 38,821 Carlson buildings — adding the U-Net to the baseline is worth +4.1 recall points for 1.9 points of precision (0.768 → 0.809 recall, 0.851 → 0.832 precision at 10 m; 0.785 → 0.828 at 20 m). That is four times the marginal Stage 9b measures against DINS, and the reason is what the two truth sets are: Carlson enumerates every major building, DINS only the inspected ones, so most of what the detector adds is invisible to DINS by construction. This is the first evidence that the detector's additions are mostly real.

The other direction — footprints with no building under them

A baseline footprint inside Carlson's coverage with no Carlson building within 10 m is a candidate phantom: demolished before the fire, a digitising error, a minor structure Carlson never counted, or a Carlson miss.

The boundary artefact is four times the scope artefact, and neither is what the raw 14.8 % invites a reader to think. The 50 m² line also under-corrects, and CAL FIRE says by how much: 608 of the 23,594 candidates have a DINS record within 10 m, 536 of them major-sized — and 112 of those 536 are DINS "Other Minor Structure", median area 74 m². Those are real minor buildings above the area cut that no area rule can find. Read 0.094 as the tightest upper bound we can defend on footprints that are not there, not as a measurement of them.

Nothing is removed. No operating point of the phantom signal gets removal precision above 0.36, so roughly two removals in three would delete a real building. The phantom score ships as a triage column for a human reviewer, and the review is not feasible at scale: the full queue for five fires is 23,594 polygons, 66 person-days at one polygon a minute. One analyst-week buys the ~1,800 polygons a prior-free detector pass is most suspicious of — a sample for estimating the rate, not a cleaning pass.

measured against · CAL FIRE DINS inspections

Against CAL FIRE DINS

DINS is the only per-structure truth set that covers fires after Carlson ends, so it is the product's primary out-of-sample check — and it is California only, which bounds everything it can say.

Detection: how many inspected structures the inventory found

Pooled over the 15 fires of 2024–2025, the full inventory matches 0.753 of the 38,497 DINS records one-to-one at 10 m and 0.786 at 20 m. Per fire this runs from 0.825 (Coches 2, suburban) to 0.432 (Park, rural Sierra) — the per-fire figures are the blue and ember marks in the chart above. Restricted to major structures, the Carlson-compatible population, suburban fires reach 0.85–0.95.

Precision is not measurable from DINS, and the page says so rather than reporting a number that looks like one. Of 2,267 U-Net points, 347 are confirmed against an inspection, 409 sit inside an inspected area and matched nothing, and 1,511 sit where nobody inspected. So at most 54 % of our additions inside an inspected area are wrong, and DINS cannot say more than that, because it records damaged and threatened structures, not every structure.

Damage: the loss call, per incident, out of sample

The eight 2024–25 inspection incidents are in the damage model's own 74-fire training pool, so none of them is scored by it. Each carries a leave-one-incident-out probability from a model refitted on the other seven plus the historical fires — same features, same learner, same seed, same threshold.

Each fold's own threshold is recorded and not shipped. Fitted the production way on out-of-fold scores, the nine folds want 0.79–0.88 against the shipped 0.85. That spread is narrow, and it is why a per-fire rule looked safe on the validation table — and why the next section matters.

⚠️ When two fires reach the same building, one rule decides which may claim it. Franklin 2024 ignited near Malibu on 9 December; the Palisades fire burned 13.0 km² of Franklin's outer ring on 7 January — day 29 of Franklin's own 74-day observation window — so a building destroyed by Palisades was being attributed to Franklin. The attribution rule took structures called destroyed under two fires from 271 to 0, and left fourteen of fifteen fires byte-for-byte unchanged. Franklin ships 35 destroyed calls and scores F1 0.286, with 879 of its rows carrying no damage call at all and a reason column saying why. It is the worst fire in the set, and this is why.

Is the destroyed probability a probability?

No — it is a ranking, until it is calibrated. Reliability in 10 equal-count bins, pooled, on both halves of the training pool:

Source: outputs/uncertainty/{v5,dins_v5}/calibration_pooled.csv (n_bins_requested = 10) and calibration_ci.csv, dated 2026-09-12.

Expected calibration error is 0.0514 [0.0365, 0.0737] on the 66-fire national table and 0.0651 [0.0514, 0.1032] on the eight inspection incidents. An isotonic map fitted leave-one-fire-out takes those to 0.0005 and 0.0071, and the Brier score from 0.0328 to 0.0208 and from 0.0729 to 0.0616. It ships as destroyed_prob_calibrated beside destroyed_prob, not instead of it: the map is monotone, so it cannot re-rank anything, and the operating cut stays on the raw score. Calibration is not a per-fire cut rule and does not recover the out-of-state threshold loss.

the headline caveat · a rule that passed its bar and failed the product

Two loss calls, and why the fixed threshold is the primary one

The single most important thing to understand about this dataset, and the reason it ships two destroyed calls instead of replacing one with the other.

Everywhere else in this project a threshold is a compromise: one number applied to every fire, costing the fires whose score distribution sits somewhere else. A per-fire threshold rule that reads only the model's own scores was built to fix that, scored leave-one-fire-out against a five-criterion bar pre-registered before the first fold, and it cleared all five — twice, the second time with the fitting fully nested over 2,211 model fits. That pass is real and nothing here contradicts it.

Then it was applied to the product, and it did not transfer. The reason is not a flaw in the rule or in the bar. It was validated on one population and applied to another.

The rule reads the fire's own score distribution and nothing else — that is what makes it label-free, and what keeps it inside the covariate rule. It is also what makes it move when the population underneath it changes. On a table of inspected structures where roughly half burned, the expected-F1 threshold sits near the fixed one. On a whole inventory where nine in a hundred burned, the same arithmetic drags the threshold down and the model starts calling structures destroyed that nobody ever looked at. Taking the threshold from the near-perimeter zone, whose share destroyed is nearer the validation table's, is what keeps it in range — and it is what ships.

One leak a reader would reasonably suspect is closed by measurement rather than assertion: re-fitting the calibration map leave-one-fire-out gives a variant that is bit-identical on 204,797 of 204,797 rows, because the product already reads each held-out fire's own fold calibration.

And what the advisory call buys is the count. Predicted destroyed over the fifteen fires' whole inventories goes 14,504 → 15,951 against 17,902 inspected as destroyed — a ratio of 0.810 → 0.891. So the two columns disagree in a legible way: the fixed threshold is the better classifier per structure, and the per-fire one gets the total right. The release notes reduce it to one line: use damage_class to classify buildings; use damage_class_adaptive if you are counting them — and say which column you used.

Outside California it is the better call outright. On the 66-fire national table non-California mean-fire F1 goes 0.8986 → 0.9121, and on the one post-2022 non-California assessment that exists, F1 goes 0.8484 → 0.8825. On the one fire with two independent assessments it splits — 0.9705 → 0.9725 on one, 0.9273 → 0.9109 on the other. Three assessments over two fires is not a distribution.

a second model · the call that arrives later

A second model, on imagery that did not exist yet

The primary call is made 74 days after a fire, from what the satellites saw by then. A second call is made later, from an annual composite that only exists once the year is over — and it finds buildings the first one misses.

Every scored row now carries a refinement column beside the primary one: a second model's probability, its own call, its own threshold (0.69), and the two composite years it read. The extra evidence is a clean before-and-after pair of annual land-cover embeddings — one year wholly before the fire, one wholly after — which is why it cannot be produced at 74 days and why it is a separate column rather than a replacement.

Against the record this continues

If the destroyed count moves across the 2022/2023 join, is that fire behaviour or method change? This is the section a user of the joined dataset reads first.

The only way to answer it is to run this pipeline on old fires the survey already covers. Seventeen were rebuilt end to end and twenty-three carry the loss call; the whole experiment is running the pipeline backwards, and this is the summary a reader of this page needs.

⚠️ Do not compare a destroyed count from this pipeline to a count from the survey without a factor of about 0.77 — and do not treat that factor as stable. It runs 0.48 to 1.80 across seventeen fires, it tracks how well the buildings were found rather than anything about the loss model, and it is a net of two large opposite errors: we recover 6,451 of the survey's 10,808 destroyed buildings in region and add 1,862 destroyed calls on structures the survey did not record as destroyed, 970 of them on buildings it never enumerated at all.

The threat to a cross-join trend is composition, not time. The method's bias does not drift with the year; it drifts with the fire, and it tracks how much of a fire's building stock burned. A published rule of thumb that said otherwise — every fire above about 7 % destroyed lands between 0.82 and 1.06 of the survey's count — is false on twenty-three fires and has been withdrawn.

The two truth sources also disagree with each other by up to 1.8×. On the same ground the inspectors record more destroyed structures than the survey on 13 of 17 fires, up to 1.84× — they count mobile homes, second dwellings and outbuildings the survey never counted, and several inspection records can sit on one building. A modern count validated against inspections is not in the survey's units, whatever the model does.

How good are the survey's own labels? One fire can say.

Every accuracy figure in this project is measured against the survey or against the inspection record, because nothing else covers the same structures. On the Marshall fire (CO, December 2021) three independent lists do — the survey's points, a county field damage assessment and a federal remote-sensing assessment. That makes it the only place in this project where the survey's own label noise can be bounded.

Two properties of the survey's release that will break a naive count

The dataset carries the survey verbatim: nothing is dropped, merged, re-attributed or corrected, because silently cleaning another team's published census inside a derived product would make the two impossible to reconcile. One fire carries most of the duplication: CA_2008_SAYRE alone holds 19,859 of the 20,296 repeats — 40,146 rows at 20,287 locations. The multi-fire flag marks every row at such a location, because without an attribution rule there is no "later" fire to prefer. These are flags, not fixes, and not a criticism: the duplicates are almost certainly an artefact of the 145-step select-then-merge lineage the release's own metadata records, and the multi-fire locations are a correct consequence of a definition — one row per (structure, fire) — that this series shares. On our own side of the dataset the same exposure is zero: 0 structures are called destroyed under more than one fire.

The only source that gives a destroyed-structure count for a fire in a state with no inspection programme. It is a count, not a census, and this section is mostly about what it cannot do.

Two more limits, stated because they bound every count comparison on this page. An ICS-209 figure is an operational estimate made during the incident and stops being updated when the fire leaves the national situation report — usually before the damage inspection finishes, so read it as a floor. And it is per incident, and an incident is not a perimeter: a complex reports once for several fires.

Does it travel outside California?

Every out-of-sample number above is CAL FIRE DINS, and DINS is California only. The product is not.

⚠️ What this evidence is not. Twenty-five fires outside California, none later than 2022, and Texas, Kansas, Florida, New Mexico and Utah are one fire each — a state row with one fire is one fire, not a state. Every number here is Carlson-era leave-one-fire-out, so it says nothing about a 2023+ out-of-state fire, which no truth set in this project covers.

Can a label-free cut rule fix it? The bar was written down first.

Thirteen per-fire threshold rules that read only the model's own scores — never anything about the fire — were scored leave-one-fire-out on the same 66-fire table, with the fire as the unit, against a five-criterion promotion bar written into the project change log before the first fold was scored. One clears all five, and it is the rule that ships as the advisory call: mean-fire F1 0.775 → 0.788 and non-California 0.730 → 0.756, with California gaining rather than paying for it. The full table and the criterion-by-criterion verdict are on the outside-California page. What that pass does not do is transfer to the product, which is the section above.

Why no single rule can win. The states split into two shapes and the label-free signal that fixes one wrecks the other. A rule that fits nothing at all — call the top of the fire's own score distribution — takes Texas from F1 0.109 to 0.623 against a labelled ceiling of 0.622: the signal is genuinely there in the scores, with no labels and nothing about the fire. It then costs California 13 points, because California's fires are already at their threshold. California is 41 of the 66 fires, so a rule that helps eleven fires a lot and hurts forty a little can still post a positive average — which is exactly what the California, per-state and worst-fire criteria exist to catch.

10 · limits

Known limitations

  1. No forward hold-out exists yet. Every damage number is cross-validated or leave-one-fire-out; the production bundle has seen all eight DINS incidents. The fix is time. On 2026-09-10 the bundles, the cut and the composite windows were frozen and hashed, and a complete snapshot of the public CAL FIRE DINS archive taken the same day holds 132,522 records covering 2013–2025 and zero incidents of 2026. The same selection rule applied to 2025 picks exactly the 2025 fires already in the forward set, so the empty result is the archive, not the query. This project uses public data only, so the hold-out waits on CAL FIRE.
  2. Eight fires carry the out-of-sample evidence, and two of them are 84 % of it. Every inspection interval on this page is a bootstrap over eight incidents.
  3. The ring beyond 100 m from the perimeter is not scoreable and carries the headline nowhere. On the national table it is 438,704 of 582,554 rows at a destroyed rate of 0.0009, and the model scores F1 0.10 on them. Every headline in this project is carried by the near-perimeter quarter. On both outside-California fires that ring holds no destroyed structures at all.
  4. An inventory of building footprints undercounts DINS structures by roughly a fifth wherever several inspected structures sit on one parcel. A per-parcel or footprint-splitting step is the next thing to add.
  5. One inspection category should be excluded or scored separately. Infrastructure scores F1 0.286 (n 259, 16 destroyed): the model over-calls utility structures nine times out of ten, and the model and the inspector disagree about what "destroyed" means there.
  6. The intermediate damage classes do not become separable. "Affected (>0–10 %)" scores mean 0.475 and "Minor (10–25 %)" 0.488, sitting almost exactly between No Damage (0.166) and Destroyed (0.933). Reading the score as a damage-severity index is defensible; reading it as "destroyed or not" puts a fifth of them over 0.9.
  7. The historical half has no attribution rule of its own. 18.3 % of its 1,770,939 rows sit at a location a second fire also claims, and its release has no structure identifier. De-duplicate on geometry before any pooled count across historical fires.
  8. The detector's additions cannot be proven right from inspections. At most 54 % of the detected-only points inside an inspected area are wrong, and the inspection record cannot narrow that, because it does not record every structure.
  9. Nothing before 2016 can be produced by this method at all, and nothing after 2022 has a hand enumeration to be checked against. The era comparison is bounded on both sides by construction.
  10. Footprints are never removed. The phantom rate is an upper bound and the triage ranking is a queue for a person, not a rule.
