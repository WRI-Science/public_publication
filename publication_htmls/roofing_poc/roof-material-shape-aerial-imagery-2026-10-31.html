Roofing proof of concept · research results

Roof material and shape from aerial imagery · 2026-10-31

Can roof material (asphalt, tile, metal, wood, flat), roof shape and roof complexity be read from free aerial photos and lidar? Tested on Santa Cruz County CA, California wildfire areas, Riverside County CA, Pasco County FL, Cook County IL and Dallas County TX, scored on areas the models never saw. New on top: satellite data (AlphaEarth, Sentinel-2), several photo years, neighbours' roofs, and one model trained on all six places.

By Carlo Broderick, NCEAS, UC Santa Barbara · 2026-10-31 13:09 PDT

How to read this page

Each bar is a ranking score (AUC): 0.5 means the model is guessing, 1 means it always ranks roofs of that type above the rest. Whiskers show the range we can trust. Hover or tap any bar, cell or heading for counts. Each panel has a Plain | Technical switch.

Round 2 · 2026-10-31: satellites, neighbours, one model for all places

Added on top of the first night's results: AlphaEarth and Sentinel-2 satellite data, several years of aerial photos, the roofs of neighbouring buildings, one model trained on every place at once, and two new places (Riverside CA, Dallas TX).

The new models beat the first night's in 13 of 17 region-and-roof pairs (typical gain +0.02). Santa Cruz tile went from 0.90 to 0.97; wood shake in the fire areas from 0.58 to 0.73. Knowing some neighbours' roofs adds more. Riverside and Dallas are new places, so they have no round-1 bar. One model for everywhere, or one per place?

Pooling all labels into one photo model helps (typical change +0.01 over one-place models), most where a place has few or noisy labels (Santa Cruz). But a model that has never seen a place does much worse there (typical drop 0.10): each county's records draw the line between roof types differently, so a few local labels are worth getting.Same fold-0 test buildings throughout. 'Pooled' CNN trained on folds 2-4 of all places (less data than the one-place models, which used folds 1-4 for Pasco/Cook). 'Never seen' = leave-one-region-out LightGBM on the same features. Riverside and Dallas joined in round 2, so they have no one-place photo model.

Your neighbours' roof is a good guess

Roof types cluster: when 30% of buildings in the area have a known roof, scores rise in 13 of 27 place-and-roof pairs. Roofs already scored above 0.9 have little room to gain; for the 11 harder ones the typical gain is +0.03. The biggest gains are for the hard cases: Santa Cruz wood shake goes from 0.63 to 0.77. In county records part of this is how assessors coded whole streets, so the CAL FIRE inspection rows are the cleaner test. Labels only in neighbouring areas add little.Features from the 15 nearest labelled buildings within 1 km (inverse-distance class shares, nearest distance and class, count within 100 m), ignoring points under 1.5 m away (duplicates). '% known' = a random share of all labelled buildings in the place is revealed; revealed buildings are never scored. 'Next areas' = labels only from other folds. What each input adds

AlphaEarth (Google's yearly satellite summary, 10 m) and several years of aerial photos each add a clear step; Sentinel-2's extra colours (10–20 m) add a little on top. Satellite pixels are bigger than most houses, so they describe the block as much as the roof.Validation fold 1 (LightGBM trained on folds 2-4), mean one-vs-rest AUC over the roof types with ≥20 examples; small number = change vs one-year NAIP features. AlphaEarth: annual 64-d embedding (centre pixel + 3×3 mean) of the year before any fire; Sentinel-2 L2A: median of the 8 clearest scenes per year, 10 bands + indices; 'several years' = mean and spread across 2-4 years.

Round 1: roof material

Tile, metal and flat roofs can be found where they are common enough to learn; wood shake cannot, at this photo resolution.

Which roof materials can be told from above

Tile is picked out well in California and Florida (AUC 0.96–0.97); in Cook County, where only 0.6% of roofs are tile, much less so (0.78). Metal and flat roofs are found well where there are many examples. Santa Cruz has too few metal roofs to say more than 'moderate'. Wood shake is weak; Cook County's high wood score mostly reflects house size, not the shingles.Best model per study that uses no county records as inputs (Santa Cruz: NAIP + lidar features; elsewhere the image model), scored on areas or fires it never saw. Classes with fewer than 20 test roofs are left off. Pasco and Cook test sets kept every non-asphalt roof and a sample of asphalt; scores are weighted back to the real mix of roofs (asphalt weight 23× in Cook, 1.8× for asphalt gable/hip in Pasco).

Out-of-date records

Most county records describe the roof when it was first assessed, and many roofs have since been replaced. We compared the same Santa Cruz roofs across four photo years (2016–2022) and flagged the 10% whose colour changed most. In an informal look at eight of them, five were real re-roofs or rebuilds and three were trees or shadows, which suggests very roughly 2–9% of roofs were visibly replaced in six years. Records marked wood shake mostly look like ordinary composition roofs today. These are rough checks, not measured rates.

Roof shape and complexity

Roof shapes measured from lidar, Santa Cruz County

Laser points over each of 103,899 buildings were split into flat roof faces; the number, slope and direction of the faces give the shape. Against 243 OpenStreetMap tags for gable, hip, flat and shed roofs it agrees 79% of the time. Flat calls are the weak spot: it finds 85% of the flat roofs, but only 49% of its flat calls are flat in OpenStreetMap (low-pitch sheds get called flat), so the flat count is probably too high. L-/T-shaped gables and complex roofs have no outside check yet.USGS 3DEP CA_SantaCruzCounty_2020 (≈41 pts/m²), footprints shifted (+1.5, −0.6) m to the lidar; iterative RANSAC planes ≥2 m²; rules on plane count, azimuth groups, ridge/eave length ratio and flat share. Thresholds were tuned on the same OSM tags, so the agreement is somewhat optimistic.
