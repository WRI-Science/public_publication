Identifying Roof Top Characteristics with Remote Sensing · 2026-11-01

Can aerial and satellite imagery tell what a roof is made of in a county with no roof records to learn from? Tested in 58 places across the US (4.6 million labelled buildings scored; about 11 million labelled roofs available), each place scored by models that never saw its labels. Short answer: tile yes where it is common; metal right 68% of the time and flat 54%; wood shake and slate not yet. Updated 2026-11-03: two more places (Palm Beach FL, San Bernardino CA) and the photo network in every place. Then: how it was built and tested, what carries the signal, and what more data would buy.

By Carlo Broderick, NCEAS, UC Santa Barbara · 2026-11-03 07:25 PST

What you get in a county the model has never seen

Averaged over 58 counties it has never seen, the map gets 92% of houses right, against 82% for calling everything asphalt. Places differ: the middle half get 91%–96%; the hardest are Washington DC (57%), San Bernardino CA (71%), Riverside CA (73%). Tile is reliable where it is common (all places together: right 92%, finds 87%); in a typical county the map is right 80% of the time and finds 64%. County tile totals are off by 0.5 points on average (worst place 6). Metal: right 68%, finds 66%. Flat: right 54%, finds 64%; a typical county finds 50% of flat roofs. Wood shake (finds 23%) and slate (9%) are not reliable from these photos yet.

If a user needs the map to be right 70, 80 or 90% of the time

A user can trade coverage for reliability: label a roof only when the model is sure enough. The cut-off has to be set on other places. Tile holds up: aiming at 90% right gives 90% right while still finding 89% of tile roofs. Metal reaches about 70% right while finding 63%. For flat / low-slope, wood shake and slate no cut-off set on other places gets to 70% right while still finding at least 1 in 20 of those roofs; a map that must meet a reliability target for them needs some local labels or better imagery.Per material and target t, the lowest probability cut-off whose pooled precision on the other 7 held-out groups is ≥ t (places counted equally) is applied to the held-out group; reported precision and recall over all places. 'No calls': no cut-off reached t on the other groups, or it labels almost nothing.

How it was built and tested

How the model works

StepWhat happens
1. Inputs per buildingAerial photo chip (USDA NAIP, 0.6 m, red/green/blue/near-infrared + building outline, 1–4 years); AlphaEarth (Google, 10 m, 64 numbers per year summarising all satellites); Sentinel-2 (10 colour/infrared bands, yearly clear-sky median); surroundings (building density, what the nearby buildings look like from space); climate and terrain (rain, heat, cold, snowpack, hail and wind reports, elevation, distance to coast); building use and form (Overture: residential or commercial, height, floors, footprint size and shape).
2. Two modelsA neural network (ConvNeXt-Tiny, pretrained on ImageNet-22k, fine-tuned here) that looks at the photo chip; and gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM) on about 430 numbers from all the other inputs.
3. Second passEach building is also given the model's own first-pass predictions for its 15 nearest buildings, because neighbours tend to share a roof type. No real label is used, so this works in any county.
4. BlendThe two models' probabilities are combined; the weight is chosen on other places only.
5. How it is scoredPlaces are split into 8 groups; each group is scored by models trained only on the other 7, so no place is ever scored by a model that saw its labels (photo network: 5 groups). The national model is then trained once on all places.

What this test does and does not cover

  • Covered: a county with no labels of its own, scored with the inputs a national map would have everywhere (aerial photo, satellites, climate, building footprints and use). No real label from the scored place is used for training, for tuning, or through its neighbours.
  • Labels are mostly county records, set when a house was assessed and often not updated after re-roofing, so some 'errors' are the record, not the model (see the label check below). CAL FIRE inspections are the cleanest labels.
  • Some signal rides on house age, size or neighbourhood, which the model reads from surroundings rather than the roof.
  • Mostly houses: commercial buildings rarely carry a roof record in these sources.
  • Not covered: photos newer or older than the label, places unlike any of the 58 (dense city centres, Alaska, Hawaii), and roof condition or age.

What carries the signal

These input experiments were run on the 56-place data of 2026-11-01, before Palm Beach FL and San Bernardino CA were added and before the photo network ran everywhere; they compare inputs against each other, not the final scores. The aerial photo is the input the model can least do without; the satellites, surroundings and building data each carry much of the signal on their own but overlap. Climate and terrain alone score 0.58: they say which roofs are likely in a region, not on which house; with everything else in, removing them changes the score by 0.0010, nothing measurable, although the model leans on them for 12% of its decisions (they overlap with location).

What would make it better, and what it costs

The photo network, worth 0.04 for the same places and materials, now runs in all 58 places, so that gain is already in every number above. Three levers remain. More labelled places: 1 place 0.70, all 57 others 0.855, rising only slightly at the end (+0.010 for the last step). Not yet tested here: cleaner labels (inspections, recent permits) and finer imagery for wood shake and slate. Many county label files are free or cheap.

Does adding labelled places help?

The more places with roof labels the model learns from, the better it does in a county it has never seen: 1 place 0.70, 2 places 0.76, 4 places 0.78, 8 places 0.81, 16 places 0.83, 32 places 0.845, all 57 others 0.855. The average still rises slightly at the right-hand end (+0.010 for the last step) while the worst place no longer improves (0.728 to 0.722); each doubling of places adds less than the one before, so more places alone will bring small gains. Roof data that can be bought, and what it costs

The cheapest real labels are county records sold as one file: $50 for all of Lee County FL, $66 to $600 for a California county, and Clark County NV may waive its $300 fee for university research. Companies that label roofs from photos (Nearmap, CAPE, ZestyAI, Verisk) mostly publish no price; the one real figure seen is about $9,800 a year for one small town. Roof shapes measured from lidar, Santa Cruz County

Laser points over each of 103,899 buildings were split into flat roof faces; the number, slope and direction of the faces give the shape. Against 243 OpenStreetMap tags for gable, hip, flat and shed roofs it agrees 79% of the time. Flat calls are the weak spot: it finds 85% of the flat roofs, but only 49% of its flat calls are flat in OpenStreetMap (low-pitch sheds get called flat), so the flat count is probably too high. L-/T-shaped gables and complex roofs have no outside check yet.USGS 3DEP CA_SantaCruzCounty_2020 (≈41 pts/m²), footprints shifted (+1.5, −0.6) m to the lidar; iterative RANSAC planes ≥2 m²; rules on plane count, azimuth groups, ridge/eave length ratio and flat share. Thresholds were tuned on the same OSM tags, so the agreement is somewhat optimistic.

What the model learns from

58 places, 10,627,427 labelled roofs, chosen to span US climates and building traditions: desert tile (Maricopa, Riverside), Florida metal and tile, Midwest and Northeast slate and asphalt, Colorado wood shake and snow, California wildfire areas. The material mix differs enormously between places, which is why a new county is hard. Commercial buildings rarely carry a roof record in these sources (tens to a few hundred per county), so the model is mainly a model of houses.Material shares over all labelled roofs (not the chipped subsample). Up to 120,000 roofs per place are used for training, rare materials kept in full and common ones sampled, with weights restoring the real mix in scoring. How each label set was built: docs/region_*.md.
