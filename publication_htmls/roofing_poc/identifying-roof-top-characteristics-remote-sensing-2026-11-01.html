Identifying Roof Top Characteristics with Remote Sensing · 2026-11-01

Could we map roof material for every building in the US? Current best model, scored the way a national map would face it: each place predicted by models that never saw a label from it. 56 places across the US, about 10 million labelled roofs. Material is the target; roof shape is a bonus. With the training data, how the model works, and what more labels would cost.

By Carlo Broderick, NCEAS, UC Santa Barbara · 2026-11-01 06:36 PST

How to read this page

Scores are ranking scores (AUC): pick a random roof of one material and a random other roof; the score is how often the model ranks the first one higher. 0.5 is a coin flip, 0.9 is good, 1 is perfect. How this panel was checked
  • No label from the scored place was used for training, for the neighbour pass, or for choosing the blend weight.
  • CAL FIRE inspections inside Riverside County are held out together with Riverside.
  • Intervals (on hover) resample 2 km cells or whole fires, not single roofs.
  • Labels are mostly county records, which go stale after re-roofing; CAL FIRE inspections are the cleanest check.
  • The photo CNN is held out by groups of places (fewer training places than the features model), so it is slightly understated.

If we drew the map today: roofs found, and how often it is right

A map needs one cut-off per material. Here the cut-off is set on the other places only, as it would have to be in a new county. Rare materials (a few percent of roofs) come with many false alarms, because a small error rate on the many other roofs adds up; the class mix of a new county is unknown and differs a lot between places.Per place and material, the threshold maximising weighted F1 on all other places' never-seen predictions is applied to the held-out place. Weighted to the real mix. One-vs-rest; a roof can pass two thresholds.

How this panel was checked
  • Cut-offs chosen without any label from the scored place.
  • Precision depends on how rare a material is; it would improve with a county-level estimate of the mix.

What to keep in mind

  • Labels are mostly county records, set when a house was assessed and often not updated after re-roofing, so some 'errors' are the record, not the model. The CAL FIRE wildfire inspections are the cleanest check.
  • Some scores ride on house age, size or neighbourhood (for example wood shake on 1960s–70s houses), which the model can learn from the surroundings rather than the roof itself.
  • Rare materials (a few percent of roofs) are found, but a map of them will carry false alarms.
  • Mostly houses: commercial buildings rarely have roof records in these sources.

Will it get better with more data

With 1 place to learn from, the never-seen score is 0.72; with 55 places it is 0.85, and still rising. Roof labels exist for millions more buildings, free or cheap.

Does adding labelled places help?

The more places with roof labels the model learns from, the better it does in a county it has never seen: 1 place 0.72, 2 places 0.74, 4 places 0.78, 8 places 0.80, 16 places 0.83, 32 places 0.84, all 55 places 0.85. What the model learns from, and how it works

What the model learns from

56 places, 9,870,953 labelled roofs, chosen to span US climates and building traditions: desert tile (Maricopa, Riverside), Florida metal and tile, Midwest and Northeast slate and asphalt, Colorado wood shake and snow, California wildfire areas. The material mix differs enormously between places, which is why a new county is hard. Commercial buildings rarely carry a roof record in these sources (tens to a few hundred per county), so the model is mainly a model of houses.Material shares over all labelled roofs (not the chipped subsample). Up to 120,000 roofs per place are used for training, rare materials kept in full and common ones sampled, with weights restoring the real mix in scoring. How the model works

StepWhat happens
1. Inputs per buildingAerial photo chip (USDA NAIP, 0.6 m, red/green/blue/near-infrared + building outline, 1–4 years); AlphaEarth (Google, 10 m, 64 numbers per year summarising all satellites); Sentinel-2 (10 colour/infrared bands, yearly clear-sky median); surroundings (building density, what the nearby buildings look like from space); climate and terrain (rain, heat, cold, snowpack, hail and wind reports, elevation, distance to coast); building use and form (Overture: residential or commercial, height, floors, footprint size and shape).
2. Two modelsA neural network (ConvNeXt-Tiny, pretrained on ImageNet-22k, fine-tuned here) that looks at the photo chip; and gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM) on about 430 numbers from all the other inputs.
3. Second passEach building is also given the model's own first-pass predictions for its 15 nearest buildings, because neighbours tend to share a roof type. No real label is used, so this works in any county.
4. BlendThe two models' probabilities are combined; the weight is chosen on other places only.
5. How it is scoredPlaces are split into 8 groups; each group is scored by models trained only on the other 7, so no place is ever scored by a model that saw its labels (photo network: 5 groups). The national model is then trained once on all places.

Roof shapes measured from lidar, Santa Cruz County

Laser points over each of 103,899 buildings were split into flat roof faces; the number, slope and direction of the faces give the shape. Against 243 OpenStreetMap tags for gable, hip, flat and shed roofs it agrees 79% of the time.
