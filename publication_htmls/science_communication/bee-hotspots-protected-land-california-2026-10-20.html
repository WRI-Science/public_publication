Bee hotspots and protected land in California · 2026-10-20

Do California's protected lands cover its native-bee hotspots? 485,231 museum specimens of 1,782 bee species (1893–2023) summarised in 1,695 squares of 15 × 15 km, overlaid with GAP 1–2 protected land (Dec 2024) · from a draft manuscript by Gretchen LeBuhn and Katja Seltmann, not yet published

By Carlo Broderick, NCEAS, UC Santa Barbara · 2026-10-22 14:28 PDT

California has promised to protect 30% of its land by 2030 ("30×30") and has spent more than $1.3 billion on it. The state is home to more than 1,700 kinds of native bees, about 40% of all the bee species in North America, and its $60 billion farm economy leans on pollinators. But land was not chosen with bees in mind. This study checks how well the protected land lines up with the places bees need most.

A honeycomb of California

🐝 Think of California as a giant honeycomb of 15 km squares. Some squares are filled with protected land; others are empty. The question: are the squares full of bees the ones that got filled?

Most of the honeycomb is empty: 110 of the best bee places are unprotected

California is cut into 15 km squares. Each hexagon is one square that is a bee hotspot. A square counts as protected when at least 39% of it is land managed mainly for nature (parks, reserves and similar). About two in three hotspot squares are not.Hotspot = ≥ 75th percentile across the 1,695 bee-occupied cells (richness ≥ 34 species; CWE ≥ 0.0368). Protected = ≥ 39% areal overlap with dissolved GAP 1/2 lands (CA Nature 30×30 Conserved Areas, Dec 2024); threshold calibrated to the state's 26.1% benchmark (Table S1).

How this panel was checked
  • Counts are the paper's: 122/429, 144/424 and 61/171. One hexagon per cell, so areas compare directly.
  • For the authors: the text gives 449 high-richness cells, but its 122 protected + 307 unprotected, its 28.4% and the 1,266 other cells all add up to 429; this page uses 429.
  • 'Hotspot' is the top quarter of cells, a convenient but arbitrary line; the authors note a stricter cut (top 10%) is still to be run.
  • 'Protected' here means the place is inside the protected network, not that its bees are doing well there (habitat quality was not assessed).
  • Only the 1,695 squares with at least one bee record are included; places never sampled can't show up as hotspots.

Better than chance, but far from complete

Hotspots are only a little better protected than everywhere else

If protection were handed out with no thought for bees, about 26% of hotspots would be protected by chance. Hotspots for number of species do only slightly better (28%); hotspots for rare, local bees do better (34%), but two-thirds are still outside.Rates: high richness 28.4% vs lower 25.8% (+2.6 pp); high CWE 34.0% vs lower 24.0%; dual 35.7%. Reference: 26.1% statewide GAP 1/2.

Luck or design? Protected land does land on rare-bee places more than chance would

Researchers shuffled the same amount of protected land randomly across the state thousands of times. For rare, local bees the real map beats chance however the shuffle is done. For the number of species it beats a cell-by-cell shuffle, but not once the shuffled land comes in big 75–105 km clumps, the way real parks cluster.Area-weighted mean richness / CWE in GAP 1/2 cells vs 9,999 area-matched randomizations: unconstrained random cells and block-aggregated nulls (3×3, 5×5, 7×7 cells). Observed richness 30.89, CWE 0.0356. CWE: P ≤ 0.037 under all four; richness: P = 0.036 (3×3) and 0.041 (random), 0.27 (5×5), 0.42 (7×7). Chao2-estimated richness gives the same random-cell result.

Two kinds of gaps in two kinds of places

The places with the most bee species and the places with the rarest bees are partly different, so protecting one doesn't cover the other. Plants are a poor stand-in too: the number of plant species explains only 25% of the differences in bee species from place to place (companion study).Region lists from Results 3.5. Among high-CWE cells, mean CWE is slightly higher in unprotected (0.0712) than protected (0.0693) cells; for high-richness cells the reverse (76.8 vs 84.4 species). Plant vs bee richness R² = 0.25 (Alarcon-Cruz et al., in review).

Behind the numbers

What counts as 'protected'? The paper picks the rule that matches the state's 26.1%

A 15 km square can be partly parkland. Call a square protected if it has any protected land at all, and the whole state looks protected; demand 100%, and almost nothing is. The authors picked the rule (39%) under which the grid matches the state's own figure of 26.1%.Table S1: protected-cell count and grid-area share at each minimum overlap 0–100% (1% steps), all 1,989 grid cells; 39% gives 26.2% (544 cells, 107,359 km²), Δ = +0.1 pp from 26.1%.
