How far off is the paper's canopy measure?

Meta HighResCanopyHeight v1 run on NAIP aerial imagery — the share of the 0–100 ft ring around each home with vegetation at least 1 m tall — compared with airborne lidar and with Meta's newer CHMv2 model.

How to read the grids. Each grid compares v1 with one other source, named in its heading. Columns are 10% bands of canopy according to that source. Each cell is the homes in that band: the big number is how far v1 is off, in percentage points (v1 minus the comparison), and below it is v1's average reading vs the comparison's average. If v1 matched the comparison, every cell would read about ±0 and be green. Hover or tap a cell for its numbers. Each row also gives the average difference and how well v1 orders the same homes (r, and R² = share of the comparison's variation that v1 explains).

Why these comparisons

No remote measurement is exact; only a site visit is. Airborne lidar is the closest available because it measures vegetation height directly, so it is the primary comparison. Where there is no lidar (the paper's fire homes), the next most accurate source, CHMv2, stands in after calibration to lidar. The other sources were scored against lidar on the same homes but not used as comparisons, because each is less accurate than lidar and CHMv2:

What this means for the paper

v1 is close to right where there is little canopy (0–10%: about 5 pts low) and where there is a lot (70–100%: within a few points). It misses most in the middle, 20–60% canopy, by 15–25 points: mixed yards where shrubs and small trees (1–5 m) carry much of the cover and v1 does not see them. The Southwest (Arizona, New Mexico and their mountains) is worst, with misses near 30 points in those bands; the Pacific coast forests are best. v1 still ranks homes in the right order within each region, which is what the paper's comparisons rely on.

To test whether the misses change the paper's conclusions, Cat's model was re-run unchanged on the same 192,754 homes and 242 fires, swapping only the canopy column (95% intervals from a fire-cluster bootstrap):

Every vegetation term keeps its direction and the vegetation effect gets stronger when canopy is measured more accurately, so v1's under-read makes the paper conservative rather than wrong. The one claim that depends on it is the grass/shrub/desert density interaction, which is no longer significant with CHMv2. The forest “hurts above 40 structures” result was unresolved before and still is. The share of homes where clearing helps also rises partly because, on the lidar scale, removing canopy is a larger change, so fewer homes fall in the “no meaningful effect” band.

CHMv2 still reads a little low because it also misses part of the 1–5 m shrub layer, but the misses shrink from about 20 pts to about 8 pts in the 20–60% bands, the Southwest (Arizona, New Mexico and their mountains), where v1 was 30–40 pts off, comes within about 10 pts, and every ecoregion and state now looks much the same: no region stands out the way the deserts and Southwest do for v1. That consistency matters most for the paper, because the regional comparisons in its model would no longer carry a region-specific measurement bias. Caveat: CHMv2 was trained partly on NAIP paired with USGS lidar, so some of these validation areas may overlap its training data and its scores here may be somewhat optimistic.
